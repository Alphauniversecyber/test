# Grade 5 Scholarship English: What Actually Gets Marked

If you're reading this at 10pm with a tired child beside you and a stack of past papers on the table, you're not alone. The Grade 5 Scholarship Examination, run by Sri Lanka's Department of Examinations, puts a lot of weight on an eleven-year-old, and the English section is the one that worries parents the most — usually because they don't know what the examiner is actually looking for.

So here it is, in plain language. What the paper tests, where children lose marks, and what matters more than the things parents usually drill.

## What the English section actually tests

The Scholarship paper is bilingual, and the English component mixes with the general-knowledge and intelligence sections. The English questions test four things, not grammar rules:

- Can the child read a short English passage and understand it?
- Can the child match a picture to the right English word or short sentence?
- Can the child recognise basic vocabulary in context?
- Can the child follow simple instructions written in English?

Notice what's not on that list. Essay writing isn't. Complex tenses aren't. Long grammar explanations aren't. The paper isn't testing whether your child knows the rule — it's testing whether they can use everyday English fast and without panic.

## Where children lose marks (the honest list)

After years of marking practice papers in class, the same handful of mistakes come up again and again.

### Rushing the picture-match questions

A child sees a picture of a boy under a tree and four options. Three options mention a tree, and the child picks the first one that mentions a tree. They never read the whole sentence. One wrong word — *beside* vs *under*, *running* vs *sitting* — and the mark is gone.

### Not knowing the small words

Children learn "elephant" and "beautiful" and "important." They don't know "between," "beside," "above," "nearby," "through." These prepositions and position words appear in almost every scholarship English section. They're boring to learn, so classes skip them. Don't.

### Guessing instead of eliminating

On a four-option question, two options are usually clearly wrong. A child who crosses those out first gets to the right answer much more reliably than a child who scans for the "nice-looking" option.

### Reading the passage once, fast

Children read the short passage as if it were Sinhala or Tamil — eyes flying across the page. Then they try to answer the questions from memory. English comprehension needs a slower first read and a second read looking for the specific detail the question asks for.

## What actually works in preparation

Six months out from the exam is the sweet spot. Three months is tight but doable. Less than that and you're in survival mode, which is fine — just focus on the highest-value things.

### Build a 100-word picture vocabulary

Make a list of 100 everyday English words that come with pictures: *kitchen, umbrella, cupboard, toothbrush, carpet, ladder, basket, broom, bucket, slipper*. Household objects. Fruits. School things. Body parts. Weather. Positions. Your child needs to recognise each one in half a second. These show up in the paper over and over.

### Learn question words cold

*What, where, when, who, why, how, which, whose.* Children who confuse *who* and *where* lose easy marks. Drill until it's automatic.

### Do past papers under timing

Download past papers from the Department of Examinations site. Set a timer. Stop when the time's up, even if the child isn't finished. The scholarship is as much about speed as about accuracy. Doing papers untimed just builds false confidence.

### Mark together

After each practice paper, sit with your child and go through the wrong answers. Don't say "this is wrong." Ask "why did you pick this one?" Nine times out of ten, the answer tells you exactly what to teach next.

### Read a short English story every night

Not a textbook. A storybook. Short, illustrated, under ten minutes. Over six months this builds reading fluency more than any worksheet will.

## What doesn't work (but parents still do)

- **Memorising essay templates.** The English section doesn't ask for essays.
- **Buying ten different practice-paper books.** One or two good books plus the official past papers is enough.
- **Teaching tenses with grammar tables.** An eleven-year-old doesn't need the perfect progressive tense. They need to recognise "She is eating" in a picture.
- **Hours of English every day.** Thirty focused minutes beats two tired hours.

## A simple six-month plan

**Months 1–2:** Build vocabulary. One new word-set every three days. Short English reading each night.

**Months 3–4:** Add one past paper per week. Mark it the same day. Go through every wrong answer.

**Month 5:** Two past papers per week, under full timing. Focus on the question types your child loses marks on most.

**Month 6:** Timed papers only. Stop teaching new content. Build calmness and exam habits — bathroom before the paper, pencil box ready, deep breath.

## Keeping your child okay through it

The scholarship is stressful. Children feel the pressure whether we talk about it or not. Two small things help more than any extra past paper:

- Keep class days and rest days clear. A child who studies every single evening for six months arrives at the exam exhausted.
- Don't talk about scholarship school admissions until after the paper. That conversation can wait.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many marks is the English section of the Grade 5 Scholarship worth?**

The English questions appear within the general paper rather than as a separate paper, and the exact weighting can shift year to year. Check the latest paper on doenets.lk to see the current structure before you plan around specific mark counts.

**When should my child start preparing for the Grade 5 Scholarship English?**

Six months before the exam is comfortable. Starting in Grade 4 gives you room without rushing. Starting less than three months before is possible but focus only on past papers and high-frequency vocabulary.

**Where can I find Grade 5 Scholarship past papers?**

The Department of Examinations site (doenets.lk) is the official source. Avoid the scraped PDF sites — many have wrong answers in their marking schemes.

**My child reads English well but freezes on the paper. What do I do?**

Freezing is almost always a timing issue, not a knowledge issue. Practise short timed sections — ten questions in eight minutes — until the clock stops feeling scary.
