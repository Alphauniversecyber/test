# Best English Classes in Matara: A 2026 Parent's Guide

Ask ten Matara parents where to send a child for English class and you'll get ten answers. Some will swear by a Colombo-branded institute, some by a teacher down the lane, and a few will tell you they've just given up trying to pick. If you're one of those parents — or an adult who's finally decided to fix their English after years of putting it off — this guide is for you.

We'll go through what to actually look at, which questions sort the good classes from the rest, and how to match a class to your real goal. No lists of "top 10 institutes." Those change every month, and half of them are paid placements anyway.

## Start with the goal, not the brand

Before you pick a class, write down one sentence about what you want. It sounds obvious, but most parents skip it. "I want my child to be better at English" isn't a goal. "I want my daughter to pass O/L English with a credit pass" is. So is "I want to have a work conversation in English without freezing."

The big three goals we see in Matara look like this:

- **Exam results.** Grade 5 Scholarship, O/L English, A/L General English, A/L English main.
- **Spoken confidence.** The child can read fine but won't open their mouth, or the adult knows grammar but can't order food in English without nerves.
- **A specific test.** IELTS for overseas study or migration, sometimes TOEFL.

A class built for one of these isn't automatically good for another. An O/L class and an IELTS class have almost nothing in common beyond the name "English."

## What to look for in a Matara English class

Once the goal is clear, these are the things worth checking before you pay for a term.

### 1. Who actually teaches the class

Not who runs the institute. Who stands in front of the children on Wednesday evening. Ask by name. In most Matara institutes, one or two senior teachers draw the posters and a rotating set of newer teachers handle the actual batches. That's not bad — new teachers can be great — but you want to know who you're getting.

### 2. Batch size

Twenty students in a spoken English class is not a spoken English class. It's a reading class with occasional talking. For speaking goals, look for groups of eight to twelve. For written exam prep, up to twenty is fine if the teacher gives marked feedback on written work.

### 3. What happens in a normal class

Not what the brochure says. What actually happens. Ask a parent whose child has been going for six months: "What did she do in class last week?" If the answer is "she copied notes off the board," that class is a textbook with chairs.

### 4. Progress reports

A real class gives you something — a marked paper, a short report, a WhatsApp update — at least once a term. If the only sign that your child is learning is a monthly fee receipt, that's a problem.

### 5. Timetable sanity

Matara traffic in the evenings is worse than people admit. A class that ends at 7:30pm in Nupe is a class your child will be late for three weeks out of four. Pick a timetable you can actually keep.

## Group class vs private tuition vs online

### Group class

Still the default in Matara and usually the best value. Your child learns alongside others at similar levels, picks up language from peers, and the fee per hour stays reasonable. Weak if the group is too big or too mixed.

### Private tuition

Useful if your child is far ahead or far behind the group, or if you're prepping for a specific exam on a short runway. More expensive and, honestly, often less effective for speaking — because speaking needs other speakers.

### Online classes

Have grown fast since 2020, and they work well for adults, A/L students, and IELTS. For primary-age children, online classes can work if the teacher is good and the child sits with a parent nearby. Otherwise the child drifts.

## Red flags to walk away from

A few things that should make you keep looking, even if the fee is attractive.

- Teachers promise a specific exam grade or IELTS band. No honest teacher does this.
- You can't visit a class before registering. A good institute will let you sit at the back of one class.
- The institute shares no information about teachers' qualifications or experience.
- You're pushed to pay for a full year upfront.
- The classroom has no books on the shelves and no student work on the walls.

## How much should a class cost in Matara?

Fees vary too much to quote here, and prices change every term. As a rough frame: group classes for school students usually sit well below private tuition per hour; IELTS intensive courses cost more because the batches are smaller and the materials are expensive; online classes from Colombo-based teachers often cost the same as local in-person classes, so the geography doesn't always save you money.

A good rule: if the price is far below what others charge, ask why. Usually the answer is batch size or teacher experience.

## A quick word on exam-focused classes

For O/L and A/L English, pick a class that drills past papers, marks written work by hand, and teaches the marking scheme — not just the syllabus. For Grade 5 Scholarship English, look for a teacher who's taught the paper before and knows which question types show up most. For IELTS, insist on mock tests under real timing. A class that never times its students isn't an exam class.

We keep groups small enough that every student speaks every class, teachers mark written work by hand, and parents get a short written progress note each term. ## Frequently asked questions

**Which is the best English class in Matara?**

There isn't one "best" class — it depends on your goal. For exam support, look for a class that drills past papers and gives marked feedback. For spoken English, look for a small batch where every student speaks every lesson. For IELTS, look for timed mock tests and a teacher who has sat the exam themselves.

**At what age should my child start English classes in Matara?**

Most children benefit from a structured English class from around age 4 or 5, but the class must be play-based at that age, not worksheet-based. Formal exam-focused tuition is usually from Grade 3 onwards.

**Are online English classes as good as in-person classes in Matara?**

For adults and older students, yes — often better, because you save travel time and the teacher pool is wider. For primary-age children, in-person tends to work better unless a parent sits with the child during online classes.

**How do I know if my child is actually improving?**

Ask the class for a short written report each term and look at marked written work. Spoken progress is harder to measure, but after two to three months you should hear your child use new English words at home without being prompted.

# Grade 5 Scholarship English: What Actually Gets Marked

If you're reading this at 10pm with a tired child beside you and a stack of past papers on the table, you're not alone. The Grade 5 Scholarship Examination, run by Sri Lanka's Department of Examinations, puts a lot of weight on an eleven-year-old, and the English section is the one that worries parents the most — usually because they don't know what the examiner is actually looking for.

So here it is, in plain language. What the paper tests, where children lose marks, and what matters more than the things parents usually drill.

## What the English section actually tests

The Scholarship paper is bilingual, and the English component mixes with the general-knowledge and intelligence sections. The English questions test four things, not grammar rules:

- Can the child read a short English passage and understand it?
- Can the child match a picture to the right English word or short sentence?
- Can the child recognise basic vocabulary in context?
- Can the child follow simple instructions written in English?

Notice what's not on that list. Essay writing isn't. Complex tenses aren't. Long grammar explanations aren't. The paper isn't testing whether your child knows the rule — it's testing whether they can use everyday English fast and without panic.

## Where children lose marks (the honest list)

After years of marking practice papers in class, the same handful of mistakes come up again and again.

### Rushing the picture-match questions

A child sees a picture of a boy under a tree and four options. Three options mention a tree, and the child picks the first one that mentions a tree. They never read the whole sentence. One wrong word — *beside* vs *under*, *running* vs *sitting* — and the mark is gone.

### Not knowing the small words

Children learn "elephant" and "beautiful" and "important." They don't know "between," "beside," "above," "nearby," "through." These prepositions and position words appear in almost every scholarship English section. They're boring to learn, so classes skip them. Don't.

### Guessing instead of eliminating

On a four-option question, two options are usually clearly wrong. A child who crosses those out first gets to the right answer much more reliably than a child who scans for the "nice-looking" option.

### Reading the passage once, fast

Children read the short passage as if it were Sinhala or Tamil — eyes flying across the page. Then they try to answer the questions from memory. English comprehension needs a slower first read and a second read looking for the specific detail the question asks for.

## What actually works in preparation

Six months out from the exam is the sweet spot. Three months is tight but doable. Less than that and you're in survival mode, which is fine — just focus on the highest-value things.

### Build a 100-word picture vocabulary

Make a list of 100 everyday English words that come with pictures: *kitchen, umbrella, cupboard, toothbrush, carpet, ladder, basket, broom, bucket, slipper*. Household objects. Fruits. School things. Body parts. Weather. Positions. Your child needs to recognise each one in half a second. These show up in the paper over and over.

### Learn question words cold

*What, where, when, who, why, how, which, whose.* Children who confuse *who* and *where* lose easy marks. Drill until it's automatic.

### Do past papers under timing

Download past papers from the Department of Examinations site. Set a timer. Stop when the time's up, even if the child isn't finished. The scholarship is as much about speed as about accuracy. Doing papers untimed just builds false confidence.

### Mark together

After each practice paper, sit with your child and go through the wrong answers. Don't say "this is wrong." Ask "why did you pick this one?" Nine times out of ten, the answer tells you exactly what to teach next.

### Read a short English story every night

Not a textbook. A storybook. Short, illustrated, under ten minutes. Over six months this builds reading fluency more than any worksheet will.

## What doesn't work (but parents still do)

- **Memorising essay templates.** The English section doesn't ask for essays.
- **Buying ten different practice-paper books.** One or two good books plus the official past papers is enough.
- **Teaching tenses with grammar tables.** An eleven-year-old doesn't need the perfect progressive tense. They need to recognise "She is eating" in a picture.
- **Hours of English every day.** Thirty focused minutes beats two tired hours.

## A simple six-month plan

**Months 1–2:** Build vocabulary. One new word-set every three days. Short English reading each night.

**Months 3–4:** Add one past paper per week. Mark it the same day. Go through every wrong answer.

**Month 5:** Two past papers per week, under full timing. Focus on the question types your child loses marks on most.

**Month 6:** Timed papers only. Stop teaching new content. Build calmness and exam habits — bathroom before the paper, pencil box ready, deep breath.

## Keeping your child okay through it

The scholarship is stressful. Children feel the pressure whether we talk about it or not. Two small things help more than any extra past paper:

- Keep class days and rest days clear. A child who studies every single evening for six months arrives at the exam exhausted.
- Don't talk about scholarship school admissions until after the paper. That conversation can wait.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many marks is the English section of the Grade 5 Scholarship worth?**

The English questions appear within the general paper rather than as a separate paper, and the exact weighting can shift year to year. Check the latest paper on doenets.lk to see the current structure before you plan around specific mark counts.

**When should my child start preparing for the Grade 5 Scholarship English?**

Six months before the exam is comfortable. Starting in Grade 4 gives you room without rushing. Starting less than three months before is possible but focus only on past papers and high-frequency vocabulary.

**Where can I find Grade 5 Scholarship past papers?**

The Department of Examinations site (doenets.lk) is the official source. Avoid the scraped PDF sites — many have wrong answers in their marking schemes.

**My child reads English well but freezes on the paper. What do I do?**

Freezing is almost always a timing issue, not a knowledge issue. Practise short timed sections — ten questions in eight minutes — until the clock stops feeling scary.

# O/L English Paper: 9 Mistakes That Lose Marks (and Fixes)

Most students who fail to get a Distinction in O/L English don't fail because they don't know English. They fail because they repeat the same handful of avoidable mistakes under exam pressure. We mark hundreds of practice papers a year, and the list below is what we actually see — not generic advice copied from somewhere else.

If you can fix even six of these before your next practice paper, your mark goes up.

## 1. Writing the letter without the address

Paper II opens with a letter, and the first thing a tired student skips is the sender's address. That's two to three marks gone before the first sentence. Both formal and informal letters need the address and the date in the right place. Learn the format once and write it the same way every time — don't make up a new layout in the exam hall.

**Fix:** Decide on one layout you'll always use for formal letters, and one for informal, and drill them until you can write both from memory in under a minute.

## 2. Treating comprehension like a translation exercise

Students read the comprehension passage and try to translate every word into Sinhala or Tamil in their head. Then they run out of time. Comprehension doesn't test whether you understand every word. It tests whether you can find the specific information a question asks for.

**Fix:** Read the questions first, then go back to the passage looking for the answer. Underline the line where you find each answer. If a question asks "why," the answer is usually near the word *because* or the words *so that*.

## 3. One-word answers when a sentence is asked for

A question that says "Why did the writer go to Colombo?" needs a sentence. Writing "Doctor's appointment" loses half the mark. The marking scheme rewards full sentences on these questions more often than students expect.

**Fix:** If the question has the word *why*, start your answer with *Because*. If it has *how*, start with *By*. If it has *what*, start by repeating the noun from the question.

## 4. Writing dialogues as narration

Paper II often has a dialogue-completion question. Students write things like "He said that he was going home." That's narration. The question asks for the actual words of the dialogue — "I'm going home."

**Fix:** In dialogue questions, write exactly what each person says, in quotation marks, without "He said that..." Keep it conversational.

## 5. Ignoring the word count

The essay or short composition tells you a word range — usually 100 to 150 words for short pieces, more for the long one. Students either write 60 words (and lose structure marks) or 250 words (and lose relevance marks because they've padded). Both cost you.

**Fix:** Count the lines in your normal handwriting. Most students write 8 to 10 words per line. Use that to hit the range without actually counting words.

## 6. No paragraphs

A full essay written as one giant paragraph tells the examiner you don't know how to organise ideas. Even with good sentences, the mark drops.

**Fix:** Every essay gets at least three paragraphs — introduction, body, conclusion. The body usually splits into two or three. Leave a blank line between paragraphs so the examiner can see it at a glance.

## 7. Spelling mistakes on words you knew in Grade 7

*Because, friend, business, Wednesday, receive, government, their vs there.* These are the words that come back wrong on almost every paper. Not difficult vocabulary — easy words rushed.

**Fix:** Keep a personal "red list" of ten words you keep getting wrong. Write each one correctly five times before every practice paper. Your list will shrink.

## 8. Not planning the essay

Students read the essay prompt, pick up the pen, and write the first sentence that comes to mind. Three paragraphs in, they realise they're off-topic or they've run out of ideas.

**Fix:** Spend two minutes planning. Write four to five bullet points in the margin of the answer booklet. Cross them out as you cover each one. Those two minutes save you ten minutes of panic.

## 9. Blank at the literature section

If your syllabus includes the literature texts, students lose easy marks on the contextual questions — the ones where you have to name the author, the character speaking, or the situation in the text. These marks are free if you know the basic story of each poem, prose piece and play.

**Fix:** Make a one-page summary for each text — title, author, main characters, one-sentence plot, one key theme. Read the summary three times a week. You don't need to re-read the full texts — you need to recognise them under pressure.

## How to use the marking scheme (the right way)

The official marking schemes from the Department of Examinations are not a secret. They're published after each exam. Most students look at them once and move on. That's a waste.

Take a past paper, do it under timed conditions, then mark it yourself using the marking scheme. You'll find out two things: where you lose marks you thought you'd get, and where you earn marks you thought you'd lost. Both change how you prepare for the next paper.

Do this for four or five past papers in a row and you'll start thinking like an examiner — which is the single biggest jump a student can make in the last three months.

## A realistic O/L English study week

Not a 4am start, not six hours a day. This works:

- **Monday:** 30 minutes of vocabulary and grammar drill.
- **Wednesday:** 30 minutes of comprehension or dialogue practice.
- **Friday:** 30 minutes of writing — one letter or short composition.
- **Saturday:** One full past paper, timed. Rest of the day off.
- **Sunday:** Mark the paper. Note the mistakes in a book you keep for the whole year.

Two hours a week of focused, marked practice beats fifteen hours of unmarked reading.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is the O/L English paper structured in Sri Lanka?**

The paper is normally split across Paper I and Paper II, covering reading comprehension, vocabulary in context, grammar, letter writing, dialogue, and short and long composition. The literature component appears for students who sit it as a separate subject. Check the current Department of Examinations syllabus for the exact format in your exam year.

**Is O/L English hard to score an A in?**

It's not harder than any other subject, but students lose marks on small mechanical things — spelling, format, word count — more than on their actual English. Fixing those moves you from a credit pass to a distinction without needing better English.

**Should I memorise essays for the O/L English paper?**

No. Memorised essays almost always answer the wrong question, and examiners spot them. Instead, practise writing to a plan — you'll handle any topic that comes up.

**How many past papers should I do before the O/L English exam?**

Six to eight full timed papers, marked using the official marking scheme, is usually enough to see consistent improvement. More than that and you're repeating patterns rather than learning new ones.

# IELTS Band 7 from Sri Lanka: An 8-Week Realistic Plan

Band 7 is where the IELTS exam stops being about English and starts being about exam technique. If you're reading this, you probably speak and write decent English already. You've likely tried a mock test, scored a 6 or 6.5, and realised that the extra half-band isn't going to come from more vocabulary lists.

This is an eight-week plan built for working adults and A/L finishers in Sri Lanka — the two groups we see most in IELTS classes in Matara. It assumes you have about 8–10 hours a week to put in. Less than that and you'll need more weeks; more and you can do this in six.

## Before you start: get an honest baseline

Sit one full practice test. Cambridge IELTS 17 or 18 is what most teachers use. Do it under real conditions — paper, timer, no pausing. Mark it using the official band descriptors, not a rough guess.

If your baseline is below Band 6, eight weeks is tight. You can still try but plan for more revision time. If your baseline is Band 6.5, eight weeks is comfortable. If you're already at Band 7 in two of the four skills, focus the plan on the weaker two.

## The four skills aren't equal

In our Matara batches, this is roughly what we see:

- **Reading and Listening** usually climb the fastest. They're pattern-recognition skills. You get better at them by doing them.
- **Writing** is where most Sri Lankan candidates stall. The issue isn't English — it's structure, task response, and the specific style of academic writing IELTS rewards.
- **Speaking** sounds like it should be the easiest, and for confident speakers it is. But candidates who use Sinhala or Tamil as their everyday language sometimes freeze on Part 2.

Plan your time accordingly. A rough split: 20% reading, 20% listening, 35% writing, 25% speaking.

## Week 1: Diagnose and set up

- Do one full practice test on Day 1. Mark it honestly.
- List which question types you got wrong in Reading and Listening. These become your drilling targets.
- Score your Writing Task 1 and Task 2 using the band descriptors. The four criteria are Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammar. Rank yourself on each.
- Record a 2-minute Speaking Part 2 on your phone. Listen back. Note where you paused, repeated, or lost the thread.

End of Week 1 you should know your real starting point and your two or three biggest gaps.

## Week 2: Reading foundations

- Learn the question types: matching headings, True/False/Not Given, multiple choice, sentence completion, matching features.
- For each type, do 10–15 practice questions this week. Check which type you lose most marks on.
- Learn to skim and scan — read the question first, then hunt for the answer in the passage. Don't read the whole passage carefully.
- Build a timing habit: 20 minutes per passage, strict.

Common Sri Lankan candidate mistake: spending too long on the first passage because you want to be sure. The third passage is always the hardest, and you need the time for it. Move on.

## Week 3: Listening foundations

- Practise with the Cambridge test audio, not YouTube videos. The exam's accents and pace matter.
- Train your ear for distractors — the speaker says one answer then corrects themselves. The second answer is usually the right one.
- Spelling matters in Listening. A right answer spelled wrong is marked wrong. Drill the names of days, months, directions, and common British place names.
- Do Section 4 every day. It's the hardest, so stop avoiding it.

## Week 4: Writing Task 1 (Academic)

This is the week most people underestimate.

- Learn one solid structure: paragraph 1 is the paraphrase, paragraph 2 is the overview (always two sentences), paragraphs 3 and 4 are the detail.
- The overview is where most candidates lose marks. Write it before you write the detail paragraphs.
- Learn 15 synonyms for *increase, decrease, remain stable, fluctuate*. That's enough. More just makes writing slower.
- Write three Task 1 pieces this week, each under 20 minutes. Get at least one marked by a teacher.

**Trap to avoid:** describing every single data point. The task wants trends and comparisons, not a list of numbers.

## Week 5: Writing Task 2

- Four essay types cover almost all questions: opinion, discussion, problem-solution, and two-part. Learn a plan for each.
- Introduction, two body paragraphs, conclusion. Nothing fancy.
- Each body paragraph: one clear idea, one or two supporting sentences, one example. Examples can be from Sri Lanka — the examiner doesn't need UK examples.
- Grammar range matters for Band 7. If you only ever write simple sentences, you cap at 6.5. Mix in a conditional, a relative clause, and a passive construction.

Write three Task 2 essays this week, under 40 minutes each. Mark them against the descriptors.

## Week 6: Speaking

- Record every practice. Listen. You'll hear your own filler words — *actually, basically, you know* — faster than any teacher will.
- Part 1: don't over-prepare. Short, direct, two-sentence answers.
- Part 2: speak for the full two minutes. Silence kills your score. If you run out of ideas, repeat yourself with different words — the examiner would rather hear you keep going than stop.
- Part 3: longer, more abstract answers. Use phrases like *on the one hand, that said, having said that, one example would be.*

Daily practice this week: pick one Part 2 cue card, speak for two minutes, record, listen, do it again. Fifteen minutes.

## Week 7: Full mock tests

- Two full tests this week, back to back is fine on a weekend.
- Mark them the same day. Notice which question types are still weak.
- For Writing and Speaking, get these marked by a teacher if at all possible. Self-marking at this stage underestimates errors.

## Week 8: Taper and test prep

- One final mock test on Day 1. Rest between the sections like the real exam.
- Reduce new practice. Review mistakes from Weeks 2–7.
- Three days before the test: no new material. Re-read your notes. Sleep normally.
- Day before: light review only. Pack your ID, pencils, water bottle. Go to bed early.

## What to skip

- YouTube "band 9 secret" videos. Most have no secret.
- Buying ten different IELTS books. Three Cambridge test books plus one writing guide is enough.
- Memorising full essays. Examiners flag them.
- Flashcards for obscure vocabulary. Common academic words used correctly beat rare words used oddly.

## Sri Lankan context

Two things worth knowing if you're testing here:

- Test centres are mainly in Colombo, with occasional dates at other locations. Book early — slots fill fast around university application cycles.
- Both IDP and the British Council run tests. The test is the same — pick by location and date.

Confirm current fees, dates, and centres on the IDP or British Council Sri Lanka site before you book. Pricing changes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I reach IELTS Band 7 in 8 weeks?**

If your starting point is Band 6 to 6.5 and you can study 8–10 hours a week, yes — this is realistic. From Band 5.5 or lower, give yourself 12 to 16 weeks.

**Is IELTS Academic or General Training easier?**

The speaking and listening sections are identical. General Training reading is slightly easier than Academic, and General Training writing has a letter instead of a data task. Pick based on what your university or migration route requires, not difficulty.

**How often can I retake IELTS in Sri Lanka?**

There's no cooling-off period — you can book again as soon as the next slot opens. That said, a retake without a plan usually gets a similar score. Fix the gap first.

**Does Writing Task 2 count more than Task 1?**

Yes. Task 2 is worth roughly twice as much as Task 1 in the writing band calculation. If you're short on time, prioritise Task 2 in practice.

# Choosing an English Tuition Class: 10 Questions Parents Should Ask

Most parents pick an English class the way they pick a restaurant — a neighbour recommended it, so they try it. Sometimes it works. Often it doesn't, and six months later the child's English hasn't moved and the term fee is gone.

Here are ten questions you can ask before you register. None of them are rude. A good class will answer all of them without hesitation. If a class refuses to answer even three, keep looking.

## 1. Who will teach my child?

Not "who owns the institute." Who stands in front of the batch. Get the name. Ask what classes they've taught before and how long they've been teaching this level. A good teacher is happy to tell you. An institute that dodges this is hiding something.

## 2. How many students are in a batch?

You want a number, not "small groups." For a speaking-focused class, 8 to 12 is the comfortable range. For exam prep, up to 20 is fine if written work is marked. Over 25 and the class is really a lecture.

## 3. Can I sit in on a class before I register?

Any serious institute will let you observe one class — sometimes from the back of the room, sometimes a sample session. If a class refuses, there's usually a reason, and it's usually not a good one.

## 4. What does a typical lesson look like?

Ask for a walk-through of one actual lesson, not the brochure version. "We open with a 10-minute vocabulary review, then one past paper comprehension passage, then 15 minutes of pair speaking, then a written homework check" is a specific answer. "We cover the syllabus" is not.

## 5. How is written work marked?

For any exam-prep class, hand-marked written work is non-negotiable. The marks should come back with corrections and short comments, not a tick and a grade. If the class uses only self-marking or peer-marking, your child isn't getting real feedback on their writing.

## 6. How will I know my child is improving?

Good answer: a written progress note at the end of each term, plus marked past papers you can see. Bad answer: "You can ask the teacher any time." You won't, and both of you know it.

## 7. What's the timetable, and how often does it change?

Classes that shift time every month are harder on families than they look. Ask for the full term's timetable in writing. Also check start and end times against your real evening — Matara traffic, school pickup, dinner.

## 8. What happens if my child misses a class?

Good classes offer either a makeup class, a recording, or notes with the week's work. If the answer is "they just miss it," you're paying for classes that disappear.

## 9. What materials are included in the fee?

Ask if books, past paper packs, and mock tests are inside the term fee or charged separately. This matters especially for IELTS and A/L classes, where materials can add 20–30% to the real cost.

## 10. Who are last year's results?

For exam-focused classes, ask about recent student results — not individual student names, but overall patterns. "Most of our O/L students passed with Credit" is a fair answer. "100% distinctions" is almost never true.

## The three warning signs

If you hear any of these, walk away.

- **Guaranteed grades.** No honest teacher promises a specific O/L grade or IELTS band. Learning depends on the student too.
- **Full-year fee upfront with no refund terms.** A term at a time is reasonable. Twelve months with no exit is a trap.
- **No teachers' qualifications shared.** You don't need degrees in English — some excellent teachers don't have them — but the institute should be willing to tell you about them.

## The three green flags

- The office staff know your child's name by the second visit.
- The teacher knows the Department of Examinations marking scheme by heart.
- A former student recommends the class without being asked.

## Big-brand vs local vs online

### Big brand (British Council, Royal Institute, major Colombo names)

Strong curriculum, good materials, higher fees. For IELTS especially, the brand is useful because the materials are current. Downside: large batch sizes, less personal attention, often a commute.

### Local institute

The real variable. The good ones beat big brands easily because the teacher-to-student ratio is better and the owner is in the building. The weak ones have one tired teacher doing six batches. The questions above sort the two.

### Online

Convenient for adults and older students. Weak for primary-age children unless a parent sits in. Good for exam prep if the class uses live writing feedback and not just recorded videos.

## When to switch classes

A few signs it's time to move:

- Six months in and your child can't name one thing they learned last week.
- The teacher changes four times a year.
- Written work comes back with only a tick, no corrections.
- Your child dreads the class — not in a "homework is hard" way, but a "nothing's happening there" way.

Switching mid-year isn't ideal, but staying in a class that isn't working is worse.

**How much should I expect to pay for English tuition in Sri Lanka?**

Fees vary widely by city, level, and class size. Group classes for school students usually sit in a lower bracket per hour than private tuition; IELTS and A/L main English cost more because of smaller batches and materials. If a class is dramatically cheaper than others nearby, ask why — usually it's batch size.

**Is private tuition always better than group class?**

No. Group classes work better for speaking because speaking needs other speakers. Private tuition works better when a child is far ahead or far behind the group, or for a specific exam on a short runway.

**Should I pick the nearest English class or the best one?**

Within reason, the nearest one, if the quality is close. Traffic and tiredness kill classes that start too late at night. A slightly weaker class your child attends every week beats a slightly better class they skip twice a month.

**Can I move my child mid-term if the class isn't working?**

Yes. Most institutes won't refund the full term fee, but a wasted term is cheaper than a wasted year. If the warning signs are clear, move.

# How to Help Your Child Speak English Confidently at Home

Your child reads English fine. They get decent marks. But when a relative visits and asks a question in English, they freeze, look at the floor, and answer in Sinhala. Sound familiar?

This is the most common thing parents ask us about, and the good news is you can fix it at home without buying anything. The bad news is you have to be a little patient and a little willing to look silly.

## Why your child isn't speaking

Four reasons cover most children.

- **No one at home speaks English.** The child has nowhere to practise outside class.
- **The grown-ups correct every mistake.** The child learns that speaking English means being embarrassed.
- **The classroom is reading and writing only.** The child has words in their head but has never said them aloud.
- **They're shy, full stop.** Some children would rather not speak any language in front of people. English isn't the issue — confidence is.

Most children have a mix of all four. The habits below help with all of them.

## Rule one: stop correcting mistakes in the middle of the sentence

If your child says "Yesterday I go to shop," do not jump in with "went, not go." The child will stop talking. What happens next:

- You listen to the whole sentence.
- You reply naturally, using the right form: "Oh, you went to the shop? What did you buy?"
- You've modelled the correct version. The child's brain picks it up slowly.

This feels wrong because we were all corrected mid-sentence as children. But correction kills confidence, and children stop trying. Model the right form in your reply instead.

## Build a 15-minute English window

One short window a day beats a vague "speak more English" rule that no one actually follows.

- Pick a time: car ride to school, dinner, bath time.
- During that window, only English. Short sentences are fine. Mistakes are fine.
- Outside the window, go back to normal.

Fifteen minutes a day, consistent for three months, moves a child's spoken English more than any class alone will.

## Give them a job that needs English

Children speak when they have something to say. Try one of these:

- **The weather report.** Every morning, the child tells you the weather in one sentence. "It's cloudy today." Thirty seconds, every day.
- **The grocery list reader.** You dictate the list in English, the child writes it. Then they read it back while shopping.
- **The storyteller.** At bedtime, the child tells a short story — real or made-up — in English. Three minutes, no more.
- **The restaurant orderer.** When you eat out, the child orders in English. They'll fight you on this at first. It passes.

These aren't exercises. They're small responsibilities in English.

## Read aloud together, don't read alone

Reading alone builds vocabulary. Reading aloud together builds speaking. Pick a short book at your child's level. You read one line, they read the next. For younger children, they can just repeat after you.

Ten minutes of this, three nights a week, is enough.

## Audio in the background

Children pick up pronunciation from listening. Put English on in the car, at breakfast, in the background of play time.

- Children's audiobooks or stories (free on many apps).
- English cartoons they already like, in English with English subtitles if your child reads.
- Podcasts for children if they're a bit older.

Avoid dubbed content. The point is to hear real English rhythm.

## Deal with the "I don't want to" phase

Every child goes through a phase where they refuse to speak English, usually around ages 7–9. This is normal. What works:

- Don't make it a punishment. The moment English feels like homework, they'll resist harder.
- Make it situational. English during one specific activity, not all day.
- Praise effort, not correctness. "You tried that sentence — good" beats "that's wrong."
- Let them see you speaking imperfect English with someone. Children learn from watching adults make mistakes and keep going.

## What about screen time?

Used well, screens help. Used badly, they do nothing.

- Good: an English cartoon your child watches once and then retells the story to you.
- Bad: forty-five minutes of YouTube Shorts in English. No sentences, no context, no speaking afterwards.

A rough test: if your child can't tell you something from the video they just watched, the video didn't do anything.

## The shy child

If your child is simply shy — not just in English — forcing is a disaster. Instead:

- Let them speak to one trusted person first. A grandparent, a cousin, a familiar teacher.
- Build to small groups slowly.
- Spoken English classes with small batches help enormously because they lower the stakes.

Most shy children open up when the setting feels safe. Rushing this backfires every time.

## Fitting this around school

School and tuition already fill the day. The good news: none of this needs more than 15–20 focused minutes.

A realistic week:

- **Monday–Friday:** 15-minute English window, one job (weather or list).
- **Three nights:** 10 minutes reading aloud together.
- **Background audio:** car rides, breakfasts, whatever fits.

Total: under 2 hours a week of parent effort. Over six months, it rewires how your child speaks English.

## When home isn't enough

If your child is older than 10 and still won't speak English, home alone won't fix it. They need a classroom setting with other children at similar levels, where speaking is the point of the lesson rather than something that happens at the end.

That's where a dedicated spoken English class helps — not to replace what you do at home, but to add peers.

## Frequently asked questions

**At what age should my child start speaking English?**

As early as you like, as long as it's gentle. Ages 4–6 is a natural window because children have no fear of mistakes yet. By age 9 or 10, shyness kicks in and you need more patience.

**My child's English teacher says they're fine. Why can't they speak?**

School English usually tests reading, writing, and grammar — not speaking. A child can get a Credit pass in O/L English and still not speak comfortably. The two are different skills.

**Does it matter if I don't speak perfect English myself?**

No. Children don't need a perfect model — they need a confident one. Your willingness to try matters more than your accent.

**Should I only speak English at home?**

Not if your family language isn't English. All-English households for a Sinhala-speaking or Tamil-speaking family usually create confused children and awkward dinners. A short daily window works better.

# O/L English Literature: How to Write Essays That Score

English Literature at O/L level feels unfair to a lot of students. You can know the texts well, you can love the stories, and still get a mediocre mark on the essay. Meanwhile someone who barely finished the novel writes a better essay and scores higher. The difference is almost never knowledge. It's structure.

Here's how to write essays that score — written for Sri Lankan students sitting the GCE O/L Literature paper, with the quirks of the local marking scheme in mind.

## What the examiner actually wants

Every Literature essay is checking three things:

- Do you understand the text?
- Can you answer the question that was asked, not the question you wish was asked?
- Can you back up what you say with evidence from the text?

Students lose marks because they drift — they write everything they know about the text instead of answering the specific question. The marking scheme rewards relevance before depth.

## Plan before you write (two minutes, non-negotiable)

Before your pen touches the lined pages of the answer booklet:

- Underline the key words in the question. If the question says *How does the poet present loneliness*, your essay is about loneliness and about how the poet shows it. Not loneliness in general.
- Jot three main points in the margin. Each will become a body paragraph.
- Next to each point, note one short textual reference — a line, an image, a scene.
- Decide your answer to the question in one sentence. That sentence is your thesis.

Two minutes of planning saves ten minutes of wandering mid-essay.

## The PEEL structure (and when to break it)

PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link — is the backbone of a Literature paragraph.

- **Point:** the one argument this paragraph makes. One clear sentence.
- **Evidence:** a short quote or specific reference from the text. Quote exactly if you can; paraphrase if you can't.
- **Explanation:** what the evidence shows about your point. This is where the marks live.
- **Link:** a sentence tying the paragraph back to the question.

You can break PEEL for style, but at O/L level, stick with it until you're confident. The examiner recognises the structure and marks it faster.

### Example paragraph

> The poet shows the speaker's loneliness most clearly through imagery of empty space. In the second stanza, she describes the room as "a shell with no sea inside," comparing the familiar house to something hollow. The image is unsettling because a shell should hold the memory of water, but this one doesn't — the speaker feels that her life has lost its content, not just her companions. This use of metaphor lets the reader feel the loneliness without being told about it directly, which deepens the sense of isolation the poem explores.

Point, evidence, explanation, link. Under 80 words. Three to four of these makes a strong essay.

## How to use quotes

Short quotes beat long ones. One word pulled from a line, used well, scores higher than three lines quoted and barely discussed.

- Put quotes in quotation marks and keep them short.
- Integrate them into your sentence rather than dropping them in separately.
- If you can't remember the exact wording, paraphrase and say what the moment shows.

**Weak:** The writer says "Then she sat down on the chair and thought about her life and all the things she had never done."

**Strong:** The writer lingers on the moment she "sat down" to think — the stillness marks a turning point in how she sees herself.

## Common mistakes in O/L Literature essays

### Retelling the story

Three paragraphs of plot summary isn't an essay. The examiner has read the book. Your job is to answer the question, not to prove you also read it.

### Vague agreement words

"This is a very interesting poem" or "The writer uses many techniques" tells the examiner nothing. Cut these sentences. They don't earn marks; they eat time.

### Paragraphs with one sentence

A single-sentence paragraph is almost always an incomplete thought. If you only have one sentence, you haven't finished explaining — go back and add.

### Treating every text the same way

A poem isn't a short story. A play isn't a novel. The techniques you talk about should match the form. For poems: imagery, rhythm, structure, sound. For prose: character development, setting, narrative voice. For drama: dialogue, stage directions, dramatic irony.

## A simple essay template for the exam

Introduction (2–3 sentences)

- One sentence setting the text and author.
- One sentence stating your overall answer to the question (your thesis).
- Optional: one sentence previewing your three points.

Body (3 paragraphs, PEEL each)

- First point — strongest.
- Second point — different angle on the same theme.
- Third point — a nuance or counter-perspective.

Conclusion (2 sentences)

- Restate your answer in different words.
- One final thought — the "so what" of your argument.

Total: around 500–600 words for a full essay response. Enough to score well, not so much that you run out of time.

## How to revise without re-reading everything

Two months before the exam, re-reading full novels isn't the best use of your time. Instead:

- **Make a one-page text summary.** Title, author, main characters, one-sentence plot, three central themes, four or five memorable quotes.
- **Theme-map your texts.** For each major theme (love, loss, power, identity, nature), list the two or three texts that cover it and one key example from each.
- **Do past paper essays.** Two per week. Timed. Marked with the marking scheme.
- **Compare your essays to the marking scheme band descriptors.** Ask: what would push this from a Credit to a Distinction?

## Timed practice is non-negotiable

Literature essays are won and lost on time management. In the exam you have maybe 35–45 minutes per essay. If you can't finish an essay in 40 minutes at home, you certainly won't in the exam hall.

Practise with a timer. Stop when the time's up. Mark anyway. The point is to learn what you can realistically produce under pressure.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should an O/L Literature essay be?**

Around 500–600 words for a full essay response, or about 2.5 to 3 sides of the answer booklet in normal handwriting. Shorter than that usually means you haven't developed your points enough. Much longer and you're probably repeating yourself.

**Can I memorise essays for O/L Literature?**

No. Examiners are very good at spotting memorised essays, and they almost never match the specific question. Memorise textual references and structures instead — then adapt to whatever question appears.

**How many quotes should I use in a Literature essay?**

Three to five short, well-chosen quotes is plenty for a full essay. Each quote should earn its place — if you can't say something specific about it, cut it.

**What's the difference between O/L English Language and O/L English Literature?**

English Language tests general reading, writing, grammar, and composition. English Literature tests your understanding of specific set texts — poems, short stories, plays, novels — chosen by the Department of Examinations. They're different subjects, taught differently.

- **Vocabulary:** 10 new English words a week. Make flashcards with the word on one side and a picture on the other. Keep them.

- **English:** continue the 10 words a week. Add preposition practice — *beside, between, under, over, through*. Picture-matching worksheets help a lot here.

**Goal:** honest assessment of where the child is.

Do one past paper under full timing. Mark it with the official marking scheme. Now look at the result honestly:

Spend the second half of Month 3 fixing the weakest area specifically. Don't try to fix everything at once.

**Goal:** the child can finish full papers within the time limit.

- **Weekly:** one past paper under full timing. Same time of day you'd sit the real paper.
- **Daily:** 15 minutes of mental maths and English vocabulary drill — fast, short bursts.
- **Weekly marking:** sit together, go through every wrong answer. Ask *why* your child picked that option. The answer usually tells you what to teach.

By the end of Month 4, your child should be finishing the paper within the time with a few minutes to check.

**Goal:** turn remaining weak spots into automatic answers.

- Two past papers a week, alternating full and half.
- Focused drill on the two or three question types that still cost marks.
- Light on new content. You're not adding, you're sharpening.

**Goal:** the child walks into the exam hall rested and confident.

- Reduce practice volume in the last two weeks.
- Full timed papers on the weekends only.
- No new topics — if your child doesn't know it by now, it's not coming in the exam.

- Short reading passages with multiple-choice questions

- Simple grammar (is/are, was/were, do/does)

- Essay writing
- Complex tenses
- Long grammar explanations

- **Comparing with other children.** Every parent group has one parent whose child is "already doing papers from last year." Mute the group.

If three months in, your child is losing ground or hating the process:

- Switch to a shorter daily routine with more variety.

# IELTS vs TOEFL in Sri Lanka: Which One Should You Take?

If you're applying overseas from Sri Lanka, someone has probably already told you to take IELTS. Someone else told you TOEFL is harder. A third person said "it doesn't matter, just take one." All three are partly right.

Here's a straight comparison, written for Sri Lankan candidates, with the local practicalities in mind.

## The short version

- **IELTS** is more common in Sri Lanka, has in-person speaking with a real examiner, and is accepted almost everywhere.
- **TOEFL** is more common for US universities, uses a computer-based speaking section, and is accepted in most places too.
- **For most Sri Lankan candidates, IELTS is the easier logistical choice.** TOEFL still wins for some US applications.

If you need a specific answer now: ask your target university or visa route. Pick the test they explicitly accept. If both work, take IELTS.

## Format differences

### IELTS Academic

- **Listening:** 30 minutes, four recorded sections.
- **Reading:** 60 minutes, three passages.
- **Writing:** 60 minutes, a short data task plus a longer essay.
- **Speaking:** 11–14 minutes, face to face with an examiner.
- Total: about 2 hours 45 minutes.

### TOEFL iBT

- **Reading:** 35 minutes.
- **Listening:** 36 minutes.
- **Speaking:** 16 minutes, recorded into a microphone, graded later.
- **Writing:** 29 minutes, two tasks.
- Total: about 2 hours.

Both tests cover the same four skills, but the way they test them is different. TOEFL is fully computer-based and has shorter sections. IELTS can be done on paper or computer depending on the centre, with speaking always done live with a person.

## Speaking: the biggest practical difference

If you're comfortable with people and nervous with microphones, IELTS speaking will feel more natural. You sit across from an examiner, they ask questions, you answer. It's a conversation.

TOEFL speaking is harder for most Sri Lankan candidates. You speak into a microphone against a timer, often with other test takers speaking in the same room. There's no interaction. If you pause too long or go off-topic, no one guides you back.

Candidates who speak well with people but freeze with recording equipment usually do better on IELTS.

## Scoring

**IELTS:** scored on a 9-band scale, with 0.5 increments. Band 7 is competitive. Most universities ask for 6.0 to 7.5 depending on programme.

**TOEFL iBT:** scored out of 120, with 30 per section. 90 is roughly equivalent to IELTS 7. Most universities ask for 80–100.

Score requirements for Sri Lankan universities and overseas universities vary by programme — always check the specific requirement on the course page rather than trusting general numbers.

**Both tests** are accepted by universities in the UK, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Ireland, and most European countries.

**IELTS** has an edge for:

- UK visa and immigration (IELTS for UKVI is the standard route).
- Australian permanent residency.
- UK and Irish universities.
- Most Sri Lankan employers and government scholarships.

**TOEFL** has an edge for:

- Many US universities, especially older ones.
- A smaller number of institutions that only accept TOEFL.

If your target is clear, check the university or visa route's accepted tests page. If your target is vague, IELTS covers more ground.

Both tests cost more than most people expect. IELTS and TOEFL fees are in a similar range, with slight variation by test type and date. Check current fees on IDP Sri Lanka, British Council, or the ETS TOEFL site — prices change.

**Availability in Sri Lanka:**

- IELTS test dates are frequent, both in Colombo and occasionally at other centres.
- TOEFL iBT is also offered in Colombo; test slots are less frequent than IELTS.

If you need to test soon, IELTS almost always has a nearer date.

## Which is easier?

Honestly, neither is "harder." They're different. What matters is which plays to your strengths.

**IELTS tends to feel easier for:**

- Candidates with British-accent exposure (British Council classes, UK TV, UK English textbooks).
- People who prefer face-to-face speaking.
- Candidates who write faster by hand than on a keyboard.

**TOEFL tends to feel easier for:**

- Candidates with American-accent exposure.
- Fast typists comfortable with a keyboard.
- People who prefer no one watching them speak.

Most Sri Lankan students have grown up with British-leaning English through schooling. That tilts things toward IELTS without most candidates realising it.

**TOEFL** has the Official Guide to the TOEFL iBT and the official practice tests from ETS. Fewer high-quality third-party books than IELTS has, but plenty of practice through the ETS website.

Free material exists for both tests. Paid official material is still worth the money because it's closest to the real exam.

For most Sri Lankan candidates:

# Online English Classes in Sri Lanka: Do They Actually Work?

Online English classes exploded in Sri Lanka during 2020 and never fully went back. Some classes that went online then stayed online. Some students who tried them went back to classrooms. And a lot of parents are still unsure whether a Zoom class is a real class or a glorified YouTube lesson.

Here's the honest answer: online English works well for some learners and badly for others. Figuring out which group you're in matters more than any specific platform or institute.

## Who online English works for

### Adults wanting spoken English

Works well. Adults have the self-discipline to show up, the attention span to sit through 60–90 minutes, and the motivation that online classes need. The biggest gain is time — no commute means lessons you'd skip otherwise.

### A/L students and IELTS candidates

Works well, especially for IELTS. The teacher pool is wider online, mock tests are easier to deliver, and recorded speaking practice is actually easier online than in person.

### O/L students (Grades 10–11)

Mixed. Works if the class is small, the camera is on both sides, and written work is still marked. Fails if the class is a recorded video with no interaction.

### Primary school children

Works only if a parent sits nearby. Online classes for Grade 1–5 children without parental supervision tend to turn into expensive YouTube background noise. The child's face stays on screen, but their attention doesn't.

### Pre-school and Grade 1–2

Rarely works. Children this young need physical movement, real books in their hands, and a teacher who can see what they're actually doing. Online classes for 5-year-olds are mostly a convenience for parents, not a learning environment for the child.

## What makes an online class actually work

Not all online classes are the same. Six things separate the good from the weak.

### 1. Cameras on, both sides

If the teacher's camera is off, or students keep their cameras off, it's a video call in name only. The teacher can't see who's confused. Insist on cameras on, politely but firmly.

### 2. Small group sizes

A Zoom class with 40 students is a webinar. A class with 8–12 is a class. Ask the batch size before you pay.

### 3. Live, not recorded

Recorded videos have a place — for review, for catching up on a missed class, for self-study. They are not a substitute for a live class. If the "class" is only pre-recorded videos, you're buying a course, not a class.

### 4. Marked written work

Yes, even online. Students upload written work, teachers mark it, and marks come back with specific corrections. If written work isn't marked, writing doesn't improve.

### 5. Breakout rooms or turn-taking

For speaking classes, every student should speak every lesson. Good online teachers use breakout rooms or a strict turn order to make sure quiet students aren't just sitting in silence for an hour.

### 6. A real timetable

"Whenever the teacher is available" is not a schedule. A real online class has set days and times, like any in-person class.

## Where online beats in-person

- **Student access.** A student in Hambantota can join a class run by a strong teacher in Colombo. Before online classes, this wasn't possible.
- **IELTS preparation.** Mock tests, timed essays, recorded speaking — all work well online.
- **Adult spoken English.** No commute after work. Classes at 7pm instead of 9pm after travel.
- **Teacher quality options.** You're not limited to teachers within driving distance.
- **Recording for review.** Missed a class? Watch it later. A rare but real advantage.

## Where in-person still wins

- **Young children.** Grades 1–5 learn better with a teacher in the room.
- **Shy students.** Speaking up on camera is harder than speaking up in a room. For truly shy learners, in-person is gentler.
- **Students who need routine.** Some students study better when they physically leave the house to go to class.
- **Group energy.** A good classroom has a pulse to it that Zoom doesn't quite match.

## Common online class problems and how to spot them

Before you register, ask these questions:

- **"How many students are in the live class?"** Anything over 20 is too many.
- **"Can my child use a tablet, or do they need a laptop?"** Tablets are fine for younger children with parental help; older students need a keyboard for writing practice.
- **"How is homework submitted and marked?"** A real class has a clear answer: WhatsApp, email, Google Classroom. Vague answers mean marking doesn't happen.
- **"Do you record classes? Can I get the recording if my child misses one?"** A real institute does this. Random individual teachers often don't.

## A note on the technology

You don't need expensive equipment for online English classes to work.

- A stable internet connection (even 2 Mbps is usually fine for Zoom audio).
- A device with a camera. Laptop or tablet, not just a phone if possible.
- Headphones with a microphone. Cheap earphones with a mic cost under Rs. 1,000 and massively improve audio quality for the teacher.
- A quiet corner, not the middle of the living room during tea time.

## How to try online without committing

- Book a single trial class at an institute that offers one.
- Test the technology in advance — join the meeting link ten minutes before class.
- If the class works well for one month, extend. If it's not working, don't drag it out hoping it'll improve.

Online classes tend to reveal themselves fast. Within three or four sessions, you'll know whether it's working.

Written work is marked and returned through WhatsApp or email. **Are online English classes cheaper than in-person classes in Sri Lanka?**

Not always. Teacher time costs the same. Some online classes are cheaper because the batches are larger. If an online class is dramatically cheaper than an in-person class with the same teacher, check the batch size first.

**What age is too young for online English classes?**

Below Grade 3, online classes work only if a parent sits with the child. Below Grade 1, in-person is almost always better.

**Can online IELTS classes get you Band 7?**

Yes, if the class is live, small, and marks your writing and speaking. Recorded-only IELTS courses rarely get past Band 6.5 for most candidates.

A phone works for watching, but writing practice really needs a laptop or at least a tablet with a keyboard. For reading-heavy classes, a laptop is noticeably better.

# Why Your Child's English Isn't Improving (Honest Reasons)

You've been paying for an English class for a year. Maybe two. And your child's English looks pretty much the same as it did when they started. They still freeze when relatives visit. Their marks don't shift. They still say "I'm going to home" and "She don't know."

You're not imagining it, and it's not always the child's fault. Here are the six reasons we see most often, and what actually fixes each one.

## 1. The class is a reading class that calls itself a speaking class

Many "spoken English" classes in Sri Lanka are really reading and worksheet classes with a few group conversations thrown in. The child spends 90% of the lesson quiet, reading from the board or filling in blanks.

**What fixes it:** a class where every student speaks in every lesson. Small group size (8–12). Teacher calls on students by name. Real pair or small-group speaking time, not just "talk amongst yourselves."

Before you register, ask: "How often does my child actually speak in one class?" If the honest answer is "sometimes," keep looking.

## 2. No English outside the class

Two hours a week of English class can't overcome 70 hours a week of no English at all. Many children only hear English during their class and at school. That's not enough to build fluency.

**What fixes it:**

- A 15-minute English window at home each day.
- English audiobooks or cartoons in the background.
- Reading English storybooks at the child's level, aloud, with a parent.

None of this needs special materials. See our [guide to building English at home](https://woodberryplace.com/blog/help-child-speak-english-at-home/) for specifics.

## 3. Mistakes get punished, so the child stops trying

If every wrong word gets corrected, every sentence gets rewritten, every attempt gets a sigh — the child learns that English is unsafe. So they stop speaking, and then they stop improving.

**What fixes it:** adults in the child's life — parents, teachers, older siblings — listen to the whole sentence first, reply using the correct form, and don't jump in mid-word. Progress comes from attempts, not from perfection.

If your child's class corrects every sentence as it happens, consider whether the fear of mistakes is slowing them down more than the mistakes are.

## 4. The teacher is teaching the wrong level

A child in a class two levels above their English is lost. A child in a class two levels below is bored. Either way they stop improving.

**What fixes it:** ask the class to assess your child's current level — not just slot them into a group by age or grade. Good institutes do a short level check before placement. If a class doesn't, your child might be in the wrong room.

Signs your child is in the wrong level:

- They can't tell you what they did in the last lesson.
- They dread going.
- Their marks on class work are either 95%+ (too easy) or 40% or less (too hard).

## 5. The class drills grammar but never uses it

A child can list every tense and still not be able to say "I went to Galle last week." Grammar on paper is not grammar in the mouth.

**What fixes it:** classes that use the grammar in conversation and writing, not just fill-in-the-blank exercises. Ask what happens after a grammar lesson. If the answer is "a worksheet," that grammar isn't transferring.

## 6. Shyness is the real issue, not English

Some children are not struggling with English. They're struggling with speaking in front of people, in any language. English just happens to be the one that gets noticed because of the class.

**What fixes it:** patience, not pressure. Shy children open up in the right setting — one safe person, then a small group, then a class. Don't force them to perform. Don't single them out in front of relatives.

If shyness is the real issue, a good small-group class actually helps more than one-on-one, because the child gets to blend in and build confidence without being the centre of attention.

## What to do this week

If any of these sound familiar:

- **Watch one full lesson** of your child's class, if you're allowed to. You'll see fast whether real learning is happening.
- **Set up a 15-minute English window** at home. Pick a time, start today.
- **Read aloud together** three nights this week. Any short English book.
- **Stop correcting mid-sentence.** Just for a week. See what happens.

Give it four weeks. If nothing moves, think seriously about changing the class.

## When it's not the class

Sometimes the class is fine and the issue is elsewhere.

- A difficult school year or friendship problem can stall learning in every subject at once.
- A new sibling, a family move, or health issues eat attention.
- Sometimes a child is quietly decided: "I'm not good at English." Fixing that belief matters more than fixing the grammar.

If your child has gone through something hard in the last six months, give the English more time. It isn't broken — it's just not the priority right now.

## When switching classes is the right move

Four signs it's time to switch:

- Six months in, your child can't name one thing they learned last week.
- Written work comes back with only ticks, no corrections.
- The teacher changes every term.
- The child dreads it and can't say why.

Switching isn't disloyal. A class that isn't working doesn't become a class that works just because you've already paid.

**How long before I should expect to see my child's English improve?**

Real improvement usually shows at around 3–4 months of consistent class plus some English at home. If six months in you see nothing, something isn't working.

**Could it be that my child just isn't good at languages?**

Almost never. Children who grow up with Sinhala or Tamil are already managing one language; English is just a second one. If your child isn't improving, the method is usually the issue, not the ability.

Yes. School English in Sri Lanka tests reading, writing, and grammar — not speaking. A child can get Credit passes without ever saying a full English sentence out loud. The two skills need separate work.

Not purely for the language. English-medium schools help English, but they also change your child's whole school experience. A good spoken English class gives most of the language benefit without the upheaval.

# IELTS Writing Task 2: Band 7 Structure for Sri Lankan Candidates

Writing Task 2 is where most Sri Lankan IELTS candidates lose their band. Reading and Listening usually come in at 7+ for people with decent English; Speaking is manageable with practice. Writing stalls at 6 or 6.5 because the examiner is grading a specific skill — academic essay writing — not general English.

Here's the structure, the band criteria, and the specific mistakes we see from candidates every intake.

## The four things the examiner marks

Every IELTS essay is scored on four criteria, each 25% of your Task 2 band.

1. **Task Response** — did you answer the question that was asked?
2. **Coherence and Cohesion** — is your essay organised, with ideas flowing logically?
3. **Lexical Resource** — is your vocabulary varied and accurate?
4. **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — do you use a mix of sentence structures correctly?

For Band 7, you need at least 7 on each criterion. One weak criterion drags your overall band down.

## The essay structure that works at Band 7

Four paragraphs. Nothing fancy.

- **Introduction** — 2 to 3 sentences.
- **Body paragraph 1** — one main idea, developed.
- **Body paragraph 2** — a different main idea, developed.
- **Conclusion** — 2 sentences.

Total: around 270 to 300 words. The task minimum is 250. Writing much more than 320 usually introduces errors without adding marks.

### Introduction (write this in 3 minutes)

Two parts:

- **Paraphrase the question.** Don't copy — rewrite in your own words.
- **Your thesis.** State clearly what the essay will argue.

> Many governments argue that children should begin learning foreign languages in primary school rather than waiting until secondary education. This essay will argue that early language learning offers clear cognitive and practical benefits, though it works best when teaching is play-based rather than formal.

Notice: no "In today's modern world." No "It is a well-known fact that." Get to the point.

### Body paragraph 1 (10 minutes)

Pick one main idea. Develop it in four sentences:

- **Topic sentence** — the main idea.
- **Explanation** — why this is true.
- **Example** — one specific example. Sri Lankan examples are fine.
- **Link** — one sentence tying it back to the question.

> One clear benefit of early language learning is that young children acquire pronunciation more naturally than older learners. Research in language acquisition has repeatedly shown that children before the age of about ten are more flexible in picking up new sound patterns. In Sri Lanka, children who begin English in Grade 1 often speak with more natural rhythm than those who start in Grade 5, despite having similar exam results. For this reason, starting young offers a long-term advantage that later study cannot fully replace.

### Body paragraph 2 (10 minutes)

Same structure, different idea. If the question is a discussion, this paragraph can present the other side. If it's an opinion essay, this paragraph can offer a nuance or condition.

### Conclusion (3 minutes)

Two sentences:

- Restate your thesis in different words.
- Add a final thought — a recommendation, a condition, or a consequence.

> In conclusion, there are strong reasons to introduce foreign language learning early, particularly when the method suits a child's age. Governments considering this policy should invest as much in teacher training as in curriculum design, since the method matters as much as the age at which learning starts.

## The question types and what they want

Four essay types cover almost all Task 2 questions.

### Opinion essay

"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

Pick a side. Say so clearly in the introduction. Both body paragraphs support your side (or one supports and one acknowledges the other side).

### Discussion essay

"Discuss both views and give your opinion."

One body paragraph on each view. Your opinion comes in either the introduction or the conclusion — pick one and be clear.

### Problem-solution

"What are the problems and what can be done?"

One body paragraph on the problem(s), one on solutions. Be specific — "better education" is not a solution; "including financial literacy in the Grade 10 curriculum" is.

### Two-part question

"Why is this happening and how can it be addressed?"

Each body paragraph answers one part of the question. Simple.

## What pulls your band down from 7 to 6

### Not answering the actual question

If the question asks about causes and solutions, and you write about advantages and disadvantages, you're off topic. Band drops to 5 or 6 regardless of how good the English is. Read the question twice. Underline the question words. Plan to those.

### Using only simple sentences

"People are learning English. English is useful. Jobs need English." Three sentences, three simple structures. Band caps at 6 for Grammar.

**Fix:** mix sentence types.
- Simple: "English is useful."
- Compound: "English is useful, and most Sri Lankan jobs now require it."
- Complex: "Because most Sri Lankan jobs now require English, demand for classes has grown."

Use all three in your essay. Band 7 for grammar is partly about range, not just accuracy.

### Memorised phrases

"In today's fast-paced world" and "Every coin has two sides" are on every examiner's banned list. They're not clever, they're common, and they signal a memorised essay.

### Vague or missing examples

Band 7 Task Response needs developed ideas. A claim without an example is a weak claim.

### Word count under 250

Essays below 250 words are penalised. Count your lines — you need about 25 to 30 lines in normal handwriting.

## Timing: 40 minutes, strictly

- 3 minutes: read the question twice, plan.
- 5 minutes: introduction.
- 10 minutes: body paragraph 1.
- 10 minutes: body paragraph 2.
- 7 minutes: conclusion and proofread.
- 5 minutes buffer: for the paragraph that takes longer than you expected.

Do not go over 40. Task 1 is still waiting, and running over on Task 2 costs you more than finishing a weaker essay.

## Vocabulary for Band 7

You don't need rare words. You need common words used precisely and some range.

Good moves:

- Use topic-specific vocabulary. For an education question: *curriculum, pedagogy, literacy, lifelong learning, cognitive development.* Three or four such words lift the band.
- Use linking words correctly, not everywhere. *However, moreover, consequently, nevertheless* — one or two per paragraph is enough.
- Avoid very formal words you've never used before. Band 7 is for accurate use, not for showing off.

Bad moves:

- Thesaurus-swapping. "Happy" becomes "exuberant," "big" becomes "copious." If the word feels off, don't use it.
- Trying to use ten linking words in one paragraph. Reads as a transition salad.

## Sri Lankan context in your essays

Examples from Sri Lanka are fine — even preferred. Examples make your essay specific, which examiners reward.

> For example, in Sri Lanka's tourism sector, which employs over 150,000 people, English is often the single hiring factor that separates applicants.

Notice the pattern: specific number, specific country, specific effect. Beats "In many countries, English is important for jobs."

## Frequently asked questions

**Does Task 2 count more than Task 1?**

Yes — Task 2 is worth roughly two-thirds of your Writing band. If time is short, prioritise Task 2 in practice.

**Can I memorise essays for IELTS Writing Task 2?**

No. Examiners spot memorised essays easily, and they almost never match the exact question. Memorise structure and useful phrases — not full essays.

**How many words should my IELTS essay be?**

Aim for 270 to 300. Under 250 is penalised; over 320 usually adds errors without adding marks.

**Is it okay to use Sri Lankan examples in my IELTS essay?**

Yes, and often better than invented global examples. Specific real examples score higher than vague international references.

# Spoken English for Adults in Sri Lanka: Where to Actually Start

You can read English. You've written emails in English. You can follow an English film. But the moment you need to speak — a meeting, a new client, an interview, a conversation with a foreign guest — something freezes. The words are there and nothing comes out.

This is the most common pattern we see with adult learners in Sri Lanka. It's not a knowledge problem. It's a usage problem. And it's fixable at any age, from 22 to 62.

## Why adults freeze on spoken English

Three reasons cover most people.

- **School taught you written English.** Grammar rules, essay writing, comprehension. You were almost never asked to speak more than two sentences aloud.
- **You've been punished for mistakes.** Somewhere along the way — a teacher, a boss, an older relative — someone made fun of your English or corrected you in front of others. The brain remembers.
- **You don't speak English anywhere.** Your home, your friends, your workplace — all Sinhala or Tamil. English has no place in your week.

None of these mean you can't speak. They mean your speaking muscles haven't been used.

## What doesn't work (be honest)

- **Grammar books.** You already know the grammar. More rules won't help.
- **Vocabulary apps.** You can learn 2,000 words on an app and still freeze in a conversation. Words you can recognise aren't the same as words you can say.
- **Watching more English films.** Useful for listening, useless for speaking if you never speak back.
- **"Think in English."** Classic advice, almost impossible to follow on purpose.

## What works: speak every day, badly if needed

The single thing that moves adult English is talking to other people in English, out loud, imperfectly, consistently.

Three to five times a week. Even 15 minutes. Even badly.

Where to find this:

- **A spoken English class with other adults.** The gold standard. You get peers who are also there to speak, not to judge.
- **A regular conversation partner.** A colleague, a friend, a language-exchange partner. Someone who meets you for 20 minutes twice a week.
- **A patient family member.** If you have one.

Note what's not on the list: solo apps that use AI voices. They don't carry the social weight of speaking to a human. Your nerves don't fire the same way, which is both a comfort and a limitation.

## The 90-day plan

### Days 1–30: unfreezing

Goal: get to the point where you can say three sentences in English without going red.

- **Daily:** 10 minutes talking to yourself in English. Narrate what you're doing in the kitchen, in the car, while getting ready. It sounds silly. It works.
- **Three times a week:** a 20-minute English conversation with one person. Any topic.
- **Rule:** don't correct yourself mid-sentence. Finish the sentence, right or wrong.
- **One podcast or video** per week, in English, that you can retell in two sentences afterwards.

### Days 31–60: expanding

Goal: hold a 5-minute English conversation on a familiar topic.

- **Daily:** 10 minutes of structured speaking — pick one topic (your job, your weekend, your opinion on a news item), speak for 2 minutes, record, listen back.
- **Three times a week:** 30-minute conversation, this time on topics slightly outside your comfort zone. Travel, work problems, opinions.
- **Add a reading-aloud habit:** 5 minutes a day, any English text, read out loud. It trains your mouth to form the sounds comfortably.
- **Start tracking fillers.** Write down the words you use when stuck — *actually, like, you know, how to say.* Noticing is the first step to replacing them.

### Days 61–90: real-world use

Goal: switch to English in at least one real situation per week — a work meeting, a customer conversation, a client email read aloud.

- **Daily:** 15 minutes of speaking practice, now including Q&A format (practise answering questions, not just monologue).
- **Four times a week:** conversation, now longer sessions — 30 to 45 minutes.
- **One real-world English use per week.** This is the hard one. Switch a normal Sinhala or Tamil conversation at work or with a friend to English for 10 minutes. Take the discomfort.
- **Review recordings.** Pick one 2-minute speaking recording from a week ago. Listen. You'll hear improvement you didn't feel in the moment.

## What success looks like at Day 90

Not perfect. Not "fluent." But:

- You can introduce yourself and talk about your job for two minutes in English.
- You can have a 15-minute conversation without switching back to your first language.
- You can answer questions about your opinion without freezing, even if the grammar isn't textbook.
- You feel less embarrassed about mistakes.

That's a real win, and most adults never get to this point because they keep waiting to be "ready."

## Common adult-learner mistakes

### Waiting until your English is "good enough" before speaking

Speaking is how your English gets good enough. You can't study your way to fluency.

### Comparing yourself to confident speakers

That cousin who's fluent in English either grew up in an English-speaking home or lived abroad. Comparing yourself to them makes you feel worse without teaching you anything.

### Trying to lose your accent

Nobody actually needs a British or American accent. Clear Sri Lankan English is a perfectly good target and more honest than a fake accent that slips the moment you're tired.

### Signing up for a course and never attending

The plan above doesn't work if you miss sessions. Pick a realistic cadence — two sessions a week beats five sessions a week that you keep rescheduling.

## Where classes help

Adult spoken English classes work well when:

- The group is small (6–12 adults, similar level).
- The teacher creates structured speaking tasks, not just open "conversation time."
- Mistakes are not punished.
- There's homework — small tasks that extend into your week.

Signs an adult class is weak:

- Too many beginners mixed with intermediates — the intermediates don't get challenged, the beginners get overwhelmed.
- The teacher talks 80% of the time.
- No speaking homework, no recording, no reflection.

# A/L General English Paper: The Shortest Fair Guide

A/L General English is the paper students treat as an afterthought. "It doesn't count for university entry, no?" "My A/L stream is Science, English is just extra, no?"

Both of those are half-true and half-dangerous. Yes, your main subjects carry the Z-score. No, the English grade doesn't disappear — it sits on your certificate forever, and plenty of employers look at it. Treat it like a paper worth 30 focused hours over the two years of A/Ls and you'll pass it comfortably with a decent grade.

Here's the guide.

## What the paper tests

The A/L General English paper, as set by the Department of Examinations, usually covers:

- **Reading comprehension** — one or two passages with mixed question types.
- **Vocabulary in context** — meaning from surrounding sentences.
- **Grammar** — practical, not deeply technical.
- **Writing** — short pieces and one longer piece.
- **Note-making or summary** — turning a longer text into points.

Exact paper structure can shift year to year, so check the current syllabus on doenets.lk before you plan around specific question counts. The skills tested don't change much.

## How it's graded

A/L General English is reported as an A, B, C, S, or F — the same grading as A/L subjects — but it's calculated separately and doesn't count toward your Z-score. That's why students underrate it.

The grade still:

- Appears on your A/L certificate.
- Shows on job applications, visa applications, further-study applications.
- Gets looked at by local and overseas employers for entry-level roles.

An S pass in General English next to three A's in Science looks worse than a B next to the same A's. Easy to fix.

## Time budget: 30 hours over 18 months

Most students don't need 300 hours of A/L English study. They need 30 focused hours, spread out, mostly in the last six months before the exam.

A sample plan:

- **Months 1–12 of A/Ls:** read something in English weekly. That's it. A short article, a chapter, a page of a novel. Keep your English alive.
- **Months 13–15:** once a week, 30 minutes. Work through one grammar topic or one writing piece.
- **Months 16–18:** twice a week, 45 minutes. Past papers, timed sections, writing practice.

That's about 30–40 hours total — doable alongside Science or Commerce A/Ls without eating time from your main subjects.

## The specific areas to drill

### Reading comprehension

Not deep analysis — speed and accuracy on moderately academic passages.

- Read the questions before reading the passage.
- Underline the line where you find each answer.
- Don't translate in your head. Look for the answer word directly.

Practise with past papers more than anything else. Twenty minutes per comprehension passage is a reasonable target.

### Vocabulary in context

The paper tests your ability to figure out meaning from context, not your ability to memorise rare words.

- Learn common academic vocabulary at your level — the kind that appears in newspaper articles.
- When stuck on a word, ignore it and read the rest of the sentence. The context will carry most of the meaning.
- Watch for prefixes and suffixes: *in-, un-, dis-, -tion, -ment, -able*. They narrow the meaning fast.

### Grammar

The grammar section is practical — tenses in context, subject-verb agreement, prepositions, articles, basic transformations. Not grammar trivia.

- Spend time on tenses you actually misuse, not on tenses you already get right.
- Articles (a, an, the) trip up most Sri Lankan candidates. Learn the common patterns — zero article before plural general nouns, *the* for specific references.
- Prepositions — just memorise the common collocations. *Interested in*, *good at*, *afraid of*, *responsible for*. There's no rule — it's memory.

### Writing

Most candidates lose marks here because they treat it as "write something and finish."

- **Short pieces** (notices, messages, dialogues) have a specific format. Learn the format, follow it exactly.
- **Longer pieces** (letters, short essays) need structure: introduction, two or three body paragraphs, conclusion.
- **Word count** matters. Under-writing loses structure marks; over-writing adds errors.

### Note-making or summary

Underrated section — easy marks if you practise, lost marks if you don't.

- Note-making: use clean short phrases, consistent format, no full sentences.
- Summary: cut to roughly one-third of the original length, keep only the main points, no personal opinion.

## The timing habit

A/L General English isn't a time-pressured paper for most students if they practise — but timing drifts the moment you stop watching the clock.

- Do at least three full past papers under exam timing before the actual exam.
- Note where you ran over. Usually it's writing (because you wrote too much) or comprehension (because you read too slowly).
- Adjust. Practise again.

## What not to do

- **Don't memorise essays.** The topic changes every year.
- **Don't buy ten different workbooks.** Two or three good ones, plus past papers, is enough.
- **Don't skip the paper.** Students who treat it as optional often sit it unprepared and get an F for a paper they could have passed in their sleep.
- **Don't panic in the last month.** You can comfortably prepare for A/L General English in six focused weeks if you've been reading at all during A/Ls.

## The last six weeks

A realistic cram schedule if you've left it late:

- **Week 6:** one past paper, untimed. Mark it. See where you stand.
- **Week 5:** grammar review. Identify your three weak areas. Drill those.
- **Week 4:** writing practice. One short piece and one long piece, marked.
- **Week 3:** comprehension and vocabulary. Three past paper sections under timing.
- **Week 2:** two full past papers, timed. Mark both.
- **Week 1:** one final past paper. Review common mistakes. Don't learn anything new.

Our A/L General English programme in Matara runs weekly sessions that pace the full syllabus over 18 months without overloading students who already have three main subjects to manage. Past papers are marked by hand and returned with specific feedback. Intensive six-week revision batches run before each exam cycle for students who need a faster push.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does A/L General English count toward university entry?**

No — it doesn't count toward your Z-score. But the grade appears on your certificate and matters for employment and overseas applications.

**How much should I study for A/L General English?**

About 30–40 focused hours over 18 months, with more weight in the final six months. Less than that and you risk an S or F; much more and you're taking time away from your main subjects.

**Is A/L General English the same as A/L English (main subject)?**

No. A/L General English is a compulsory short paper for all A/L students. A/L English as a main subject is a full three-paper subject taken by students in the Arts or Commerce streams, covering language and literature in depth.

**Can I pass A/L General English with just past papers?**

If your English is already at a decent level — yes, past papers alone will probably get you a C or B. For an A, you'll want some targeted grammar and writing practice as well.

# English for Hotel and Tourism Jobs in Southern Sri Lanka

The tourism belt along Sri Lanka's south coast — Galle, Weligama, Mirissa, Tangalle, and around Matara — runs on English. Guests are mostly from Europe, Australia, and increasingly East Asia. The front-desk supervisor who speaks confident English earns more than the one who speaks fine Sinhala but struggles with English. The guide who can tell a good story in English gets better tips than the one who can't.

If you work — or want to work — in a hotel, villa, restaurant, or tour operation along the south coast, this is for you. Not grammar. Practical, job-ready English.

## What employers actually want

Owners and managers we've spoken to in Matara and Galle say the same things:

- **Listening first.** Can you understand what a guest is asking, even with their accent?
- **Short, clear responses.** You don't need to speak beautifully. You need to be understood.
- **The right tone.** Polite, warm, confident. Not stiff textbook English.
- **Handling complaints.** This is the skill that separates roles. A worker who can calm an unhappy guest is worth their weight in rupees.
- **Phone English.** Taking reservations, answering questions about rooms and rates.

What they don't test for:

- Complex grammar rules.
- Big vocabulary.
- A fake British or American accent.

Clear, calm, polite Sri Lankan English is a hireable skill. Chase that, not perfection.

## The 200 words that cover most situations

You don't need a huge vocabulary. You need the specific words of your role used confidently.

### Front desk

*Check-in, check-out, reservation, booking, confirmation, passport, ID, deposit, key card, floor, lift, breakfast time, pool, Wi-Fi password, invoice, tax, late checkout, early check-in, luggage, airport transfer.*

### Restaurant and bar

*Menu, special, starter, main course, dessert, drinks, vegetarian, vegan, allergy, spicy, mild, well done, medium rare, bill, service charge, tip, water bottle, ice, sparkling, still, happy hour.*

### Housekeeping

*Towels, sheets, pillows, blanket, mini bar, amenities, shampoo, soap, air conditioning, fan, remote, do-not-disturb, make up the room, extra bed.*

### Tourism and tours

*Pickup time, drop-off, itinerary, duration, guide, driver, entry ticket, sunrise, sunset, waterfall, viewpoint, safari, whale watching, surfing, lesson, beginner, advanced, lifejacket, wetsuit.*

Learn these cold. If you work in front of house, you should be able to use any of them in a sentence without thinking.

## The twelve phrases that do most of the work

If you're starting fresh, drill these first. They cover maybe 60% of daily guest interactions.

1. "Welcome, how can I help you?"
2. "May I have your name, please?"
3. "Just a moment, let me check."
4. "Would you like…?"
5. "I'm sorry about that — let me see what I can do."
6. "Breakfast is served from [time] to [time] in the [place]."
7. "The Wi-Fi password is [password], all small letters."
8. "Would you like me to call a tuk-tuk / taxi for you?"
9. "We have a table available at [time]."
10. "Can I offer you a cold towel / welcome drink?"
11. "Your room is on the [number] floor. The lift is this way."
12. "Have a lovely stay with us."

Say each one out loud 20 times until it comes out without thinking. Confidence shows in phrases you don't have to search for.

## Handling complaints — the skill that pays most

A complaining guest wants three things, in order:

- To feel heard.
- To be apologised to.
- To see a solution.

Scripts that cover 80% of situations:

- **Listen first.** "I'm so sorry — can you tell me what happened?"
- **Acknowledge.** "That's not the experience we want you to have."
- **Offer something.** "Let me speak with the manager and we'll make this right." Or: "I'll send someone up in five minutes to sort it out."
- **Follow up.** "I'll come back myself in ten minutes to make sure everything is okay."

Notice: no excuses, no "sir, sir, sir," no defensiveness. These four moves work on 80% of guests. The remaining 20% need a manager, and that's fine.

## Pronunciation: clear, not perfect

Sri Lankan English is a real variety of English. Guests understand it fine. What they struggle with:

- **Speed.** When nervous, Sri Lankan English speakers speed up. Slow down.
- **Word endings.** Say the final *s, t, d* clearly. "Guest" not "ges." "Ice" not "ai."
- **Volume.** Many hotel staff speak too quietly. Speak at the volume a guest across a lobby desk can hear comfortably.
- **Numbers.** Prices and times are where miscommunication happens. "Fifteen" vs "fifty." "Four pm" vs "five pm." Say numbers slowly and clearly.

You don't need a British accent. You need to be understood.

- **Record yourself.** At the end of your shift, pick three conversations you had with guests. Try to repeat them aloud in English, better than you managed in the moment.
- **Watch front-desk training videos** on YouTube from hotel chains. 10 minutes a day.
- **Shadow a colleague.** Someone at your hotel speaks better English than you. Listen to how they handle calls. Copy their phrasing.
- **Take one shift a week where you push yourself.** Use longer sentences, offer more, engage more. You'll be tired. You'll also improve faster.

## How to practise if you're preparing to apply

If you're not yet working in tourism and want to:

- **Start a dedicated tourism English course** if your English base is weak. A general spoken English class covers the foundations.
- **Memorise the 200 role-specific words** above for the role you want.
- **Prepare three stories** in English — one about yourself, one about Matara / your home area, one about Sri Lankan food. Guests love these.
- **Practise a mock interview** in English. Most hotel interviews test spoken English through a conversation, not a written test.

## Where to work (realistically) with each level

- **Basic English:** housekeeping, kitchen, maintenance, pool, driver. Decent wages, less customer-facing.
- **Intermediate English:** restaurant service, hotel reception support, tour support, drivers who also guide a bit.
- **Confident English:** reception supervisor, concierge, tour guide, sales, restaurant manager. These roles pay noticeably more and tip-opportunity is higher.

Improving your English isn't the only path to promotion, but it's the fastest one in this industry.

For a non-customer-facing role, basic working English is enough and can be built in 3–4 months. For reception or restaurant service, a confident level usually takes 6–12 months of consistent practice.

# IELTS Speaking: What Sri Lankan Candidates Get Wrong

The Speaking test is where Sri Lankan candidates most often leave marks on the table. Our students generally score well on Reading and Listening, pass Writing with work, and then come out of Speaking convinced they did fine — and get back a 6.0 or 6.5 when they expected 7.

Here's why that happens, and what to do differently.

## How the Speaking test is scored

Four criteria, each worth 25% of your Speaking band:

1. **Fluency and Coherence** — can you speak at length, logically, without long pauses?
2. **Lexical Resource** — do you use a varied, precise vocabulary?
3. **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — do you use a mix of sentence structures correctly?
4. **Pronunciation** — are you understood without strain?

None of these is about accent. Band 7 is reachable with a clear Sri Lankan accent. What matters is being understood easily, with range, at a natural pace.

## Mistake 1: one-word answers in Part 1

Part 1 is the warm-up — questions about your home, work or studies, hobbies.

Candidates often answer like this:

> Examiner: Do you enjoy cooking?
> Candidate: Yes.

Technically correct. Band 5. Fluency can't be assessed in one word.

### Fix

Give a two-sentence answer. One sentence directly answers. The second adds detail.

> Yes, I enjoy cooking, especially on weekends when I have time to try new recipes. My favourite thing to cook is a slow-cooked chicken curry — my grandmother's recipe.

That's enough. Don't go longer than three sentences in Part 1 — that's for Part 2.

## Mistake 2: running out of ideas in Part 2

Part 2 gives you a cue card with a topic and four bullet points. You get one minute to prepare, then speak for 1–2 minutes.

The classic mistake: candidate speaks for 40 seconds, then stops.

Silence in Part 2 costs you heavily on Fluency.

### Fix

- **Use the full minute of prep time.** Write quick notes — two or three words per bullet point.
- **If you run out of ideas, repeat yourself using different words.** Describe something again from a different angle. Give an example. Compare it to something.
- **Speak for the full two minutes.** The examiner stops you. You don't stop yourself.

A rough rule: if you think you're running long, keep going. If you think you're done, you're probably at 50 seconds.

## Mistake 3: memorised answers

Candidates learn full answers to common Part 2 cards — "describe a book you read," "describe a place you visited" — and recite them.

Examiners hear this constantly. They move you to a different topic in Part 3, which you weren't ready for, and your band drops hard.

### Fix

- Don't memorise answers. Memorise frameworks. For "describe a place," your framework is: name the place, when and why I went, what I did there, why it matters to me. Fill the framework with real content on the day.
- Practise with uncommon topics so you're not dependent on a specific card.

## Mistake 4: filler words

*Ah, uh, like, basically, actually, you know, how to say.*

These happen when you're thinking. The occasional filler is fine. Constant fillers drop Fluency.

### Fix

- Record yourself for 2 minutes on any topic.
- Count fillers.
- Over a few weeks, replace fillers with silent thinking pauses. A short pause with a calm face is fine. A string of *ahhhs* isn't.

## Mistake 5: simple sentences only

"I live in Matara. Matara is nice. I like my school." Three sentences, all simple. Band caps at 6 for Grammar.

### Fix

Mix in longer structures:

- Compound: "I live in Matara, and I've lived here for most of my life."
- Complex: "Because my family has been in Matara for generations, I feel very connected to the town."
- Relative clause: "Matara, which sits right on the south coast, is known for its beaches."

You don't need every sentence long. You need some variety.

## Mistake 6: translating in your head

Candidates think the sentence in Sinhala or Tamil first, then translate into English. You can hear it in the pauses.

### Fix

- Practise speaking directly in English. Narrate your day in English to yourself.
- Speak faster than feels comfortable at first. Fluency practice is about breaking the translation habit.
- Read English aloud daily. Trains your mouth to form English directly.

## Mistake 7: trying to sound formal

Written essay vocabulary doesn't belong in Speaking. Words like *henceforth, therefore, consequently, elucidate* sound weird in conversation.

### Fix

Use natural spoken English. Linking words for speaking are things like *so, actually, I mean, like I said, that's a good question.* The last one is especially useful in Part 3 — gives you two seconds to think.

## Mistake 8: pronouncing word endings quietly

Many Sri Lankan English speakers soften final sounds — *s, t, d* at the ends of words. The examiner has to work harder to understand, which lowers your Pronunciation score.

### Fix

- Practise minimal pairs: *walk* vs *walks*, *trip* vs *tripped*, *pass* vs *past*.
- Record a short reading. Listen back. If word endings disappear, slow down and pronounce them fully.

## Mistake 9: Part 3 going too short

Part 3 is about abstract discussion. The examiner asks something general, and candidates give a one-sentence answer.

> Examiner: How has technology changed the way people learn?
> Candidate: It's easier now. People can learn online.

Too short. Band drops for both Fluency and Task Response.

### Fix

Answer in three parts: direct answer, explanation, example.

> Technology has changed learning massively — mostly for the better. People can now take courses from universities abroad without leaving home, which wasn't possible twenty years ago. For instance, many Sri Lankan professionals do their master's degrees online with UK universities now, which saves them having to leave their jobs or families.

Three sentences. Direct, developed, specific.

## Mistake 10: not asking the examiner to repeat

Candidates are afraid to say they didn't understand, so they answer a different question than the one that was asked.

### Fix

It is completely acceptable to say:

- "Sorry, could you repeat the question?"
- "Could you rephrase that, please?"

Only in Parts 1 and 3. In Part 2, you work from the written card.

Doing this correctly doesn't lower your band. Answering the wrong question does.

## A realistic weekly practice plan

- **Daily, 10 minutes:** speak for 2 minutes on any topic, recorded. Listen back. Note one thing to improve next time.
- **Three times a week:** full Part 2 practice with a timer. Prepare for 1 minute, speak for 2 minutes. Different topic each time.
- **Twice a week:** Part 3 discussion with a partner or tutor. Not solo — Part 3 is best practised live.
- **Weekly:** full mock Speaking test. Three parts, timed, recorded.

Four weeks of this moves most candidates up half a band in Speaking.

## On test day

- Smile when you walk in. It changes your voice and the examiner's first impression.
- Treat it as a conversation, not a test. The examiner is not your enemy.
- Don't apologise for mistakes. If you catch yourself, say the correction naturally and move on.
- Breathe before Part 2. One slow breath while you take your notes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does my accent affect my IELTS Speaking band?**

Not the accent itself — as long as you can be understood easily. Candidates with clear Sri Lankan accents regularly score Band 7 and above.

**How long is the IELTS Speaking test?**

11 to 14 minutes total. Part 1 is 4–5 minutes, Part 2 is 3–4 minutes (including 1 minute of preparation), Part 3 is 4–5 minutes.

**Can I use "like" and "you know" in IELTS Speaking?**

Occasionally, yes — real spoken English has them. Constantly, no — too many fillers lower your Fluency band.

**What if I don't understand the Part 2 topic?**

You still have to speak on it. There's no changing the card. If the topic is unfamiliar, focus on any angle you can honestly discuss — even if you have to interpret the prompt loosely.

# Phonics for Sri Lankan Kids: A Simple Starter

Your four-year-old can recognise the shapes of letters. They know "A is for apple." They might even write their name. But when you give them a simple English word they've never seen — *cat*, *mat*, *sun* — they look at you as though it's a puzzle. That's because they haven't been taught phonics yet, only letter names.

Phonics is the step that turns letter recognition into actual reading. For Sri Lankan children whose first language is Sinhala or Tamil, it's the single most useful thing a parent can teach in the pre-school years.

## What phonics actually is

Phonics teaches children the **sounds** that letters make, not just their **names**.

- The letter A's **name** is "ay."
- The letter A's **sound** (short) is "a," as in *cat*.

When a child knows letter sounds, they can look at an unfamiliar word like *bat* and sound it out: "buh — a — tuh — bat." They've just read a word they've never seen. That's the beginning of independent reading.

Most Sri Lankan children are taught letter names first and never taught sounds, which is why many struggle with reading English until Grade 3 or beyond.

## Why phonics works especially well for Sri Lankan children

English is tricky because the same letter makes different sounds in different words. But about 80% of basic English words follow phonics rules reliably. If a child masters those rules, reading most storybooks becomes possible around age 5 or 6.

Sinhala and Tamil are more phonetic languages than English — each letter makes one sound almost every time. Children who already know Sinhala or Tamil letters are used to the idea that letters make sounds. Phonics in English fits naturally with that habit.

## The four stages

### Stage 1: Letter sounds (ages 3–5)

Learn the single sound each letter makes.

- Short vowel sounds: **a** (apple), **e** (egg), **i** (insect), **o** (orange), **u** (umbrella).
- Consonants: start with b, c, d, f, g, h, j, k, l, m, n, p, r, s, t.

Teach one new sound every few days. Don't rush.

### Stage 2: Blending (ages 4–6)

The magic step. Children learn to put sounds together into words.

- c + a + t = cat
- s + u + n = sun
- m + a + p = map

This is where children often get stuck for a few weeks. Keep it playful. Use three-letter words only at this stage.

### Stage 3: Common patterns (ages 5–7)

- **Digraphs** — two letters that make one sound. *sh, ch, th, ck, ng.*
- **Magic e** — the *e* at the end that changes the vowel. *cap* becomes *cape*. *hop* becomes *hope*.
- **Vowel teams** — *ee, oo, ai, oa.*

By the end of this stage, a child can read most storybook text with help.

### Stage 4: Tricky words (ages 6–8)

Words that break the rules and just have to be memorised. *The, said, was, were, one, two, you, where, there.* These get a sticker list at home.

## What to use at home

You don't need to buy a kit. These work:

- **Three-letter word flashcards.** Make them. Cat, mat, bat, hat, rat. Point and sound them out together.
- **Magnetic letters on the fridge.** Children build words themselves. Cheap and surprisingly effective.
- **Picture books at the right level.** Oxford Reading Tree, Ladybird Key Words, Biff and Chip — if you can get your hands on them. Local-library stories work too.
- **One phonics app, not ten.** Many are fine. Pick one. Stop looking for the perfect app.
- **A whiteboard.** Children love writing and wiping. Letters and small words stick faster this way than in a notebook.

## How long each day

This matters more than what you use.

- **Ages 3–4:** 5 minutes, maybe twice a day. More is too much.
- **Ages 4–5:** 10 minutes, once or twice a day.
- **Ages 5–7:** 15–20 minutes. Can be longer if the child is enjoying it.

Phonics is not a subject you cram. It's a daily habit, closer to toothbrushing than homework.

## Common parent mistakes

### Teaching letter names and sounds together, confusingly

"A says 'a,' and the letter A is also called 'ay.'" This confuses young children. Teach **sounds first, exclusively.** Add letter names later, once sounds are solid.

### Moving to full words too soon

A child who hasn't nailed the five short vowel sounds can't blend. Rushing to full words fails. Spend time on sounds alone.

### Using a mix of English accents

Some phonics apps teach with strong American accents, others with British. Pick one source and stick with it for the first six months. Consistent sound patterns matter.

### Correcting every mistake

If the child says "ssun" instead of "sun," don't say "no, that's wrong." Model the correct sound: "Sun, yes! Can you hear — sss-uh-nnn, sun?" Keep it light.

### Making it feel like school

Children aged 3–6 learn through play. Phonics works best inside stories, songs, and games. The moment it becomes a worksheet marathon, children switch off.

## Signs your child is ready for the next stage

- They recognise most letter sounds in isolation → ready for blending.
- They can blend three-letter words comfortably → ready for digraphs.
- They can read simple sentences in a familiar book → ready for magic e and vowel teams.
- They can read most beginner storybooks with some help → ready for broader reading.

Don't push ahead until the current stage is comfortable. A shaky foundation will hold a child back for years.

## When to get help

If your child is five or six and still struggling to blend simple words, it's worth having a short assessment with a teacher. It's not always a problem — some children just develop later — but occasional reading differences (dyslexia, phonological processing issues) are easier to support early.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our Little Learners programme in Matara uses phonics as the backbone of early English learning. Classes are play-based, with songs, stories, and games — not worksheets. The focus is on children aged 4–7 who are starting English for the first time, or who need to build a stronger foundation before Grade 3.

## Frequently asked questions

**At what age should I start phonics with my child?**

Around age 3 or 4 for letter sounds, informally. Around age 5 for structured blending. Earlier than 3 is usually too soon — children learn more from being read to at that age.

**Does phonics work for Sri Lankan children whose first language isn't English?**

Yes, and often very well. Children who already know Sinhala or Tamil letter sounds adapt quickly to the idea that English letters have sounds too.

**How long until my child can read storybooks after starting phonics?**

With consistent daily practice, most children can read simple storybooks within 6–9 months of starting Stage 1.

**Is phonics enough, or do I also need to teach whole words?**

Phonics is the foundation. You'll still teach sight words — the irregular words like *the*, *said*, and *was* — but phonics does the heavy lifting for 80% of English reading.

# O/L English Grammar: The 7 Rules That Show Up Every Year

If you spend six months reviewing every grammar rule in the syllabus, you'll know everything and remember nothing by exam day. Here's the opposite approach: seven rules that appear on almost every O/L English paper in Sri Lanka, and the specific errors that cost students marks.

Nail these seven and you'll handle most of the grammar section without panic.

## Rule 1: Subject-verb agreement

The verb matches the subject in number.

- **Singular:** The boy **walks** to school.
- **Plural:** The boys **walk** to school.

Easy when the subject is obvious. Students lose marks when the sentence hides the real subject.

### Where students slip

- **One of the**: "One of the students **has** passed." (Not *have* — the subject is *one*, which is singular.)
- **Each / every**: "Each of the children **has** a pencil." (Not *have*.)
- **Either / neither**: "Neither my brother nor my sister **is** home." (Not *are*.)
- **Collective nouns** like *team, family, government*: usually treated as singular in formal writing. "The team **is** winning."

### Quick drill

Rewrite each wrong sentence correctly:
- "Every one of my friends are coming to the party."
- "The list of names were on the table."
- "Each of these books cost two hundred rupees."

## Rule 2: Tense consistency

Pick a tense and stay in it. Switching tenses mid-paragraph confuses the reader and costs marks.

### Where students slip

Writing a narrative in past tense and then slipping into present:

> I **went** to school yesterday. When I **arrive**, the bell **was** ringing.

*Arrive* should be *arrived*. Simple past throughout.

### The fix

After you write a paragraph, scan every verb. If your opening sentence is past tense, every verb except quoted speech should be past tense.

### Quick drill

Correct the tense in these sentences:
- "She opened the door and sees her brother."
- "We were at the beach and we swim for an hour."
- "The teacher asks the class to be quiet and gave out the papers."

## Rule 3: Articles — a, an, the

The small words that cost big marks.

- **a / an** — one of many, mentioned first time. *A book, an apple.*
- **the** — specific one, already known. *The book on my desk.*
- **No article** — general plurals, uncountable nouns. *Children are noisy. Water is wet.*

### Where students slip

- Missing *the* before superlatives: "She is best student in the class." → "She is **the** best student."
- Missing *the* before unique things: "Sun rises in east." → "**The** sun rises in **the** east."
- Using *the* with uncountable general ideas: "**The** happiness is important." → "Happiness is important."

### Quick drill

Add articles where needed:
- "I saw ___ elephant at ___ zoo. ___ elephant was eating ___ banana."
- "She plays ___ piano beautifully."
- "He is ___ best cricketer I know."

## Rule 4: Prepositions after verbs and adjectives

These don't follow rules — they have to be memorised. The good news: a short list covers the paper.

### Must-know collocations

- *interested in*, *good at*, *bad at*, *afraid of*, *proud of*, *tired of*
- *responsible for*, *famous for*, *known for*, *suitable for*
- *married to*, *similar to*, *related to*, *kind to*, *rude to*
- *angry with*, *pleased with*, *disappointed with*
- *agree with* (a person), *agree on* (an idea), *agree to* (a proposal)
- *depend on*, *rely on*, *insist on*, *concentrate on*

### Where students slip

- "She is good **in** English." → "She is good **at** English."
- "I am interested **about** music." → "I am interested **in** music."
- "He is married **with** my cousin." → "He is married **to** my cousin."

### Quick drill

Fill in the correct preposition:
- "He's responsible ____ the whole project."
- "She's not very good ____ maths."
- "They're similar ____ each other in many ways."

## Rule 5: Direct and indirect speech

The transformation that appears almost every year.

### Direct speech

Uses the actual words, in quotation marks.

> She said, "I am going to Colombo tomorrow."

### Indirect (reported) speech

Reports what was said, usually with tense shifting backwards.

> She said that she **was** going to Colombo the **next day**.

### Key changes

- **Tense goes back one step**: *am* → *was*; *will go* → *would go*; *has eaten* → *had eaten*.
- **Time and place words change**: *tomorrow* → *the next day*; *yesterday* → *the previous day*; *here* → *there*; *now* → *then*.
- **Pronouns shift** to match the speaker: *I* becomes *he* or *she*; *we* becomes *they*.
- **Questions become statements**: "Are you coming?" → "She asked whether I was coming."

### Where students slip

Forgetting to shift pronouns, or keeping the question word order:

- Wrong: "He asked that where I was going."
- Right: "He asked where I was going."

### Quick drill

Convert to indirect speech:
- Ramya said, "I am tired."
- "Have you finished your work?" asked the teacher.
- "I will see you tomorrow," he said.

## Rule 6: Active and passive voice

Another common transformation on the paper.

### Active

Subject does the action.

> The teacher marked the papers.

### Passive

The action is done to the subject.

> The papers were marked by the teacher.

### Formula

- Active: Subject + verb + object
- Passive: Object + *be* (correct tense) + past participle + *by* + subject

### Where students slip

- Wrong tense of *be*: "The letter is **wrote** by him." → "The letter **was written** by him."
- Omitting *be*: "The cake eaten by the children." → "The cake **was** eaten by the children."

### Quick drill

Convert to passive:
- Sri Lanka won the match.
- Someone has stolen my bag.
- The farmer plants rice every year.

## Rule 7: Conditional sentences

Three common types appear on the paper.

### Type 1 — Real possibility (future)

> If it **rains**, we **will stay** home.

- *If* clause: present simple.
- Main clause: *will* + base verb.

### Type 2 — Hypothetical present/future

> If I **had** more money, I **would travel** to Japan.

- *If* clause: past simple.
- Main clause: *would* + base verb.

### Type 3 — Impossible past

> If she **had studied**, she **would have passed**.

- *If* clause: past perfect (*had* + past participle).
- Main clause: *would have* + past participle.

### Where students slip

- Mixing Type 2 and Type 3: "If I **had** more money, I **would have gone** to Japan."
- Using *would* in the *if* clause: "If I **would** have more money…" → wrong. *If* clauses never take *would*.

### Quick drill

Complete the sentence:
- If I were you, I ______ (study) harder.
- If he had told us, we ______ (help) him.
- If it rains tomorrow, we ______ (not go) to the beach.

## How to practise all seven

Not by reading grammar books. By doing paper questions.

- Download five past papers from doenets.lk.
- Do only the grammar sections, timed.
- Mark against the marking scheme.
- Note which rule you got wrong each time.
- The next week, do the same five rule-types from a different past paper.

Four weeks of this covers the seven rules thoroughly. Better than any grammar textbook.

Our O/L English programme in Matara drills these seven rules directly into weekly past-paper practice rather than teaching them as separate lessons. The point is to catch errors in context, where they actually show up in the exam, not in isolated worksheets.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much grammar do I need to know for O/L English?**

Less than most students think. The seven rules above, plus basic punctuation and sentence structure, cover 90% of the grammar marks.

**Is grammar more important in Paper I or Paper II?**

Paper II has more grammar-specific questions — transformations, gap-fills — while Paper I tests grammar indirectly through comprehension and vocabulary in context.

**Should I use a grammar reference book?**

One good one is enough — the Cambridge *English Grammar in Use* (intermediate level) is the standard. Don't buy multiple.

**Is Sri Lankan English grammar different from British English grammar?**

No, the grammar is the same. Spelling and some vocabulary differ slightly, but the O/L paper uses standard British English conventions throughout.

# Teaching English to a 4-Year-Old: What Works, What Doesn't

Four is a lovely age to start English. Children aren't shy yet. They copy sounds effortlessly. They think everything is a game. But four-year-olds also have the attention span of a butterfly, and teaching them English looks nothing like teaching an older child. If you try to use worksheets and drills at this age, you'll get a bored child and no English.

Here's what actually works with Sri Lankan 4-year-olds, drawn from years of running Little Learners classes and talking to parents.

## The three principles

Before anything else, three rules shape everything:

1. **Learning through play, not through study.** If it feels like school, they'll resist.
2. **Short sessions, often.** Ten minutes four times a day beats one hour once a day.
3. **Zero pressure.** No "say it properly," no corrections, no "do it again." A four-year-old who feels judged shuts down.

Keep these in mind through every activity below.

## What actually works

### Storytime in English

Read a short English picture book every day. Not a textbook. A storybook with pictures — *The Very Hungry Caterpillar*, *Brown Bear Brown Bear*, *We're Going on a Bear Hunt*, or anything with a repeating pattern.

- Point to pictures as you read. Say the word.
- Let your child point to pictures they recognise.
- Read the same book over and over. Children love repetition. Repetition is how vocabulary sticks.

Ten minutes a day, consistent. This alone builds a huge vocabulary over a year.

### Songs and rhymes

Children's English songs — *Twinkle Twinkle*, *Head Shoulders Knees and Toes*, *Old MacDonald*, *If You're Happy and You Know It* — teach vocabulary and rhythm without any effort.

- Sing while doing other things — in the car, at bath time, while cooking.
- Do the actions. Movement helps memory.
- YouTube has endless children's song playlists. Pick one with clear pronunciation and loop it.

### Daily routines in English

Pick one or two parts of the day to do in English:

- **Bath time:** "Let's wash your hair. Where are your toes? Is the water warm?"
- **Mealtime:** "What's on your plate? Is it hot? Can you say 'thank you'?"
- **Getting dressed:** "Red T-shirt or blue T-shirt today?"

Twenty minutes a day across these moments, in English, teaches more vocabulary than any flashcard set.

### Colours, shapes, animals, body parts

The classic early-English vocabulary groups. Teach them through pointing games:

- "Can you find something red?"
- "Where is your nose? Where is my nose?"
- "What's this animal? What sound does it make?"

Make it a game, not a lesson. The score is laughter, not correctness.

### Simple instructions

Give your child short English instructions to follow:

- "Bring me the ball."
- "Put the book on the table."
- "Jump five times."

Following instructions in English — without translating in their head — builds real comprehension fast.

### Short videos

A short English cartoon episode (10 minutes), then a conversation about what happened.

- "Who was in the story?"
- "What did the elephant do?"
- "Was he happy or sad?"

Passive watching builds nothing. Watching plus talking afterwards builds a lot.

## What doesn't work

### Worksheets

A four-year-old filling in blanks or copying letters for 30 minutes is a four-year-old who will hate English by age 5. Writing can wait. Oral and listening comprehension come first.

### Flashcards as drills

Flashcards can be used in games ("find the apple," "match the colours") — fine. Rapid-fire quizzing with flashcards — "What's this? What's this? What's this?" — stops being fun at word four and stays unfun for life.

### Expecting them to speak on command

Many parents want their child to perform English for visiting relatives. "Say good morning in English, say your name in English." The child freezes, the parent gets embarrassed, the child learns that English causes embarrassment. Skip this.

### Correcting pronunciation

If your child says "ellyfant," do not say "no, elephant." Just say "yes, the elephant!" and move on. The correct version is now in the air. They'll adjust naturally.

### Only screens

An iPad with an English app for an hour isn't teaching English. It's teaching how to use an iPad. Real spoken interaction with a human — you, a teacher, another child — does far more at this age.

### Long sessions

A 60-minute "English class" for a four-year-old is a 60-minute meltdown waiting to happen. Fifteen minutes is the upper limit for focused activity at this age.

## A realistic weekly plan

### Daily (15–20 minutes total, in small bursts)

- 5 minutes of storybook reading.
- 10 minutes of English-speaking during a routine — bath, breakfast, dressing.
- 5 minutes of a song.

### Weekly

- One short English cartoon with a conversation afterwards.
- One new vocabulary group (fruits one week, animals another).
- One playdate or social moment where the child hears or uses English with someone new.

### Not every day

- Worksheets. Avoid them at four.
- Writing practice. Save for five or six.

This builds a solid English foundation by age six without turning the child off.

## When to think about a class

Not every four-year-old needs a formal English class. But a good one can help if:

- You don't speak English yourself and can't model it comfortably at home.
- Your child is very social and thrives with other kids.
- You want structured exposure without having to plan it yourself.

What a good class looks like at this age:

- **Small group** — six to ten children.
- **Short lessons** — 45 minutes to an hour, not more.
- **Play-based** — songs, stories, games, movement. Not worksheets.
- **Warm teachers** — the child should come home happy.

Avoid classes that drill alphabet writing for an hour. At four, that's not learning. That's endurance.

## The "English only at home" trap

Some parents try to speak only English at home, thinking it will accelerate things. For most Sri Lankan families, this backfires:

- The parents' English isn't strong enough to carry full-day conversations naturally.
- The child misses out on rich Sinhala or Tamil conversation at the age when their first language is forming.
- The household gets quieter and more awkward.

A short daily English window is usually better than a half-hearted all-day attempt.

## Signs of progress at four

Every child moves differently, but after six months of consistent gentle exposure you can usually expect:

- Recognises 50–100 English words when they hear them.
- Can follow simple two-step English instructions ("Bring me your shoes and sit here.").
- Uses 20–40 English words themselves, mostly single words or two-word phrases.
- Enjoys English storybooks and asks for favourites.
- Doesn't freeze when someone speaks English to them.

If none of this has happened after six months, the issue is probably the method — not the child.

Sessions are short — around an hour — and parents are welcome to observe for the first few weeks until the child settles.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it okay to start English with my four-year-old if we only speak Sinhala or Tamil at home?**

Yes. Many Sri Lankan children start English at four without any English spoken at home and do very well. What matters is consistency, not a bilingual household.

**Will learning English confuse my child if they're still learning Sinhala or Tamil?**

No. Young children handle two or three languages at once without trouble. Each language fills its own space in their mind.

**Should my 4-year-old be writing English letters?**

Writing can wait until 5 or 6. Fine motor skills aren't ready at four, and pushing writing early builds frustration. Focus on listening and speaking.

**How much screen time is okay for English learning at this age?**

Around 15–30 minutes a day of good-quality English content with a conversation afterwards. More than that and screens stop teaching and start distracting.

# English Classes vs School English: What Private Tuition Actually Adds

Not every child needs English tuition. Some children get strong English at home, at school, through reading, and never see the inside of a tuition class. If that's your family, good — save the money and the evenings.

But many parents sign up for English tuition out of a vague feeling that it's necessary, without knowing what the class is actually supposed to add. Here's a clearer way to think about it.

## What school English does well

Sri Lankan schools, both government and private, cover a lot of English well:

- **Grammar foundations.** Tenses, parts of speech, sentence structure.
- **Reading comprehension.** Texts at age-appropriate levels, standard question types.
- **Writing basics.** Letter writing, essays, short compositions.
- **Vocabulary.** Through readers, textbooks, and classroom discussion.
- **Exam format familiarity.** Students see past paper questions from the O/L and A/L syllabus through the year.

If your child has a good English teacher and a reasonable class size, school English covers the written side of the subject reasonably well.

## What school English struggles with

Class sizes of 35 to 45 children mean three things get crowded out, whatever the teacher's skill:

### 1. Speaking practice

Thirty-five children, forty minutes, maybe one student speaks per minute for a few seconds. By the end of a year, each child has spoken English in class for a total of maybe five hours. That's not enough to build fluency.

### 2. Individual feedback on writing

A teacher with 45 essays to mark can give ticks and grades, not detailed feedback. Students don't know exactly what pushed them from a B to a C, or how to get to an A.

### 3. Specific weakness coaching

A child who struggles with articles, or reported speech, or essay structure can't get individual attention in a large class. The teacher has to move on.

These three gaps are what tuition classes exist to fill. Any English class that doesn't actively fix at least one of them isn't really adding anything.

## What a good English tuition class should add

If you're considering tuition, this is the measure. A class is worth it when it adds one or more of these:

### Real speaking time

Small groups where every student speaks every lesson. Not big "spoken English" classes where speaking is a theoretical topic. If your child doesn't come home more comfortable speaking English after three months, the class isn't adding speaking.

### Hand-marked writing

Essays and letters returned with corrections, specific comments, and suggestions. Not a tick and a grade. If the only mark on the page is a tick, writing isn't improving.

### Exam-specific coaching

Past paper drills, marking scheme familiarity, specific question-type weaknesses addressed. Especially important in Grades 10, 11, and A/L.

### Fluency through small groups

Peer learning. Children speak more freely around other children than around adults, and more freely still in groups of eight than in classes of forty.

### Confidence

A child who's shy in school because the class is huge may be the loudest in a small tuition group. That's real progress.

## What a bad English tuition class adds

Be honest with yourself about what you're paying for. Tuition isn't worth it if:

- The class is just school material covered again, in a different room.
- The batch has 30 students (same as school).
- Written work is returned with ticks only.
- Your child says nothing in class.
- The teacher reads from a book for an hour.

In this case, you're paying for a second school day. Your child would do better with more sleep.

## When tuition genuinely helps

Some clear scenarios where tuition adds real value:

### For speaking

A child reads English fluently but freezes when asked to speak. School can't fix this. A small-group spoken English class can.

### For exam prep

Grades 10 and 11 students facing O/L, and A/L students in their final year. Past-paper drilling and marking-scheme familiarity are where tuition pays off most clearly.

### For foundations if school was weak

Children who had weak primary-school English — maybe the teacher was off sick a lot, or the school's English programme is poor — benefit hugely from a solid tuition class to catch up.

### For specific tests

IELTS, TOEFL, specific overseas or scholarship tests. School doesn't teach these. Tuition does.

### For working adults

School English is long behind you. A class is often the quickest way back to usable English.

## When tuition probably isn't needed

A few honest scenarios:

- Your child reads English books for fun. (A reader at home will often outpace a student in class.)
- Your child speaks English at home with at least one parent or sibling.
- Your child is in Grades 1–3 and school English is going fine. More English isn't automatically better — childhood matters.
- You can't actually afford it. A stressed family is worse than a slightly weaker English grade. There are free alternatives — library books, YouTube, English cartoons, conversation practice at home.

## How to pick, if you decide you want tuition

Four questions to ask before you register:

- **Will my child actually speak in class?** Ask the question literally. Get a real answer.
- **How is written work marked?** Ticks only = don't bother. Corrections and comments = good.
- **What's the batch size?** Under 15 is good. Over 25 is just school again.
- **Can I observe one class?** Any good class will let you watch.

## How to get the most out of tuition if you commit

- **Keep the slot sacred.** Tuition skipped half the time is tuition wasted. Pick a day and time you can keep.
- **Follow up at home.** Ten minutes of English at home each day multiplies the class effect.
- **Talk to the teacher.** Once a term. Ask what your child is weakest at. Fix at home what's not being covered in class.
- **Don't double up classes.** Two English tuitions in a week is usually one too many. One strong class plus home practice beats two weak classes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is private English tuition necessary in Sri Lanka?**

No — not for every child. It's genuinely useful when school is leaving gaps (speaking, marked writing, exam-specific coaching) and it's not useful when it just doubles up on what school already covers.

**When should I start English tuition for my child?**

Usually from around Grade 3 or 4, if your child needs it. Earlier can work if the class is play-based. Starting tuition before age 5 is rarely necessary.

**How many classes a week does my child need?**

One well-chosen class a week, plus some English at home, is enough for most students. Twice-weekly is appropriate for exam-year students or children catching up on a weak foundation.

**Can tuition replace school English?**

No. Tuition supplements school English. The school's classroom time, syllabus coverage, and peer group all matter. A good tuition class adds the parts the school can't deliver with 40 students in a room.
