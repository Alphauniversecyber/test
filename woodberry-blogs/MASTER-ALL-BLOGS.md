# Best English Classes in Matara: A 2026 Parent's Guide

Ask ten Matara parents where to send a child for English class and you'll get ten answers. Some will swear by a Colombo-branded institute, some by a teacher down the lane, and a few will tell you they've just given up trying to pick. If you're one of those parents — or an adult who's finally decided to fix their English after years of putting it off — this guide is for you.

We'll go through what to actually look at, which questions sort the good classes from the rest, and how to match a class to your real goal. No lists of "top 10 institutes." Those change every month, and half of them are paid placements anyway.

## Start with the goal, not the brand

Before you pick a class, write down one sentence about what you want. It sounds obvious, but most parents skip it. "I want my child to be better at English" isn't a goal. "I want my daughter to pass O/L English with a credit pass" is. So is "I want to have a work conversation in English without freezing."

The big three goals we see in Matara look like this:

- **Exam results.** Grade 5 Scholarship, O/L English, A/L General English, A/L English main.
- **Spoken confidence.** The child can read fine but won't open their mouth, or the adult knows grammar but can't order food in English without nerves.
- **A specific test.** IELTS for overseas study or migration, sometimes TOEFL.

A class built for one of these isn't automatically good for another. An O/L class and an IELTS class have almost nothing in common beyond the name "English."

## What to look for in a Matara English class

Once the goal is clear, these are the things worth checking before you pay for a term.

### 1. Who actually teaches the class

Not who runs the institute. Who stands in front of the children on Wednesday evening. Ask by name. In most Matara institutes, one or two senior teachers draw the posters and a rotating set of newer teachers handle the actual batches. That's not bad — new teachers can be great — but you want to know who you're getting.

### 2. Batch size

Twenty students in a spoken English class is not a spoken English class. It's a reading class with occasional talking. For speaking goals, look for groups of eight to twelve. For written exam prep, up to twenty is fine if the teacher gives marked feedback on written work.

### 3. What happens in a normal class

Not what the brochure says. What actually happens. Ask a parent whose child has been going for six months: "What did she do in class last week?" If the answer is "she copied notes off the board," that class is a textbook with chairs.

### 4. Progress reports

A real class gives you something — a marked paper, a short report, a WhatsApp update — at least once a term. If the only sign that your child is learning is a monthly fee receipt, that's a problem.

### 5. Timetable sanity

Matara traffic in the evenings is worse than people admit. A class that ends at 7:30pm in Nupe is a class your child will be late for three weeks out of four. Pick a timetable you can actually keep.

## Group class vs private tuition vs online

### Group class

Still the default in Matara and usually the best value. Your child learns alongside others at similar levels, picks up language from peers, and the fee per hour stays reasonable. Weak if the group is too big or too mixed.

### Private tuition

Useful if your child is far ahead or far behind the group, or if you're prepping for a specific exam on a short runway. More expensive and, honestly, often less effective for speaking — because speaking needs other speakers.

### Online classes

Have grown fast since 2020, and they work well for adults, A/L students, and IELTS. For primary-age children, online classes can work if the teacher is good and the child sits with a parent nearby. Otherwise the child drifts.

## Red flags to walk away from

A few things that should make you keep looking, even if the fee is attractive.

- Teachers promise a specific exam grade or IELTS band. No honest teacher does this.
- You can't visit a class before registering. A good institute will let you sit at the back of one class.
- The institute shares no information about teachers' qualifications or experience.
- You're pushed to pay for a full year upfront.
- The classroom has no books on the shelves and no student work on the walls.

## How much should a class cost in Matara?

Fees vary too much to quote here, and prices change every term. As a rough frame: group classes for school students usually sit well below private tuition per hour; IELTS intensive courses cost more because the batches are smaller and the materials are expensive; online classes from Colombo-based teachers often cost the same as local in-person classes, so the geography doesn't always save you money.

A good rule: if the price is far below what others charge, ask why. Usually the answer is batch size or teacher experience.

## A quick word on exam-focused classes

For O/L and A/L English, pick a class that drills past papers, marks written work by hand, and teaches the marking scheme — not just the syllabus. For Grade 5 Scholarship English, look for a teacher who's taught the paper before and knows which question types show up most. For IELTS, insist on mock tests under real timing. A class that never times its students isn't an exam class.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which is the best English class in Matara?**

There isn't one "best" class — it depends on your goal. For exam support, look for a class that drills past papers and gives marked feedback. For spoken English, look for a small batch where every student speaks every lesson. For IELTS, look for timed mock tests and a teacher who has sat the exam themselves.

**At what age should my child start English classes in Matara?**

Most children benefit from a structured English class from around age 4 or 5, but the class must be play-based at that age, not worksheet-based. Formal exam-focused tuition is usually from Grade 3 onwards.

**Are online English classes as good as in-person classes in Matara?**

For adults and older students, yes — often better, because you save travel time and the teacher pool is wider. For primary-age children, in-person tends to work better unless a parent sits with the child during online classes.

**How do I know if my child is actually improving?**

Ask the class for a short written report each term and look at marked written work. Spoken progress is harder to measure, but after two to three months you should hear your child use new English words at home without being prompted.

# Grade 5 Scholarship English: What Actually Gets Marked

If you're reading this at 10pm with a tired child beside you and a stack of past papers on the table, you're not alone. The Grade 5 Scholarship Examination, run by Sri Lanka's Department of Examinations, puts a lot of weight on an eleven-year-old, and the English section is the one that worries parents the most — usually because they don't know what the examiner is actually looking for.

So here it is, in plain language. What the paper tests, where children lose marks, and what matters more than the things parents usually drill.

## What the English section actually tests

The Scholarship paper is bilingual, and the English component mixes with the general-knowledge and intelligence sections. The English questions test four things, not grammar rules:

- Can the child read a short English passage and understand it?
- Can the child match a picture to the right English word or short sentence?
- Can the child recognise basic vocabulary in context?
- Can the child follow simple instructions written in English?

Notice what's not on that list. Essay writing isn't. Complex tenses aren't. Long grammar explanations aren't. The paper isn't testing whether your child knows the rule — it's testing whether they can use everyday English fast and without panic.

## Where children lose marks (the honest list)

After years of marking practice papers in class, the same handful of mistakes come up again and again.

### Rushing the picture-match questions

A child sees a picture of a boy under a tree and four options. Three options mention a tree, and the child picks the first one that mentions a tree. They never read the whole sentence. One wrong word — *beside* vs *under*, *running* vs *sitting* — and the mark is gone.

### Not knowing the small words

Children learn "elephant" and "beautiful" and "important." They don't know "between," "beside," "above," "nearby," "through." These prepositions and position words appear in almost every scholarship English section. They're boring to learn, so classes skip them. Don't.

### Guessing instead of eliminating

On a four-option question, two options are usually clearly wrong. A child who crosses those out first gets to the right answer much more reliably than a child who scans for the "nice-looking" option.

### Reading the passage once, fast

Children read the short passage as if it were Sinhala or Tamil — eyes flying across the page. Then they try to answer the questions from memory. English comprehension needs a slower first read and a second read looking for the specific detail the question asks for.

## What actually works in preparation

Six months out from the exam is the sweet spot. Three months is tight but doable. Less than that and you're in survival mode, which is fine — just focus on the highest-value things.

### Build a 100-word picture vocabulary

Make a list of 100 everyday English words that come with pictures: *kitchen, umbrella, cupboard, toothbrush, carpet, ladder, basket, broom, bucket, slipper*. Household objects. Fruits. School things. Body parts. Weather. Positions. Your child needs to recognise each one in half a second. These show up in the paper over and over.

### Learn question words cold

*What, where, when, who, why, how, which, whose.* Children who confuse *who* and *where* lose easy marks. Drill until it's automatic.

### Do past papers under timing

Download past papers from the Department of Examinations site. Set a timer. Stop when the time's up, even if the child isn't finished. The scholarship is as much about speed as about accuracy. Doing papers untimed just builds false confidence.

### Mark together

After each practice paper, sit with your child and go through the wrong answers. Don't say "this is wrong." Ask "why did you pick this one?" Nine times out of ten, the answer tells you exactly what to teach next.

### Read a short English story every night

Not a textbook. A storybook. Short, illustrated, under ten minutes. Over six months this builds reading fluency more than any worksheet will.

## What doesn't work (but parents still do)

- **Memorising essay templates.** The English section doesn't ask for essays.
- **Buying ten different practice-paper books.** One or two good books plus the official past papers is enough.
- **Teaching tenses with grammar tables.** An eleven-year-old doesn't need the perfect progressive tense. They need to recognise "She is eating" in a picture.
- **Hours of English every day.** Thirty focused minutes beats two tired hours.

## A simple six-month plan

**Months 1–2:** Build vocabulary. One new word-set every three days. Short English reading each night.

**Months 3–4:** Add one past paper per week. Mark it the same day. Go through every wrong answer.

**Month 5:** Two past papers per week, under full timing. Focus on the question types your child loses marks on most.

**Month 6:** Timed papers only. Stop teaching new content. Build calmness and exam habits — bathroom before the paper, pencil box ready, deep breath.

## Keeping your child okay through it

The scholarship is stressful. Children feel the pressure whether we talk about it or not. Two small things help more than any extra past paper:

- Keep class days and rest days clear. A child who studies every single evening for six months arrives at the exam exhausted.
- Don't talk about scholarship school admissions until after the paper. That conversation can wait.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many marks is the English section of the Grade 5 Scholarship worth?**

The English questions appear within the general paper rather than as a separate paper, and the exact weighting can shift year to year. Check the latest paper on doenets.lk to see the current structure before you plan around specific mark counts.

**When should my child start preparing for the Grade 5 Scholarship English?**

Six months before the exam is comfortable. Starting in Grade 4 gives you room without rushing. Starting less than three months before is possible but focus only on past papers and high-frequency vocabulary.

**Where can I find Grade 5 Scholarship past papers?**

The Department of Examinations site (doenets.lk) is the official source. Avoid the scraped PDF sites — many have wrong answers in their marking schemes.

**My child reads English well but freezes on the paper. What do I do?**

Freezing is almost always a timing issue, not a knowledge issue. Practise short timed sections — ten questions in eight minutes — until the clock stops feeling scary.

# O/L English Paper: 9 Mistakes That Lose Marks (and Fixes)

Most students who fail to get a Distinction in O/L English don't fail because they don't know English. They fail because they repeat the same handful of avoidable mistakes under exam pressure. We mark hundreds of practice papers a year, and the list below is what we actually see — not generic advice copied from somewhere else.

If you can fix even six of these before your next practice paper, your mark goes up.

## 1. Writing the letter without the address

Paper II opens with a letter, and the first thing a tired student skips is the sender's address. That's two to three marks gone before the first sentence. Both formal and informal letters need the address and the date in the right place. Learn the format once and write it the same way every time — don't make up a new layout in the exam hall.

**Fix:** Decide on one layout you'll always use for formal letters, and one for informal, and drill them until you can write both from memory in under a minute.

## 2. Treating comprehension like a translation exercise

Students read the comprehension passage and try to translate every word into Sinhala or Tamil in their head. Then they run out of time. Comprehension doesn't test whether you understand every word. It tests whether you can find the specific information a question asks for.

**Fix:** Read the questions first, then go back to the passage looking for the answer. Underline the line where you find each answer. If a question asks "why," the answer is usually near the word *because* or the words *so that*.

## 3. One-word answers when a sentence is asked for

A question that says "Why did the writer go to Colombo?" needs a sentence. Writing "Doctor's appointment" loses half the mark. The marking scheme rewards full sentences on these questions more often than students expect.

**Fix:** If the question has the word *why*, start your answer with *Because*. If it has *how*, start with *By*. If it has *what*, start by repeating the noun from the question.

## 4. Writing dialogues as narration

Paper II often has a dialogue-completion question. Students write things like "He said that he was going home." That's narration. The question asks for the actual words of the dialogue — "I'm going home."

**Fix:** In dialogue questions, write exactly what each person says, in quotation marks, without "He said that..." Keep it conversational.

## 5. Ignoring the word count

The essay or short composition tells you a word range — usually 100 to 150 words for short pieces, more for the long one. Students either write 60 words (and lose structure marks) or 250 words (and lose relevance marks because they've padded). Both cost you.

**Fix:** Count the lines in your normal handwriting. Most students write 8 to 10 words per line. Use that to hit the range without actually counting words.

## 6. No paragraphs

A full essay written as one giant paragraph tells the examiner you don't know how to organise ideas. Even with good sentences, the mark drops.

**Fix:** Every essay gets at least three paragraphs — introduction, body, conclusion. The body usually splits into two or three. Leave a blank line between paragraphs so the examiner can see it at a glance.

## 7. Spelling mistakes on words you knew in Grade 7

*Because, friend, business, Wednesday, receive, government, their vs there.* These are the words that come back wrong on almost every paper. Not difficult vocabulary — easy words rushed.

**Fix:** Keep a personal "red list" of ten words you keep getting wrong. Write each one correctly five times before every practice paper. Your list will shrink.

## 8. Not planning the essay

Students read the essay prompt, pick up the pen, and write the first sentence that comes to mind. Three paragraphs in, they realise they're off-topic or they've run out of ideas.

**Fix:** Spend two minutes planning. Write four to five bullet points in the margin of the answer booklet. Cross them out as you cover each one. Those two minutes save you ten minutes of panic.

## 9. Blank at the literature section

If your syllabus includes the literature texts, students lose easy marks on the contextual questions — the ones where you have to name the author, the character speaking, or the situation in the text. These marks are free if you know the basic story of each poem, prose piece and play.

**Fix:** Make a one-page summary for each text — title, author, main characters, one-sentence plot, one key theme. Read the summary three times a week. You don't need to re-read the full texts — you need to recognise them under pressure.

## How to use the marking scheme (the right way)

The official marking schemes from the Department of Examinations are not a secret. They're published after each exam. Most students look at them once and move on. That's a waste.

Take a past paper, do it under timed conditions, then mark it yourself using the marking scheme. You'll find out two things: where you lose marks you thought you'd get, and where you earn marks you thought you'd lost. Both change how you prepare for the next paper.

Do this for four or five past papers in a row and you'll start thinking like an examiner — which is the single biggest jump a student can make in the last three months.

## A realistic O/L English study week

Not a 4am start, not six hours a day. This works:

- **Monday:** 30 minutes of vocabulary and grammar drill.
- **Wednesday:** 30 minutes of comprehension or dialogue practice.
- **Friday:** 30 minutes of writing — one letter or short composition.
- **Saturday:** One full past paper, timed. Rest of the day off.
- **Sunday:** Mark the paper. Note the mistakes in a book you keep for the whole year.

Two hours a week of focused, marked practice beats fifteen hours of unmarked reading.

## Frequently asked questions

**How is the O/L English paper structured in Sri Lanka?**

The paper is normally split across Paper I and Paper II, covering reading comprehension, vocabulary in context, grammar, letter writing, dialogue, and short and long composition. The literature component appears for students who sit it as a separate subject. Check the current Department of Examinations syllabus for the exact format in your exam year.

**Is O/L English hard to score an A in?**

It's not harder than any other subject, but students lose marks on small mechanical things — spelling, format, word count — more than on their actual English. Fixing those moves you from a credit pass to a distinction without needing better English.

**Should I memorise essays for the O/L English paper?**

No. Memorised essays almost always answer the wrong question, and examiners spot them. Instead, practise writing to a plan — you'll handle any topic that comes up.

**How many past papers should I do before the O/L English exam?**

Six to eight full timed papers, marked using the official marking scheme, is usually enough to see consistent improvement. More than that and you're repeating patterns rather than learning new ones.

# IELTS Band 7 from Sri Lanka: An 8-Week Realistic Plan

Band 7 is where the IELTS exam stops being about English and starts being about exam technique. If you're reading this, you probably speak and write decent English already. You've likely tried a mock test, scored a 6 or 6.5, and realised that the extra half-band isn't going to come from more vocabulary lists.

This is an eight-week plan built for working adults and A/L finishers in Sri Lanka — the two groups we see most in IELTS classes in Matara. It assumes you have about 8–10 hours a week to put in. Less than that and you'll need more weeks; more and you can do this in six.

## Before you start: get an honest baseline

Sit one full practice test. Cambridge IELTS 17 or 18 is what most teachers use. Do it under real conditions — paper, timer, no pausing. Mark it using the official band descriptors, not a rough guess.

If your baseline is below Band 6, eight weeks is tight. You can still try but plan for more revision time. If your baseline is Band 6.5, eight weeks is comfortable. If you're already at Band 7 in two of the four skills, focus the plan on the weaker two.

## The four skills aren't equal

In our Matara batches, this is roughly what we see:

- **Reading and Listening** usually climb the fastest. They're pattern-recognition skills. You get better at them by doing them.
- **Writing** is where most Sri Lankan candidates stall. The issue isn't English — it's structure, task response, and the specific style of academic writing IELTS rewards.
- **Speaking** sounds like it should be the easiest, and for confident speakers it is. But candidates who use Sinhala or Tamil as their everyday language sometimes freeze on Part 2.

Plan your time accordingly. A rough split: 20% reading, 20% listening, 35% writing, 25% speaking.

## Week 1: Diagnose and set up

- Do one full practice test on Day 1. Mark it honestly.
- List which question types you got wrong in Reading and Listening. These become your drilling targets.
- Score your Writing Task 1 and Task 2 using the band descriptors. The four criteria are Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammar. Rank yourself on each.
- Record a 2-minute Speaking Part 2 on your phone. Listen back. Note where you paused, repeated, or lost the thread.

End of Week 1 you should know your real starting point and your two or three biggest gaps.

## Week 2: Reading foundations

- Learn the question types: matching headings, True/False/Not Given, multiple choice, sentence completion, matching features.
- For each type, do 10–15 practice questions this week. Check which type you lose most marks on.
- Learn to skim and scan — read the question first, then hunt for the answer in the passage. Don't read the whole passage carefully.
- Build a timing habit: 20 minutes per passage, strict.

Common Sri Lankan candidate mistake: spending too long on the first passage because you want to be sure. The third passage is always the hardest, and you need the time for it. Move on.

## Week 3: Listening foundations

- Practise with the Cambridge test audio, not YouTube videos. The exam's accents and pace matter.
- Train your ear for distractors — the speaker says one answer then corrects themselves. The second answer is usually the right one.
- Spelling matters in Listening. A right answer spelled wrong is marked wrong. Drill the names of days, months, directions, and common British place names.
- Do Section 4 every day. It's the hardest, so stop avoiding it.

## Week 4: Writing Task 1 (Academic)

This is the week most people underestimate.

- Learn one solid structure: paragraph 1 is the paraphrase, paragraph 2 is the overview (always two sentences), paragraphs 3 and 4 are the detail.
- The overview is where most candidates lose marks. Write it before you write the detail paragraphs.
- Learn 15 synonyms for *increase, decrease, remain stable, fluctuate*. That's enough. More just makes writing slower.
- Write three Task 1 pieces this week, each under 20 minutes. Get at least one marked by a teacher.

**Trap to avoid:** describing every single data point. The task wants trends and comparisons, not a list of numbers.

## Week 5: Writing Task 2

- Four essay types cover almost all questions: opinion, discussion, problem-solution, and two-part. Learn a plan for each.
- Introduction, two body paragraphs, conclusion. Nothing fancy.
- Each body paragraph: one clear idea, one or two supporting sentences, one example. Examples can be from Sri Lanka — the examiner doesn't need UK examples.
- Grammar range matters for Band 7. If you only ever write simple sentences, you cap at 6.5. Mix in a conditional, a relative clause, and a passive construction.

Write three Task 2 essays this week, under 40 minutes each. Mark them against the descriptors.

## Week 6: Speaking

- Record every practice. Listen. You'll hear your own filler words — *actually, basically, you know* — faster than any teacher will.
- Part 1: don't over-prepare. Short, direct, two-sentence answers.
- Part 2: speak for the full two minutes. Silence kills your score. If you run out of ideas, repeat yourself with different words — the examiner would rather hear you keep going than stop.
- Part 3: longer, more abstract answers. Use phrases like *on the one hand, that said, having said that, one example would be.*

Daily practice this week: pick one Part 2 cue card, speak for two minutes, record, listen, do it again. Fifteen minutes.

## Week 7: Full mock tests

- Two full tests this week, back to back is fine on a weekend.
- Mark them the same day. Notice which question types are still weak.
- For Writing and Speaking, get these marked by a teacher if at all possible. Self-marking at this stage underestimates errors.

## Week 8: Taper and test prep

- One final mock test on Day 1. Rest between the sections like the real exam.
- Reduce new practice. Review mistakes from Weeks 2–7.
- Three days before the test: no new material. Re-read your notes. Sleep normally.
- Day before: light review only. Pack your ID, pencils, water bottle. Go to bed early.

## What to skip

- YouTube "band 9 secret" videos. Most have no secret.
- Buying ten different IELTS books. Three Cambridge test books plus one writing guide is enough.
- Memorising full essays. Examiners flag them.
- Flashcards for obscure vocabulary. Common academic words used correctly beat rare words used oddly.

## Sri Lankan context

Two things worth knowing if you're testing here:

- Test centres are mainly in Colombo, with occasional dates at other locations. Book early — slots fill fast around university application cycles.
- Both IDP and the British Council run tests. The test is the same — pick by location and date.

Confirm current fees, dates, and centres on the IDP or British Council Sri Lanka site before you book. Pricing changes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I reach IELTS Band 7 in 8 weeks?**

If your starting point is Band 6 to 6.5 and you can study 8–10 hours a week, yes — this is realistic. From Band 5.5 or lower, give yourself 12 to 16 weeks.

**Is IELTS Academic or General Training easier?**

The speaking and listening sections are identical. General Training reading is slightly easier than Academic, and General Training writing has a letter instead of a data task. Pick based on what your university or migration route requires, not difficulty.

**How often can I retake IELTS in Sri Lanka?**

There's no cooling-off period — you can book again as soon as the next slot opens. That said, a retake without a plan usually gets a similar score. Fix the gap first.

**Does Writing Task 2 count more than Task 1?**

Yes. Task 2 is worth roughly twice as much as Task 1 in the writing band calculation. If you're short on time, prioritise Task 2 in practice.

# Choosing an English Tuition Class: 10 Questions Parents Should Ask

Most parents pick an English class the way they pick a restaurant — a neighbour recommended it, so they try it. Sometimes it works. Often it doesn't, and six months later the child's English hasn't moved and the term fee is gone.

Here are ten questions you can ask before you register. None of them are rude. A good class will answer all of them without hesitation. If a class refuses to answer even three, keep looking.

## 1. Who will teach my child?

Not "who owns the institute." Who stands in front of the batch. Get the name. Ask what classes they've taught before and how long they've been teaching this level. A good teacher is happy to tell you. An institute that dodges this is hiding something.

## 2. How many students are in a batch?

You want a number, not "small groups." For a speaking-focused class, 8 to 12 is the comfortable range. For exam prep, up to 20 is fine if written work is marked. Over 25 and the class is really a lecture.

## 3. Can I sit in on a class before I register?

Any serious institute will let you observe one class — sometimes from the back of the room, sometimes a sample session. If a class refuses, there's usually a reason, and it's usually not a good one.

## 4. What does a typical lesson look like?

Ask for a walk-through of one actual lesson, not the brochure version. "We open with a 10-minute vocabulary review, then one past paper comprehension passage, then 15 minutes of pair speaking, then a written homework check" is a specific answer. "We cover the syllabus" is not.

## 5. How is written work marked?

For any exam-prep class, hand-marked written work is non-negotiable. The marks should come back with corrections and short comments, not a tick and a grade. If the class uses only self-marking or peer-marking, your child isn't getting real feedback on their writing.

## 6. How will I know my child is improving?

Good answer: a written progress note at the end of each term, plus marked past papers you can see. Bad answer: "You can ask the teacher any time." You won't, and both of you know it.

## 7. What's the timetable, and how often does it change?

Classes that shift time every month are harder on families than they look. Ask for the full term's timetable in writing. Also check start and end times against your real evening — Matara traffic, school pickup, dinner.

## 8. What happens if my child misses a class?

Good classes offer either a makeup class, a recording, or notes with the week's work. If the answer is "they just miss it," you're paying for classes that disappear.

## 9. What materials are included in the fee?

Ask if books, past paper packs, and mock tests are inside the term fee or charged separately. This matters especially for IELTS and A/L classes, where materials can add 20–30% to the real cost.

## 10. Who are last year's results?

For exam-focused classes, ask about recent student results — not individual student names, but overall patterns. "Most of our O/L students passed with Credit" is a fair answer. "100% distinctions" is almost never true.

## The three warning signs

If you hear any of these, walk away.

- **Guaranteed grades.** No honest teacher promises a specific O/L grade or IELTS band. Learning depends on the student too.
- **Full-year fee upfront with no refund terms.** A term at a time is reasonable. Twelve months with no exit is a trap.
- **No teachers' qualifications shared.** You don't need degrees in English — some excellent teachers don't have them — but the institute should be willing to tell you about them.

## The three green flags

- The office staff know your child's name by the second visit.
- The teacher knows the Department of Examinations marking scheme by heart.
- A former student recommends the class without being asked.

## Big-brand vs local vs online

### Big brand (British Council, Royal Institute, major Colombo names)

Strong curriculum, good materials, higher fees. For IELTS especially, the brand is useful because the materials are current. Downside: large batch sizes, less personal attention, often a commute.

### Local institute

The real variable. The good ones beat big brands easily because the teacher-to-student ratio is better and the owner is in the building. The weak ones have one tired teacher doing six batches. The questions above sort the two.

### Online

Convenient for adults and older students. Weak for primary-age children unless a parent sits in. Good for exam prep if the class uses live writing feedback and not just recorded videos.

## When to switch classes

A few signs it's time to move:

- Six months in and your child can't name one thing they learned last week.
- The teacher changes four times a year.
- Written work comes back with only a tick, no corrections.
- Your child dreads the class — not in a "homework is hard" way, but a "nothing's happening there" way.

Switching mid-year isn't ideal, but staying in a class that isn't working is worse.

**How much should I expect to pay for English tuition in Sri Lanka?**

Fees vary widely by city, level, and class size. Group classes for school students usually sit in a lower bracket per hour than private tuition; IELTS and A/L main English cost more because of smaller batches and materials. If a class is dramatically cheaper than others nearby, ask why — usually it's batch size.

**Is private tuition always better than group class?**

No. Group classes work better for speaking because speaking needs other speakers. Private tuition works better when a child is far ahead or far behind the group, or for a specific exam on a short runway.

**Should I pick the nearest English class or the best one?**

Within reason, the nearest one, if the quality is close. Traffic and tiredness kill classes that start too late at night. A slightly weaker class your child attends every week beats a slightly better class they skip twice a month.

**Can I move my child mid-term if the class isn't working?**

Yes. Most institutes won't refund the full term fee, but a wasted term is cheaper than a wasted year. If the warning signs are clear, move.

# How to Help Your Child Speak English Confidently at Home

Your child reads English fine. They get decent marks. But when a relative visits and asks a question in English, they freeze, look at the floor, and answer in Sinhala. Sound familiar?

This is the most common thing parents ask us about, and the good news is you can fix it at home without buying anything. The bad news is you have to be a little patient and a little willing to look silly.

## Why your child isn't speaking

Four reasons cover most children.

- **No one at home speaks English.** The child has nowhere to practise outside class.
- **The grown-ups correct every mistake.** The child learns that speaking English means being embarrassed.
- **The classroom is reading and writing only.** The child has words in their head but has never said them aloud.
- **They're shy, full stop.** Some children would rather not speak any language in front of people. English isn't the issue — confidence is.

Most children have a mix of all four. The habits below help with all of them.

## Rule one: stop correcting mistakes in the middle of the sentence

If your child says "Yesterday I go to shop," do not jump in with "went, not go." The child will stop talking. What happens next:

- You listen to the whole sentence.
- You reply naturally, using the right form: "Oh, you went to the shop? What did you buy?"
- You've modelled the correct version. The child's brain picks it up slowly.

This feels wrong because we were all corrected mid-sentence as children. But correction kills confidence, and children stop trying. Model the right form in your reply instead.

## Build a 15-minute English window

One short window a day beats a vague "speak more English" rule that no one actually follows.

- Pick a time: car ride to school, dinner, bath time.
- During that window, only English. Short sentences are fine. Mistakes are fine.
- Outside the window, go back to normal.

Fifteen minutes a day, consistent for three months, moves a child's spoken English more than any class alone will.

## Give them a job that needs English

Children speak when they have something to say. Try one of these:

- **The weather report.** Every morning, the child tells you the weather in one sentence. "It's cloudy today." Thirty seconds, every day.
- **The grocery list reader.** You dictate the list in English, the child writes it. Then they read it back while shopping.
- **The storyteller.** At bedtime, the child tells a short story — real or made-up — in English. Three minutes, no more.
- **The restaurant orderer.** When you eat out, the child orders in English. They'll fight you on this at first. It passes.

These aren't exercises. They're small responsibilities in English.

## Read aloud together, don't read alone

Reading alone builds vocabulary. Reading aloud together builds speaking. Pick a short book at your child's level. You read one line, they read the next. For younger children, they can just repeat after you.

Ten minutes of this, three nights a week, is enough.

## Audio in the background

Children pick up pronunciation from listening. Put English on in the car, at breakfast, in the background of play time.

- Children's audiobooks or stories (free on many apps).
- English cartoons they already like, in English with English subtitles if your child reads.
- Podcasts for children if they're a bit older.

Avoid dubbed content. The point is to hear real English rhythm.

## Deal with the "I don't want to" phase

Every child goes through a phase where they refuse to speak English, usually around ages 7–9. This is normal. What works:

- Don't make it a punishment. The moment English feels like homework, they'll resist harder.
- Make it situational. English during one specific activity, not all day.
- Praise effort, not correctness. "You tried that sentence — good" beats "that's wrong."
- Let them see you speaking imperfect English with someone. Children learn from watching adults make mistakes and keep going.

## What about screen time?

Used well, screens help. Used badly, they do nothing.

- Good: an English cartoon your child watches once and then retells the story to you.
- Bad: forty-five minutes of YouTube Shorts in English. No sentences, no context, no speaking afterwards.

A rough test: if your child can't tell you something from the video they just watched, the video didn't do anything.

## The shy child

If your child is simply shy — not just in English — forcing is a disaster. Instead:

- Let them speak to one trusted person first. A grandparent, a cousin, a familiar teacher.
- Build to small groups slowly.
- Spoken English classes with small batches help enormously because they lower the stakes.

Most shy children open up when the setting feels safe. Rushing this backfires every time.

## Fitting this around school

School and tuition already fill the day. The good news: none of this needs more than 15–20 focused minutes.

A realistic week:

- **Monday–Friday:** 15-minute English window, one job (weather or list).
- **Three nights:** 10 minutes reading aloud together.
- **Background audio:** car rides, breakfasts, whatever fits.

Total: under 2 hours a week of parent effort. Over six months, it rewires how your child speaks English.

## When home isn't enough

If your child is older than 10 and still won't speak English, home alone won't fix it. They need a classroom setting with other children at similar levels, where speaking is the point of the lesson rather than something that happens at the end.

That's where a dedicated spoken English class helps — not to replace what you do at home, but to add peers.

## Frequently asked questions

**At what age should my child start speaking English?**

As early as you like, as long as it's gentle. Ages 4–6 is a natural window because children have no fear of mistakes yet. By age 9 or 10, shyness kicks in and you need more patience.

**My child's English teacher says they're fine. Why can't they speak?**

School English usually tests reading, writing, and grammar — not speaking. A child can get a Credit pass in O/L English and still not speak comfortably. The two are different skills.

**Does it matter if I don't speak perfect English myself?**

No. Children don't need a perfect model — they need a confident one. Your willingness to try matters more than your accent.

**Should I only speak English at home?**

Not if your family language isn't English. All-English households for a Sinhala-speaking or Tamil-speaking family usually create confused children and awkward dinners. A short daily window works better.

# O/L English Literature: How to Write Essays That Score

English Literature at O/L level feels unfair to a lot of students. You can know the texts well, you can love the stories, and still get a mediocre mark on the essay. Meanwhile someone who barely finished the novel writes a better essay and scores higher. The difference is almost never knowledge. It's structure.

Here's how to write essays that score — written for Sri Lankan students sitting the GCE O/L Literature paper, with the quirks of the local marking scheme in mind.

## What the examiner actually wants

Every Literature essay is checking three things:

- Do you understand the text?
- Can you answer the question that was asked, not the question you wish was asked?
- Can you back up what you say with evidence from the text?

Students lose marks because they drift — they write everything they know about the text instead of answering the specific question. The marking scheme rewards relevance before depth.

## Plan before you write (two minutes, non-negotiable)

Before your pen touches the lined pages of the answer booklet:

- Underline the key words in the question. If the question says *How does the poet present loneliness*, your essay is about loneliness and about how the poet shows it. Not loneliness in general.
- Jot three main points in the margin. Each will become a body paragraph.
- Next to each point, note one short textual reference — a line, an image, a scene.
- Decide your answer to the question in one sentence. That sentence is your thesis.

Two minutes of planning saves ten minutes of wandering mid-essay.

## The PEEL structure (and when to break it)

PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link — is the backbone of a Literature paragraph.

- **Point:** the one argument this paragraph makes. One clear sentence.
- **Evidence:** a short quote or specific reference from the text. Quote exactly if you can; paraphrase if you can't.
- **Explanation:** what the evidence shows about your point. This is where the marks live.
- **Link:** a sentence tying the paragraph back to the question.

You can break PEEL for style, but at O/L level, stick with it until you're confident. The examiner recognises the structure and marks it faster.

### Example paragraph

> The poet shows the speaker's loneliness most clearly through imagery of empty space. In the second stanza, she describes the room as "a shell with no sea inside," comparing the familiar house to something hollow. The image is unsettling because a shell should hold the memory of water, but this one doesn't — the speaker feels that her life has lost its content, not just her companions. This use of metaphor lets the reader feel the loneliness without being told about it directly, which deepens the sense of isolation the poem explores.

Point, evidence, explanation, link. Under 80 words. Three to four of these makes a strong essay.

## How to use quotes

Short quotes beat long ones. One word pulled from a line, used well, scores higher than three lines quoted and barely discussed.

- Put quotes in quotation marks and keep them short.
- Integrate them into your sentence rather than dropping them in separately.
- If you can't remember the exact wording, paraphrase and say what the moment shows.

**Weak:** The writer says "Then she sat down on the chair and thought about her life and all the things she had never done."

**Strong:** The writer lingers on the moment she "sat down" to think — the stillness marks a turning point in how she sees herself.

## Common mistakes in O/L Literature essays

### Retelling the story

Three paragraphs of plot summary isn't an essay. The examiner has read the book. Your job is to answer the question, not to prove you also read it.

### Vague agreement words

"This is a very interesting poem" or "The writer uses many techniques" tells the examiner nothing. Cut these sentences. They don't earn marks; they eat time.

### Paragraphs with one sentence

A single-sentence paragraph is almost always an incomplete thought. If you only have one sentence, you haven't finished explaining — go back and add.

### Treating every text the same way

A poem isn't a short story. A play isn't a novel. The techniques you talk about should match the form. For poems: imagery, rhythm, structure, sound. For prose: character development, setting, narrative voice. For drama: dialogue, stage directions, dramatic irony.

## A simple essay template for the exam

Introduction (2–3 sentences)

- One sentence setting the text and author.
- One sentence stating your overall answer to the question (your thesis).
- Optional: one sentence previewing your three points.

Body (3 paragraphs, PEEL each)

- First point — strongest.
- Second point — different angle on the same theme.
- Third point — a nuance or counter-perspective.

Conclusion (2 sentences)

- Restate your answer in different words.
- One final thought — the "so what" of your argument.

Total: around 500–600 words for a full essay response. Enough to score well, not so much that you run out of time.

## How to revise without re-reading everything

Two months before the exam, re-reading full novels isn't the best use of your time. Instead:

- **Make a one-page text summary.** Title, author, main characters, one-sentence plot, three central themes, four or five memorable quotes.
- **Theme-map your texts.** For each major theme (love, loss, power, identity, nature), list the two or three texts that cover it and one key example from each.
- **Do past paper essays.** Two per week. Timed. Marked with the marking scheme.
- **Compare your essays to the marking scheme band descriptors.** Ask: what would push this from a Credit to a Distinction?

## Timed practice is non-negotiable

Literature essays are won and lost on time management. In the exam you have maybe 35–45 minutes per essay. If you can't finish an essay in 40 minutes at home, you certainly won't in the exam hall.

Practise with a timer. Stop when the time's up. Mark anyway. The point is to learn what you can realistically produce under pressure.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should an O/L Literature essay be?**

Around 500–600 words for a full essay response, or about 2.5 to 3 sides of the answer booklet in normal handwriting. Shorter than that usually means you haven't developed your points enough. Much longer and you're probably repeating yourself.

**Can I memorise essays for O/L Literature?**

No. Examiners are very good at spotting memorised essays, and they almost never match the specific question. Memorise textual references and structures instead — then adapt to whatever question appears.

**How many quotes should I use in a Literature essay?**

Three to five short, well-chosen quotes is plenty for a full essay. Each quote should earn its place — if you can't say something specific about it, cut it.

**What's the difference between O/L English Language and O/L English Literature?**

English Language tests general reading, writing, grammar, and composition. English Literature tests your understanding of specific set texts — poems, short stories, plays, novels — chosen by the Department of Examinations. They're different subjects, taught differently.

- **Vocabulary:** 10 new English words a week. Make flashcards with the word on one side and a picture on the other. Keep them.

- **English:** continue the 10 words a week. Add preposition practice — *beside, between, under, over, through*. Picture-matching worksheets help a lot here.

**Goal:** honest assessment of where the child is.

Do one past paper under full timing. Mark it with the official marking scheme. Now look at the result honestly:

Spend the second half of Month 3 fixing the weakest area specifically. Don't try to fix everything at once.

**Goal:** the child can finish full papers within the time limit.

- **Weekly:** one past paper under full timing. Same time of day you'd sit the real paper.
- **Daily:** 15 minutes of mental maths and English vocabulary drill — fast, short bursts.
- **Weekly marking:** sit together, go through every wrong answer. Ask *why* your child picked that option. The answer usually tells you what to teach.

By the end of Month 4, your child should be finishing the paper within the time with a few minutes to check.

**Goal:** turn remaining weak spots into automatic answers.

- Two past papers a week, alternating full and half.
- Focused drill on the two or three question types that still cost marks.
- Light on new content. You're not adding, you're sharpening.

**Goal:** the child walks into the exam hall rested and confident.

- Reduce practice volume in the last two weeks.
- Full timed papers on the weekends only.
- No new topics — if your child doesn't know it by now, it's not coming in the exam.

- Short reading passages with multiple-choice questions

- Simple grammar (is/are, was/were, do/does)

- Essay writing
- Complex tenses
- Long grammar explanations

- **Comparing with other children.** Every parent group has one parent whose child is "already doing papers from last year." Mute the group.

If three months in, your child is losing ground or hating the process:

- Switch to a shorter daily routine with more variety.

# IELTS vs TOEFL in Sri Lanka: Which One Should You Take?

If you're applying overseas from Sri Lanka, someone has probably already told you to take IELTS. Someone else told you TOEFL is harder. A third person said "it doesn't matter, just take one." All three are partly right.

Here's a straight comparison, written for Sri Lankan candidates, with the local practicalities in mind.

## The short version

- **IELTS** is more common in Sri Lanka, has in-person speaking with a real examiner, and is accepted almost everywhere.
- **TOEFL** is more common for US universities, uses a computer-based speaking section, and is accepted in most places too.
- **For most Sri Lankan candidates, IELTS is the easier logistical choice.** TOEFL still wins for some US applications.

If you need a specific answer now: ask your target university or visa route. Pick the test they explicitly accept. If both work, take IELTS.

## Format differences

### IELTS Academic

- **Listening:** 30 minutes, four recorded sections.
- **Reading:** 60 minutes, three passages.
- **Writing:** 60 minutes, a short data task plus a longer essay.
- **Speaking:** 11–14 minutes, face to face with an examiner.
- Total: about 2 hours 45 minutes.

### TOEFL iBT

- **Reading:** 35 minutes.
- **Listening:** 36 minutes.
- **Speaking:** 16 minutes, recorded into a microphone, graded later.
- **Writing:** 29 minutes, two tasks.
- Total: about 2 hours.

Both tests cover the same four skills, but the way they test them is different. TOEFL is fully computer-based and has shorter sections. IELTS can be done on paper or computer depending on the centre, with speaking always done live with a person.

## Speaking: the biggest practical difference

If you're comfortable with people and nervous with microphones, IELTS speaking will feel more natural. You sit across from an examiner, they ask questions, you answer. It's a conversation.

TOEFL speaking is harder for most Sri Lankan candidates. You speak into a microphone against a timer, often with other test takers speaking in the same room. There's no interaction. If you pause too long or go off-topic, no one guides you back.

Candidates who speak well with people but freeze with recording equipment usually do better on IELTS.

## Scoring

**IELTS:** scored on a 9-band scale, with 0.5 increments. Band 7 is competitive. Most universities ask for 6.0 to 7.5 depending on programme.

**TOEFL iBT:** scored out of 120, with 30 per section. 90 is roughly equivalent to IELTS 7. Most universities ask for 80–100.

Score requirements for Sri Lankan universities and overseas universities vary by programme — always check the specific requirement on the course page rather than trusting general numbers.

**Both tests** are accepted by universities in the UK, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Ireland, and most European countries.

**IELTS** has an edge for:

- UK visa and immigration (IELTS for UKVI is the standard route).
- Australian permanent residency.
- UK and Irish universities.
- Most Sri Lankan employers and government scholarships.

**TOEFL** has an edge for:

- Many US universities, especially older ones.
- A smaller number of institutions that only accept TOEFL.

If your target is clear, check the university or visa route's accepted tests page. If your target is vague, IELTS covers more ground.

Both tests cost more than most people expect. IELTS and TOEFL fees are in a similar range, with slight variation by test type and date. Check current fees on IDP Sri Lanka, British Council, or the ETS TOEFL site — prices change.

**Availability in Sri Lanka:**

- IELTS test dates are frequent, both in Colombo and occasionally at other centres.
- TOEFL iBT is also offered in Colombo; test slots are less frequent than IELTS.

If you need to test soon, IELTS almost always has a nearer date.

## Which is easier?

Honestly, neither is "harder." They're different. What matters is which plays to your strengths.

**IELTS tends to feel easier for:**

- Candidates with British-accent exposure (British Council classes, UK TV, UK English textbooks).
- People who prefer face-to-face speaking.
- Candidates who write faster by hand than on a keyboard.

**TOEFL tends to feel easier for:**

- Candidates with American-accent exposure.
- Fast typists comfortable with a keyboard.
- People who prefer no one watching them speak.

Most Sri Lankan students have grown up with British-leaning English through schooling. That tilts things toward IELTS without most candidates realising it.

**TOEFL** has the Official Guide to the TOEFL iBT and the official practice tests from ETS. Fewer high-quality third-party books than IELTS has, but plenty of practice through the ETS website.

Free material exists for both tests. Paid official material is still worth the money because it's closest to the real exam.

For most Sri Lankan candidates:

# Online English Classes in Sri Lanka: Do They Actually Work?

Online English classes exploded in Sri Lanka during 2020 and never fully went back. Some classes that went online then stayed online. Some students who tried them went back to classrooms. And a lot of parents are still unsure whether a Zoom class is a real class or a glorified YouTube lesson.

Here's the honest answer: online English works well for some learners and badly for others. Figuring out which group you're in matters more than any specific platform or institute.

## Who online English works for

### Adults wanting spoken English

Works well. Adults have the self-discipline to show up, the attention span to sit through 60–90 minutes, and the motivation that online classes need. The biggest gain is time — no commute means lessons you'd skip otherwise.

### A/L students and IELTS candidates

Works well, especially for IELTS. The teacher pool is wider online, mock tests are easier to deliver, and recorded speaking practice is actually easier online than in person.

### O/L students (Grades 10–11)

Mixed. Works if the class is small, the camera is on both sides, and written work is still marked. Fails if the class is a recorded video with no interaction.

### Primary school children

Works only if a parent sits nearby. Online classes for Grade 1–5 children without parental supervision tend to turn into expensive YouTube background noise. The child's face stays on screen, but their attention doesn't.

### Pre-school and Grade 1–2

Rarely works. Children this young need physical movement, real books in their hands, and a teacher who can see what they're actually doing. Online classes for 5-year-olds are mostly a convenience for parents, not a learning environment for the child.

## What makes an online class actually work

Not all online classes are the same. Six things separate the good from the weak.

### 1. Cameras on, both sides

If the teacher's camera is off, or students keep their cameras off, it's a video call in name only. The teacher can't see who's confused. Insist on cameras on, politely but firmly.

### 2. Small group sizes

A Zoom class with 40 students is a webinar. A class with 8–12 is a class. Ask the batch size before you pay.

### 3. Live, not recorded

Recorded videos have a place — for review, for catching up on a missed class, for self-study. They are not a substitute for a live class. If the "class" is only pre-recorded videos, you're buying a course, not a class.

### 4. Marked written work

Yes, even online. Students upload written work, teachers mark it, and marks come back with specific corrections. If written work isn't marked, writing doesn't improve.

### 5. Breakout rooms or turn-taking

For speaking classes, every student should speak every lesson. Good online teachers use breakout rooms or a strict turn order to make sure quiet students aren't just sitting in silence for an hour.

### 6. A real timetable

"Whenever the teacher is available" is not a schedule. A real online class has set days and times, like any in-person class.

## Where online beats in-person

- **Student access.** A student in Hambantota can join a class run by a strong teacher in Colombo. Before online classes, this wasn't possible.
- **IELTS preparation.** Mock tests, timed essays, recorded speaking — all work well online.
- **Adult spoken English.** No commute after work. Classes at 7pm instead of 9pm after travel.
- **Teacher quality options.** You're not limited to teachers within driving distance.
- **Recording for review.** Missed a class? Watch it later. A rare but real advantage.

## Where in-person still wins

- **Young children.** Grades 1–5 learn better with a teacher in the room.
- **Shy students.** Speaking up on camera is harder than speaking up in a room. For truly shy learners, in-person is gentler.
- **Students who need routine.** Some students study better when they physically leave the house to go to class.
- **Group energy.** A good classroom has a pulse to it that Zoom doesn't quite match.

## Common online class problems and how to spot them

Before you register, ask these questions:

- **"How many students are in the live class?"** Anything over 20 is too many.
- **"Can my child use a tablet, or do they need a laptop?"** Tablets are fine for younger children with parental help; older students need a keyboard for writing practice.
- **"How is homework submitted and marked?"** A real class has a clear answer: WhatsApp, email, Google Classroom. Vague answers mean marking doesn't happen.
- **"Do you record classes? Can I get the recording if my child misses one?"** A real institute does this. Random individual teachers often don't.

## A note on the technology

You don't need expensive equipment for online English classes to work.

- A stable internet connection (even 2 Mbps is usually fine for Zoom audio).
- A device with a camera. Laptop or tablet, not just a phone if possible.
- Headphones with a microphone. Cheap earphones with a mic cost under Rs. 1,000 and massively improve audio quality for the teacher.
- A quiet corner, not the middle of the living room during tea time.

## How to try online without committing

- Book a single trial class at an institute that offers one.
- Test the technology in advance — join the meeting link ten minutes before class.
- If the class works well for one month, extend. If it's not working, don't drag it out hoping it'll improve.

Online classes tend to reveal themselves fast. Within three or four sessions, you'll know whether it's working.

Written work is marked and returned through WhatsApp or email. **Are online English classes cheaper than in-person classes in Sri Lanka?**

Not always. Teacher time costs the same. Some online classes are cheaper because the batches are larger. If an online class is dramatically cheaper than an in-person class with the same teacher, check the batch size first.

**What age is too young for online English classes?**

Below Grade 3, online classes work only if a parent sits with the child. Below Grade 1, in-person is almost always better.

**Can online IELTS classes get you Band 7?**

Yes, if the class is live, small, and marks your writing and speaking. Recorded-only IELTS courses rarely get past Band 6.5 for most candidates.

A phone works for watching, but writing practice really needs a laptop or at least a tablet with a keyboard. For reading-heavy classes, a laptop is noticeably better.

# Why Your Child's English Isn't Improving (Honest Reasons)

You've been paying for an English class for a year. Maybe two. And your child's English looks pretty much the same as it did when they started. They still freeze when relatives visit. Their marks don't shift. They still say "I'm going to home" and "She don't know."

You're not imagining it, and it's not always the child's fault. Here are the six reasons we see most often, and what actually fixes each one.

## 1. The class is a reading class that calls itself a speaking class

Many "spoken English" classes in Sri Lanka are really reading and worksheet classes with a few group conversations thrown in. The child spends 90% of the lesson quiet, reading from the board or filling in blanks.

**What fixes it:** a class where every student speaks in every lesson. Small group size (8–12). Teacher calls on students by name. Real pair or small-group speaking time, not just "talk amongst yourselves."

Before you register, ask: "How often does my child actually speak in one class?" If the honest answer is "sometimes," keep looking.

## 2. No English outside the class

Two hours a week of English class can't overcome 70 hours a week of no English at all. Many children only hear English during their class and at school. That's not enough to build fluency.

**What fixes it:**

- A 15-minute English window at home each day.
- English audiobooks or cartoons in the background.
- Reading English storybooks at the child's level, aloud, with a parent.

None of this needs special materials. See our [guide to building English at home](https://woodberryplace.com/blog/help-child-speak-english-at-home/) for specifics.

## 3. Mistakes get punished, so the child stops trying

If every wrong word gets corrected, every sentence gets rewritten, every attempt gets a sigh — the child learns that English is unsafe. So they stop speaking, and then they stop improving.

**What fixes it:** adults in the child's life — parents, teachers, older siblings — listen to the whole sentence first, reply using the correct form, and don't jump in mid-word. Progress comes from attempts, not from perfection.

If your child's class corrects every sentence as it happens, consider whether the fear of mistakes is slowing them down more than the mistakes are.

## 4. The teacher is teaching the wrong level

A child in a class two levels above their English is lost. A child in a class two levels below is bored. Either way they stop improving.

**What fixes it:** ask the class to assess your child's current level — not just slot them into a group by age or grade. Good institutes do a short level check before placement. If a class doesn't, your child might be in the wrong room.

Signs your child is in the wrong level:

- They can't tell you what they did in the last lesson.
- They dread going.
- Their marks on class work are either 95%+ (too easy) or 40% or less (too hard).

## 5. The class drills grammar but never uses it

A child can list every tense and still not be able to say "I went to Galle last week." Grammar on paper is not grammar in the mouth.

**What fixes it:** classes that use the grammar in conversation and writing, not just fill-in-the-blank exercises. Ask what happens after a grammar lesson. If the answer is "a worksheet," that grammar isn't transferring.

## 6. Shyness is the real issue, not English

Some children are not struggling with English. They're struggling with speaking in front of people, in any language. English just happens to be the one that gets noticed because of the class.

**What fixes it:** patience, not pressure. Shy children open up in the right setting — one safe person, then a small group, then a class. Don't force them to perform. Don't single them out in front of relatives.

If shyness is the real issue, a good small-group class actually helps more than one-on-one, because the child gets to blend in and build confidence without being the centre of attention.

## What to do this week

If any of these sound familiar:

- **Watch one full lesson** of your child's class, if you're allowed to. You'll see fast whether real learning is happening.
- **Set up a 15-minute English window** at home. Pick a time, start today.
- **Read aloud together** three nights this week. Any short English book.
- **Stop correcting mid-sentence.** Just for a week. See what happens.

Give it four weeks. If nothing moves, think seriously about changing the class.

## When it's not the class

Sometimes the class is fine and the issue is elsewhere.

- A difficult school year or friendship problem can stall learning in every subject at once.
- A new sibling, a family move, or health issues eat attention.
- Sometimes a child is quietly decided: "I'm not good at English." Fixing that belief matters more than fixing the grammar.

If your child has gone through something hard in the last six months, give the English more time. It isn't broken — it's just not the priority right now.

## When switching classes is the right move

Four signs it's time to switch:

- Six months in, your child can't name one thing they learned last week.
- Written work comes back with only ticks, no corrections.
- The teacher changes every term.
- The child dreads it and can't say why.

Switching isn't disloyal. A class that isn't working doesn't become a class that works just because you've already paid.

**How long before I should expect to see my child's English improve?**

Real improvement usually shows at around 3–4 months of consistent class plus some English at home. If six months in you see nothing, something isn't working.

**Could it be that my child just isn't good at languages?**

Almost never. Children who grow up with Sinhala or Tamil are already managing one language; English is just a second one. If your child isn't improving, the method is usually the issue, not the ability.

Yes. School English in Sri Lanka tests reading, writing, and grammar — not speaking. A child can get Credit passes without ever saying a full English sentence out loud. The two skills need separate work.

Not purely for the language. English-medium schools help English, but they also change your child's whole school experience. A good spoken English class gives most of the language benefit without the upheaval.

# IELTS Writing Task 2: Band 7 Structure for Sri Lankan Candidates

Writing Task 2 is where most Sri Lankan IELTS candidates lose their band. Reading and Listening usually come in at 7+ for people with decent English; Speaking is manageable with practice. Writing stalls at 6 or 6.5 because the examiner is grading a specific skill — academic essay writing — not general English.

Here's the structure, the band criteria, and the specific mistakes we see from candidates every intake.

## The four things the examiner marks

Every IELTS essay is scored on four criteria, each 25% of your Task 2 band.

1. **Task Response** — did you answer the question that was asked?
2. **Coherence and Cohesion** — is your essay organised, with ideas flowing logically?
3. **Lexical Resource** — is your vocabulary varied and accurate?
4. **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — do you use a mix of sentence structures correctly?

For Band 7, you need at least 7 on each criterion. One weak criterion drags your overall band down.

## The essay structure that works at Band 7

Four paragraphs. Nothing fancy.

- **Introduction** — 2 to 3 sentences.
- **Body paragraph 1** — one main idea, developed.
- **Body paragraph 2** — a different main idea, developed.
- **Conclusion** — 2 sentences.

Total: around 270 to 300 words. The task minimum is 250. Writing much more than 320 usually introduces errors without adding marks.

### Introduction (write this in 3 minutes)

Two parts:

- **Paraphrase the question.** Don't copy — rewrite in your own words.
- **Your thesis.** State clearly what the essay will argue.

> Many governments argue that children should begin learning foreign languages in primary school rather than waiting until secondary education. This essay will argue that early language learning offers clear cognitive and practical benefits, though it works best when teaching is play-based rather than formal.

Notice: no "In today's modern world." No "It is a well-known fact that." Get to the point.

### Body paragraph 1 (10 minutes)

Pick one main idea. Develop it in four sentences:

- **Topic sentence** — the main idea.
- **Explanation** — why this is true.
- **Example** — one specific example. Sri Lankan examples are fine.
- **Link** — one sentence tying it back to the question.

> One clear benefit of early language learning is that young children acquire pronunciation more naturally than older learners. Research in language acquisition has repeatedly shown that children before the age of about ten are more flexible in picking up new sound patterns. In Sri Lanka, children who begin English in Grade 1 often speak with more natural rhythm than those who start in Grade 5, despite having similar exam results. For this reason, starting young offers a long-term advantage that later study cannot fully replace.

### Body paragraph 2 (10 minutes)

Same structure, different idea. If the question is a discussion, this paragraph can present the other side. If it's an opinion essay, this paragraph can offer a nuance or condition.

### Conclusion (3 minutes)

Two sentences:

- Restate your thesis in different words.
- Add a final thought — a recommendation, a condition, or a consequence.

> In conclusion, there are strong reasons to introduce foreign language learning early, particularly when the method suits a child's age. Governments considering this policy should invest as much in teacher training as in curriculum design, since the method matters as much as the age at which learning starts.

## The question types and what they want

Four essay types cover almost all Task 2 questions.

### Opinion essay

"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

Pick a side. Say so clearly in the introduction. Both body paragraphs support your side (or one supports and one acknowledges the other side).

### Discussion essay

"Discuss both views and give your opinion."

One body paragraph on each view. Your opinion comes in either the introduction or the conclusion — pick one and be clear.

### Problem-solution

"What are the problems and what can be done?"

One body paragraph on the problem(s), one on solutions. Be specific — "better education" is not a solution; "including financial literacy in the Grade 10 curriculum" is.

### Two-part question

"Why is this happening and how can it be addressed?"

Each body paragraph answers one part of the question. Simple.

## What pulls your band down from 7 to 6

### Not answering the actual question

If the question asks about causes and solutions, and you write about advantages and disadvantages, you're off topic. Band drops to 5 or 6 regardless of how good the English is. Read the question twice. Underline the question words. Plan to those.

### Using only simple sentences

"People are learning English. English is useful. Jobs need English." Three sentences, three simple structures. Band caps at 6 for Grammar.

**Fix:** mix sentence types.
- Simple: "English is useful."
- Compound: "English is useful, and most Sri Lankan jobs now require it."
- Complex: "Because most Sri Lankan jobs now require English, demand for classes has grown."

Use all three in your essay. Band 7 for grammar is partly about range, not just accuracy.

### Memorised phrases

"In today's fast-paced world" and "Every coin has two sides" are on every examiner's banned list. They're not clever, they're common, and they signal a memorised essay.

### Vague or missing examples

Band 7 Task Response needs developed ideas. A claim without an example is a weak claim.

### Word count under 250

Essays below 250 words are penalised. Count your lines — you need about 25 to 30 lines in normal handwriting.

## Timing: 40 minutes, strictly

- 3 minutes: read the question twice, plan.
- 5 minutes: introduction.
- 10 minutes: body paragraph 1.
- 10 minutes: body paragraph 2.
- 7 minutes: conclusion and proofread.
- 5 minutes buffer: for the paragraph that takes longer than you expected.

Do not go over 40. Task 1 is still waiting, and running over on Task 2 costs you more than finishing a weaker essay.

## Vocabulary for Band 7

You don't need rare words. You need common words used precisely and some range.

Good moves:

- Use topic-specific vocabulary. For an education question: *curriculum, pedagogy, literacy, lifelong learning, cognitive development.* Three or four such words lift the band.
- Use linking words correctly, not everywhere. *However, moreover, consequently, nevertheless* — one or two per paragraph is enough.
- Avoid very formal words you've never used before. Band 7 is for accurate use, not for showing off.

Bad moves:

- Thesaurus-swapping. "Happy" becomes "exuberant," "big" becomes "copious." If the word feels off, don't use it.
- Trying to use ten linking words in one paragraph. Reads as a transition salad.

## Sri Lankan context in your essays

Examples from Sri Lanka are fine — even preferred. Examples make your essay specific, which examiners reward.

> For example, in Sri Lanka's tourism sector, which employs over 150,000 people, English is often the single hiring factor that separates applicants.

Notice the pattern: specific number, specific country, specific effect. Beats "In many countries, English is important for jobs."

## Frequently asked questions

**Does Task 2 count more than Task 1?**

Yes — Task 2 is worth roughly two-thirds of your Writing band. If time is short, prioritise Task 2 in practice.

**Can I memorise essays for IELTS Writing Task 2?**

No. Examiners spot memorised essays easily, and they almost never match the exact question. Memorise structure and useful phrases — not full essays.

**How many words should my IELTS essay be?**

Aim for 270 to 300. Under 250 is penalised; over 320 usually adds errors without adding marks.

**Is it okay to use Sri Lankan examples in my IELTS essay?**

Yes, and often better than invented global examples. Specific real examples score higher than vague international references.

# Spoken English for Adults in Sri Lanka: Where to Actually Start

You can read English. You've written emails in English. You can follow an English film. But the moment you need to speak — a meeting, a new client, an interview, a conversation with a foreign guest — something freezes. The words are there and nothing comes out.

This is the most common pattern we see with adult learners in Sri Lanka. It's not a knowledge problem. It's a usage problem. And it's fixable at any age, from 22 to 62.

## Why adults freeze on spoken English

Three reasons cover most people.

- **School taught you written English.** Grammar rules, essay writing, comprehension. You were almost never asked to speak more than two sentences aloud.
- **You've been punished for mistakes.** Somewhere along the way — a teacher, a boss, an older relative — someone made fun of your English or corrected you in front of others. The brain remembers.
- **You don't speak English anywhere.** Your home, your friends, your workplace — all Sinhala or Tamil. English has no place in your week.

None of these mean you can't speak. They mean your speaking muscles haven't been used.

## What doesn't work (be honest)

- **Grammar books.** You already know the grammar. More rules won't help.
- **Vocabulary apps.** You can learn 2,000 words on an app and still freeze in a conversation. Words you can recognise aren't the same as words you can say.
- **Watching more English films.** Useful for listening, useless for speaking if you never speak back.
- **"Think in English."** Classic advice, almost impossible to follow on purpose.

## What works: speak every day, badly if needed

The single thing that moves adult English is talking to other people in English, out loud, imperfectly, consistently.

Three to five times a week. Even 15 minutes. Even badly.

Where to find this:

- **A spoken English class with other adults.** The gold standard. You get peers who are also there to speak, not to judge.
- **A regular conversation partner.** A colleague, a friend, a language-exchange partner. Someone who meets you for 20 minutes twice a week.
- **A patient family member.** If you have one.

Note what's not on the list: solo apps that use AI voices. They don't carry the social weight of speaking to a human. Your nerves don't fire the same way, which is both a comfort and a limitation.

## The 90-day plan

### Days 1–30: unfreezing

Goal: get to the point where you can say three sentences in English without going red.

- **Daily:** 10 minutes talking to yourself in English. Narrate what you're doing in the kitchen, in the car, while getting ready. It sounds silly. It works.
- **Three times a week:** a 20-minute English conversation with one person. Any topic.
- **Rule:** don't correct yourself mid-sentence. Finish the sentence, right or wrong.
- **One podcast or video** per week, in English, that you can retell in two sentences afterwards.

### Days 31–60: expanding

Goal: hold a 5-minute English conversation on a familiar topic.

- **Daily:** 10 minutes of structured speaking — pick one topic (your job, your weekend, your opinion on a news item), speak for 2 minutes, record, listen back.
- **Three times a week:** 30-minute conversation, this time on topics slightly outside your comfort zone. Travel, work problems, opinions.
- **Add a reading-aloud habit:** 5 minutes a day, any English text, read out loud. It trains your mouth to form the sounds comfortably.
- **Start tracking fillers.** Write down the words you use when stuck — *actually, like, you know, how to say.* Noticing is the first step to replacing them.

### Days 61–90: real-world use

Goal: switch to English in at least one real situation per week — a work meeting, a customer conversation, a client email read aloud.

- **Daily:** 15 minutes of speaking practice, now including Q&A format (practise answering questions, not just monologue).
- **Four times a week:** conversation, now longer sessions — 30 to 45 minutes.
- **One real-world English use per week.** This is the hard one. Switch a normal Sinhala or Tamil conversation at work or with a friend to English for 10 minutes. Take the discomfort.
- **Review recordings.** Pick one 2-minute speaking recording from a week ago. Listen. You'll hear improvement you didn't feel in the moment.

## What success looks like at Day 90

Not perfect. Not "fluent." But:

- You can introduce yourself and talk about your job for two minutes in English.
- You can have a 15-minute conversation without switching back to your first language.
- You can answer questions about your opinion without freezing, even if the grammar isn't textbook.
- You feel less embarrassed about mistakes.

That's a real win, and most adults never get to this point because they keep waiting to be "ready."

## Common adult-learner mistakes

### Waiting until your English is "good enough" before speaking

Speaking is how your English gets good enough. You can't study your way to fluency.

### Comparing yourself to confident speakers

That cousin who's fluent in English either grew up in an English-speaking home or lived abroad. Comparing yourself to them makes you feel worse without teaching you anything.

### Trying to lose your accent

Nobody actually needs a British or American accent. Clear Sri Lankan English is a perfectly good target and more honest than a fake accent that slips the moment you're tired.

### Signing up for a course and never attending

The plan above doesn't work if you miss sessions. Pick a realistic cadence — two sessions a week beats five sessions a week that you keep rescheduling.

## Where classes help

Adult spoken English classes work well when:

- The group is small (6–12 adults, similar level).
- The teacher creates structured speaking tasks, not just open "conversation time."
- Mistakes are not punished.
- There's homework — small tasks that extend into your week.

Signs an adult class is weak:

- Too many beginners mixed with intermediates — the intermediates don't get challenged, the beginners get overwhelmed.
- The teacher talks 80% of the time.
- No speaking homework, no recording, no reflection.

# A/L General English Paper: The Shortest Fair Guide

A/L General English is the paper students treat as an afterthought. "It doesn't count for university entry, no?" "My A/L stream is Science, English is just extra, no?"

Both of those are half-true and half-dangerous. Yes, your main subjects carry the Z-score. No, the English grade doesn't disappear — it sits on your certificate forever, and plenty of employers look at it. Treat it like a paper worth 30 focused hours over the two years of A/Ls and you'll pass it comfortably with a decent grade.

Here's the guide.

## What the paper tests

The A/L General English paper, as set by the Department of Examinations, usually covers:

- **Reading comprehension** — one or two passages with mixed question types.
- **Vocabulary in context** — meaning from surrounding sentences.
- **Grammar** — practical, not deeply technical.
- **Writing** — short pieces and one longer piece.
- **Note-making or summary** — turning a longer text into points.

Exact paper structure can shift year to year, so check the current syllabus on doenets.lk before you plan around specific question counts. The skills tested don't change much.

## How it's graded

A/L General English is reported as an A, B, C, S, or F — the same grading as A/L subjects — but it's calculated separately and doesn't count toward your Z-score. That's why students underrate it.

The grade still:

- Appears on your A/L certificate.
- Shows on job applications, visa applications, further-study applications.
- Gets looked at by local and overseas employers for entry-level roles.

An S pass in General English next to three A's in Science looks worse than a B next to the same A's. Easy to fix.

## Time budget: 30 hours over 18 months

Most students don't need 300 hours of A/L English study. They need 30 focused hours, spread out, mostly in the last six months before the exam.

A sample plan:

- **Months 1–12 of A/Ls:** read something in English weekly. That's it. A short article, a chapter, a page of a novel. Keep your English alive.
- **Months 13–15:** once a week, 30 minutes. Work through one grammar topic or one writing piece.
- **Months 16–18:** twice a week, 45 minutes. Past papers, timed sections, writing practice.

That's about 30–40 hours total — doable alongside Science or Commerce A/Ls without eating time from your main subjects.

## The specific areas to drill

### Reading comprehension

Not deep analysis — speed and accuracy on moderately academic passages.

- Read the questions before reading the passage.
- Underline the line where you find each answer.
- Don't translate in your head. Look for the answer word directly.

Practise with past papers more than anything else. Twenty minutes per comprehension passage is a reasonable target.

### Vocabulary in context

The paper tests your ability to figure out meaning from context, not your ability to memorise rare words.

- Learn common academic vocabulary at your level — the kind that appears in newspaper articles.
- When stuck on a word, ignore it and read the rest of the sentence. The context will carry most of the meaning.
- Watch for prefixes and suffixes: *in-, un-, dis-, -tion, -ment, -able*. They narrow the meaning fast.

### Grammar

The grammar section is practical — tenses in context, subject-verb agreement, prepositions, articles, basic transformations. Not grammar trivia.

- Spend time on tenses you actually misuse, not on tenses you already get right.
- Articles (a, an, the) trip up most Sri Lankan candidates. Learn the common patterns — zero article before plural general nouns, *the* for specific references.
- Prepositions — just memorise the common collocations. *Interested in*, *good at*, *afraid of*, *responsible for*. There's no rule — it's memory.

### Writing

Most candidates lose marks here because they treat it as "write something and finish."

- **Short pieces** (notices, messages, dialogues) have a specific format. Learn the format, follow it exactly.
- **Longer pieces** (letters, short essays) need structure: introduction, two or three body paragraphs, conclusion.
- **Word count** matters. Under-writing loses structure marks; over-writing adds errors.

### Note-making or summary

Underrated section — easy marks if you practise, lost marks if you don't.

- Note-making: use clean short phrases, consistent format, no full sentences.
- Summary: cut to roughly one-third of the original length, keep only the main points, no personal opinion.

## The timing habit

A/L General English isn't a time-pressured paper for most students if they practise — but timing drifts the moment you stop watching the clock.

- Do at least three full past papers under exam timing before the actual exam.
- Note where you ran over. Usually it's writing (because you wrote too much) or comprehension (because you read too slowly).
- Adjust. Practise again.

## What not to do

- **Don't memorise essays.** The topic changes every year.
- **Don't buy ten different workbooks.** Two or three good ones, plus past papers, is enough.
- **Don't skip the paper.** Students who treat it as optional often sit it unprepared and get an F for a paper they could have passed in their sleep.
- **Don't panic in the last month.** You can comfortably prepare for A/L General English in six focused weeks if you've been reading at all during A/Ls.

## The last six weeks

A realistic cram schedule if you've left it late:

- **Week 6:** one past paper, untimed. Mark it. See where you stand.
- **Week 5:** grammar review. Identify your three weak areas. Drill those.
- **Week 4:** writing practice. One short piece and one long piece, marked.
- **Week 3:** comprehension and vocabulary. Three past paper sections under timing.
- **Week 2:** two full past papers, timed. Mark both.
- **Week 1:** one final past paper. Review common mistakes. Don't learn anything new.

Our A/L General English programme in Matara runs weekly sessions that pace the full syllabus over 18 months without overloading students who already have three main subjects to manage. Past papers are marked by hand and returned with specific feedback. Intensive six-week revision batches run before each exam cycle for students who need a faster push.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does A/L General English count toward university entry?**

No — it doesn't count toward your Z-score. But the grade appears on your certificate and matters for employment and overseas applications.

**How much should I study for A/L General English?**

About 30–40 focused hours over 18 months, with more weight in the final six months. Less than that and you risk an S or F; much more and you're taking time away from your main subjects.

**Is A/L General English the same as A/L English (main subject)?**

No. A/L General English is a compulsory short paper for all A/L students. A/L English as a main subject is a full three-paper subject taken by students in the Arts or Commerce streams, covering language and literature in depth.

**Can I pass A/L General English with just past papers?**

If your English is already at a decent level — yes, past papers alone will probably get you a C or B. For an A, you'll want some targeted grammar and writing practice as well.

# English for Hotel and Tourism Jobs in Southern Sri Lanka

The tourism belt along Sri Lanka's south coast — Galle, Weligama, Mirissa, Tangalle, and around Matara — runs on English. Guests are mostly from Europe, Australia, and increasingly East Asia. The front-desk supervisor who speaks confident English earns more than the one who speaks fine Sinhala but struggles with English. The guide who can tell a good story in English gets better tips than the one who can't.

If you work — or want to work — in a hotel, villa, restaurant, or tour operation along the south coast, this is for you. Not grammar. Practical, job-ready English.

## What employers actually want

Owners and managers we've spoken to in Matara and Galle say the same things:

- **Listening first.** Can you understand what a guest is asking, even with their accent?
- **Short, clear responses.** You don't need to speak beautifully. You need to be understood.
- **The right tone.** Polite, warm, confident. Not stiff textbook English.
- **Handling complaints.** This is the skill that separates roles. A worker who can calm an unhappy guest is worth their weight in rupees.
- **Phone English.** Taking reservations, answering questions about rooms and rates.

What they don't test for:

- Complex grammar rules.
- Big vocabulary.
- A fake British or American accent.

Clear, calm, polite Sri Lankan English is a hireable skill. Chase that, not perfection.

## The 200 words that cover most situations

You don't need a huge vocabulary. You need the specific words of your role used confidently.

### Front desk

*Check-in, check-out, reservation, booking, confirmation, passport, ID, deposit, key card, floor, lift, breakfast time, pool, Wi-Fi password, invoice, tax, late checkout, early check-in, luggage, airport transfer.*

### Restaurant and bar

*Menu, special, starter, main course, dessert, drinks, vegetarian, vegan, allergy, spicy, mild, well done, medium rare, bill, service charge, tip, water bottle, ice, sparkling, still, happy hour.*

### Housekeeping

*Towels, sheets, pillows, blanket, mini bar, amenities, shampoo, soap, air conditioning, fan, remote, do-not-disturb, make up the room, extra bed.*

### Tourism and tours

*Pickup time, drop-off, itinerary, duration, guide, driver, entry ticket, sunrise, sunset, waterfall, viewpoint, safari, whale watching, surfing, lesson, beginner, advanced, lifejacket, wetsuit.*

Learn these cold. If you work in front of house, you should be able to use any of them in a sentence without thinking.

## The twelve phrases that do most of the work

If you're starting fresh, drill these first. They cover maybe 60% of daily guest interactions.

1. "Welcome, how can I help you?"
2. "May I have your name, please?"
3. "Just a moment, let me check."
4. "Would you like…?"
5. "I'm sorry about that — let me see what I can do."
6. "Breakfast is served from [time] to [time] in the [place]."
7. "The Wi-Fi password is [password], all small letters."
8. "Would you like me to call a tuk-tuk / taxi for you?"
9. "We have a table available at [time]."
10. "Can I offer you a cold towel / welcome drink?"
11. "Your room is on the [number] floor. The lift is this way."
12. "Have a lovely stay with us."

Say each one out loud 20 times until it comes out without thinking. Confidence shows in phrases you don't have to search for.

## Handling complaints — the skill that pays most

A complaining guest wants three things, in order:

- To feel heard.
- To be apologised to.
- To see a solution.

Scripts that cover 80% of situations:

- **Listen first.** "I'm so sorry — can you tell me what happened?"
- **Acknowledge.** "That's not the experience we want you to have."
- **Offer something.** "Let me speak with the manager and we'll make this right." Or: "I'll send someone up in five minutes to sort it out."
- **Follow up.** "I'll come back myself in ten minutes to make sure everything is okay."

Notice: no excuses, no "sir, sir, sir," no defensiveness. These four moves work on 80% of guests. The remaining 20% need a manager, and that's fine.

## Pronunciation: clear, not perfect

Sri Lankan English is a real variety of English. Guests understand it fine. What they struggle with:

- **Speed.** When nervous, Sri Lankan English speakers speed up. Slow down.
- **Word endings.** Say the final *s, t, d* clearly. "Guest" not "ges." "Ice" not "ai."
- **Volume.** Many hotel staff speak too quietly. Speak at the volume a guest across a lobby desk can hear comfortably.
- **Numbers.** Prices and times are where miscommunication happens. "Fifteen" vs "fifty." "Four pm" vs "five pm." Say numbers slowly and clearly.

You don't need a British accent. You need to be understood.

- **Record yourself.** At the end of your shift, pick three conversations you had with guests. Try to repeat them aloud in English, better than you managed in the moment.
- **Watch front-desk training videos** on YouTube from hotel chains. 10 minutes a day.
- **Shadow a colleague.** Someone at your hotel speaks better English than you. Listen to how they handle calls. Copy their phrasing.
- **Take one shift a week where you push yourself.** Use longer sentences, offer more, engage more. You'll be tired. You'll also improve faster.

## How to practise if you're preparing to apply

If you're not yet working in tourism and want to:

- **Start a dedicated tourism English course** if your English base is weak. A general spoken English class covers the foundations.
- **Memorise the 200 role-specific words** above for the role you want.
- **Prepare three stories** in English — one about yourself, one about Matara / your home area, one about Sri Lankan food. Guests love these.
- **Practise a mock interview** in English. Most hotel interviews test spoken English through a conversation, not a written test.

## Where to work (realistically) with each level

- **Basic English:** housekeeping, kitchen, maintenance, pool, driver. Decent wages, less customer-facing.
- **Intermediate English:** restaurant service, hotel reception support, tour support, drivers who also guide a bit.
- **Confident English:** reception supervisor, concierge, tour guide, sales, restaurant manager. These roles pay noticeably more and tip-opportunity is higher.

Improving your English isn't the only path to promotion, but it's the fastest one in this industry.

For a non-customer-facing role, basic working English is enough and can be built in 3–4 months. For reception or restaurant service, a confident level usually takes 6–12 months of consistent practice.

# IELTS Speaking: What Sri Lankan Candidates Get Wrong

The Speaking test is where Sri Lankan candidates most often leave marks on the table. Our students generally score well on Reading and Listening, pass Writing with work, and then come out of Speaking convinced they did fine — and get back a 6.0 or 6.5 when they expected 7.

Here's why that happens, and what to do differently.

## How the Speaking test is scored

Four criteria, each worth 25% of your Speaking band:

1. **Fluency and Coherence** — can you speak at length, logically, without long pauses?
2. **Lexical Resource** — do you use a varied, precise vocabulary?
3. **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — do you use a mix of sentence structures correctly?
4. **Pronunciation** — are you understood without strain?

None of these is about accent. Band 7 is reachable with a clear Sri Lankan accent. What matters is being understood easily, with range, at a natural pace.

## Mistake 1: one-word answers in Part 1

Part 1 is the warm-up — questions about your home, work or studies, hobbies.

Candidates often answer like this:

> Examiner: Do you enjoy cooking?
> Candidate: Yes.

Technically correct. Band 5. Fluency can't be assessed in one word.

### Fix

Give a two-sentence answer. One sentence directly answers. The second adds detail.

> Yes, I enjoy cooking, especially on weekends when I have time to try new recipes. My favourite thing to cook is a slow-cooked chicken curry — my grandmother's recipe.

That's enough. Don't go longer than three sentences in Part 1 — that's for Part 2.

## Mistake 2: running out of ideas in Part 2

Part 2 gives you a cue card with a topic and four bullet points. You get one minute to prepare, then speak for 1–2 minutes.

The classic mistake: candidate speaks for 40 seconds, then stops.

Silence in Part 2 costs you heavily on Fluency.

### Fix

- **Use the full minute of prep time.** Write quick notes — two or three words per bullet point.
- **If you run out of ideas, repeat yourself using different words.** Describe something again from a different angle. Give an example. Compare it to something.
- **Speak for the full two minutes.** The examiner stops you. You don't stop yourself.

A rough rule: if you think you're running long, keep going. If you think you're done, you're probably at 50 seconds.

## Mistake 3: memorised answers

Candidates learn full answers to common Part 2 cards — "describe a book you read," "describe a place you visited" — and recite them.

Examiners hear this constantly. They move you to a different topic in Part 3, which you weren't ready for, and your band drops hard.

### Fix

- Don't memorise answers. Memorise frameworks. For "describe a place," your framework is: name the place, when and why I went, what I did there, why it matters to me. Fill the framework with real content on the day.
- Practise with uncommon topics so you're not dependent on a specific card.

## Mistake 4: filler words

*Ah, uh, like, basically, actually, you know, how to say.*

These happen when you're thinking. The occasional filler is fine. Constant fillers drop Fluency.

### Fix

- Record yourself for 2 minutes on any topic.
- Count fillers.
- Over a few weeks, replace fillers with silent thinking pauses. A short pause with a calm face is fine. A string of *ahhhs* isn't.

## Mistake 5: simple sentences only

"I live in Matara. Matara is nice. I like my school." Three sentences, all simple. Band caps at 6 for Grammar.

### Fix

Mix in longer structures:

- Compound: "I live in Matara, and I've lived here for most of my life."
- Complex: "Because my family has been in Matara for generations, I feel very connected to the town."
- Relative clause: "Matara, which sits right on the south coast, is known for its beaches."

You don't need every sentence long. You need some variety.

## Mistake 6: translating in your head

Candidates think the sentence in Sinhala or Tamil first, then translate into English. You can hear it in the pauses.

### Fix

- Practise speaking directly in English. Narrate your day in English to yourself.
- Speak faster than feels comfortable at first. Fluency practice is about breaking the translation habit.
- Read English aloud daily. Trains your mouth to form English directly.

## Mistake 7: trying to sound formal

Written essay vocabulary doesn't belong in Speaking. Words like *henceforth, therefore, consequently, elucidate* sound weird in conversation.

### Fix

Use natural spoken English. Linking words for speaking are things like *so, actually, I mean, like I said, that's a good question.* The last one is especially useful in Part 3 — gives you two seconds to think.

## Mistake 8: pronouncing word endings quietly

Many Sri Lankan English speakers soften final sounds — *s, t, d* at the ends of words. The examiner has to work harder to understand, which lowers your Pronunciation score.

### Fix

- Practise minimal pairs: *walk* vs *walks*, *trip* vs *tripped*, *pass* vs *past*.
- Record a short reading. Listen back. If word endings disappear, slow down and pronounce them fully.

## Mistake 9: Part 3 going too short

Part 3 is about abstract discussion. The examiner asks something general, and candidates give a one-sentence answer.

> Examiner: How has technology changed the way people learn?
> Candidate: It's easier now. People can learn online.

Too short. Band drops for both Fluency and Task Response.

### Fix

Answer in three parts: direct answer, explanation, example.

> Technology has changed learning massively — mostly for the better. People can now take courses from universities abroad without leaving home, which wasn't possible twenty years ago. For instance, many Sri Lankan professionals do their master's degrees online with UK universities now, which saves them having to leave their jobs or families.

Three sentences. Direct, developed, specific.

## Mistake 10: not asking the examiner to repeat

Candidates are afraid to say they didn't understand, so they answer a different question than the one that was asked.

### Fix

It is completely acceptable to say:

- "Sorry, could you repeat the question?"
- "Could you rephrase that, please?"

Only in Parts 1 and 3. In Part 2, you work from the written card.

Doing this correctly doesn't lower your band. Answering the wrong question does.

## A realistic weekly practice plan

- **Daily, 10 minutes:** speak for 2 minutes on any topic, recorded. Listen back. Note one thing to improve next time.
- **Three times a week:** full Part 2 practice with a timer. Prepare for 1 minute, speak for 2 minutes. Different topic each time.
- **Twice a week:** Part 3 discussion with a partner or tutor. Not solo — Part 3 is best practised live.
- **Weekly:** full mock Speaking test. Three parts, timed, recorded.

Four weeks of this moves most candidates up half a band in Speaking.

## On test day

- Smile when you walk in. It changes your voice and the examiner's first impression.
- Treat it as a conversation, not a test. The examiner is not your enemy.
- Don't apologise for mistakes. If you catch yourself, say the correction naturally and move on.
- Breathe before Part 2. One slow breath while you take your notes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does my accent affect my IELTS Speaking band?**

Not the accent itself — as long as you can be understood easily. Candidates with clear Sri Lankan accents regularly score Band 7 and above.

**How long is the IELTS Speaking test?**

11 to 14 minutes total. Part 1 is 4–5 minutes, Part 2 is 3–4 minutes (including 1 minute of preparation), Part 3 is 4–5 minutes.

**Can I use "like" and "you know" in IELTS Speaking?**

Occasionally, yes — real spoken English has them. Constantly, no — too many fillers lower your Fluency band.

**What if I don't understand the Part 2 topic?**

You still have to speak on it. There's no changing the card. If the topic is unfamiliar, focus on any angle you can honestly discuss — even if you have to interpret the prompt loosely.

# Phonics for Sri Lankan Kids: A Simple Starter

Your four-year-old can recognise the shapes of letters. They know "A is for apple." They might even write their name. But when you give them a simple English word they've never seen — *cat*, *mat*, *sun* — they look at you as though it's a puzzle. That's because they haven't been taught phonics yet, only letter names.

Phonics is the step that turns letter recognition into actual reading. For Sri Lankan children whose first language is Sinhala or Tamil, it's the single most useful thing a parent can teach in the pre-school years.

## What phonics actually is

Phonics teaches children the **sounds** that letters make, not just their **names**.

- The letter A's **name** is "ay."
- The letter A's **sound** (short) is "a," as in *cat*.

When a child knows letter sounds, they can look at an unfamiliar word like *bat* and sound it out: "buh — a — tuh — bat." They've just read a word they've never seen. That's the beginning of independent reading.

Most Sri Lankan children are taught letter names first and never taught sounds, which is why many struggle with reading English until Grade 3 or beyond.

## Why phonics works especially well for Sri Lankan children

English is tricky because the same letter makes different sounds in different words. But about 80% of basic English words follow phonics rules reliably. If a child masters those rules, reading most storybooks becomes possible around age 5 or 6.

Sinhala and Tamil are more phonetic languages than English — each letter makes one sound almost every time. Children who already know Sinhala or Tamil letters are used to the idea that letters make sounds. Phonics in English fits naturally with that habit.

## The four stages

### Stage 1: Letter sounds (ages 3–5)

Learn the single sound each letter makes.

- Short vowel sounds: **a** (apple), **e** (egg), **i** (insect), **o** (orange), **u** (umbrella).
- Consonants: start with b, c, d, f, g, h, j, k, l, m, n, p, r, s, t.

Teach one new sound every few days. Don't rush.

### Stage 2: Blending (ages 4–6)

The magic step. Children learn to put sounds together into words.

- c + a + t = cat
- s + u + n = sun
- m + a + p = map

This is where children often get stuck for a few weeks. Keep it playful. Use three-letter words only at this stage.

### Stage 3: Common patterns (ages 5–7)

- **Digraphs** — two letters that make one sound. *sh, ch, th, ck, ng.*
- **Magic e** — the *e* at the end that changes the vowel. *cap* becomes *cape*. *hop* becomes *hope*.
- **Vowel teams** — *ee, oo, ai, oa.*

By the end of this stage, a child can read most storybook text with help.

### Stage 4: Tricky words (ages 6–8)

Words that break the rules and just have to be memorised. *The, said, was, were, one, two, you, where, there.* These get a sticker list at home.

## What to use at home

You don't need to buy a kit. These work:

- **Three-letter word flashcards.** Make them. Cat, mat, bat, hat, rat. Point and sound them out together.
- **Magnetic letters on the fridge.** Children build words themselves. Cheap and surprisingly effective.
- **Picture books at the right level.** Oxford Reading Tree, Ladybird Key Words, Biff and Chip — if you can get your hands on them. Local-library stories work too.
- **One phonics app, not ten.** Many are fine. Pick one. Stop looking for the perfect app.
- **A whiteboard.** Children love writing and wiping. Letters and small words stick faster this way than in a notebook.

## How long each day

This matters more than what you use.

- **Ages 3–4:** 5 minutes, maybe twice a day. More is too much.
- **Ages 4–5:** 10 minutes, once or twice a day.
- **Ages 5–7:** 15–20 minutes. Can be longer if the child is enjoying it.

Phonics is not a subject you cram. It's a daily habit, closer to toothbrushing than homework.

## Common parent mistakes

### Teaching letter names and sounds together, confusingly

"A says 'a,' and the letter A is also called 'ay.'" This confuses young children. Teach **sounds first, exclusively.** Add letter names later, once sounds are solid.

### Moving to full words too soon

A child who hasn't nailed the five short vowel sounds can't blend. Rushing to full words fails. Spend time on sounds alone.

### Using a mix of English accents

Some phonics apps teach with strong American accents, others with British. Pick one source and stick with it for the first six months. Consistent sound patterns matter.

### Correcting every mistake

If the child says "ssun" instead of "sun," don't say "no, that's wrong." Model the correct sound: "Sun, yes! Can you hear — sss-uh-nnn, sun?" Keep it light.

### Making it feel like school

Children aged 3–6 learn through play. Phonics works best inside stories, songs, and games. The moment it becomes a worksheet marathon, children switch off.

## Signs your child is ready for the next stage

- They recognise most letter sounds in isolation → ready for blending.
- They can blend three-letter words comfortably → ready for digraphs.
- They can read simple sentences in a familiar book → ready for magic e and vowel teams.
- They can read most beginner storybooks with some help → ready for broader reading.

Don't push ahead until the current stage is comfortable. A shaky foundation will hold a child back for years.

## When to get help

If your child is five or six and still struggling to blend simple words, it's worth having a short assessment with a teacher. It's not always a problem — some children just develop later — but occasional reading differences (dyslexia, phonological processing issues) are easier to support early.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our Little Learners programme in Matara uses phonics as the backbone of early English learning. Classes are play-based, with songs, stories, and games — not worksheets. The focus is on children aged 4–7 who are starting English for the first time, or who need to build a stronger foundation before Grade 3.

## Frequently asked questions

**At what age should I start phonics with my child?**

Around age 3 or 4 for letter sounds, informally. Around age 5 for structured blending. Earlier than 3 is usually too soon — children learn more from being read to at that age.

**Does phonics work for Sri Lankan children whose first language isn't English?**

Yes, and often very well. Children who already know Sinhala or Tamil letter sounds adapt quickly to the idea that English letters have sounds too.

**How long until my child can read storybooks after starting phonics?**

With consistent daily practice, most children can read simple storybooks within 6–9 months of starting Stage 1.

**Is phonics enough, or do I also need to teach whole words?**

Phonics is the foundation. You'll still teach sight words — the irregular words like *the*, *said*, and *was* — but phonics does the heavy lifting for 80% of English reading.

# O/L English Grammar: The 7 Rules That Show Up Every Year

If you spend six months reviewing every grammar rule in the syllabus, you'll know everything and remember nothing by exam day. Here's the opposite approach: seven rules that appear on almost every O/L English paper in Sri Lanka, and the specific errors that cost students marks.

Nail these seven and you'll handle most of the grammar section without panic.

## Rule 1: Subject-verb agreement

The verb matches the subject in number.

- **Singular:** The boy **walks** to school.
- **Plural:** The boys **walk** to school.

Easy when the subject is obvious. Students lose marks when the sentence hides the real subject.

### Where students slip

- **One of the**: "One of the students **has** passed." (Not *have* — the subject is *one*, which is singular.)
- **Each / every**: "Each of the children **has** a pencil." (Not *have*.)
- **Either / neither**: "Neither my brother nor my sister **is** home." (Not *are*.)
- **Collective nouns** like *team, family, government*: usually treated as singular in formal writing. "The team **is** winning."

### Quick drill

Rewrite each wrong sentence correctly:
- "Every one of my friends are coming to the party."
- "The list of names were on the table."
- "Each of these books cost two hundred rupees."

## Rule 2: Tense consistency

Pick a tense and stay in it. Switching tenses mid-paragraph confuses the reader and costs marks.

### Where students slip

Writing a narrative in past tense and then slipping into present:

> I **went** to school yesterday. When I **arrive**, the bell **was** ringing.

*Arrive* should be *arrived*. Simple past throughout.

### The fix

After you write a paragraph, scan every verb. If your opening sentence is past tense, every verb except quoted speech should be past tense.

### Quick drill

Correct the tense in these sentences:
- "She opened the door and sees her brother."
- "We were at the beach and we swim for an hour."
- "The teacher asks the class to be quiet and gave out the papers."

## Rule 3: Articles — a, an, the

The small words that cost big marks.

- **a / an** — one of many, mentioned first time. *A book, an apple.*
- **the** — specific one, already known. *The book on my desk.*
- **No article** — general plurals, uncountable nouns. *Children are noisy. Water is wet.*

### Where students slip

- Missing *the* before superlatives: "She is best student in the class." → "She is **the** best student."
- Missing *the* before unique things: "Sun rises in east." → "**The** sun rises in **the** east."
- Using *the* with uncountable general ideas: "**The** happiness is important." → "Happiness is important."

### Quick drill

Add articles where needed:
- "I saw ___ elephant at ___ zoo. ___ elephant was eating ___ banana."
- "She plays ___ piano beautifully."
- "He is ___ best cricketer I know."

## Rule 4: Prepositions after verbs and adjectives

These don't follow rules — they have to be memorised. The good news: a short list covers the paper.

### Must-know collocations

- *interested in*, *good at*, *bad at*, *afraid of*, *proud of*, *tired of*
- *responsible for*, *famous for*, *known for*, *suitable for*
- *married to*, *similar to*, *related to*, *kind to*, *rude to*
- *angry with*, *pleased with*, *disappointed with*
- *agree with* (a person), *agree on* (an idea), *agree to* (a proposal)
- *depend on*, *rely on*, *insist on*, *concentrate on*

### Where students slip

- "She is good **in** English." → "She is good **at** English."
- "I am interested **about** music." → "I am interested **in** music."
- "He is married **with** my cousin." → "He is married **to** my cousin."

### Quick drill

Fill in the correct preposition:
- "He's responsible ____ the whole project."
- "She's not very good ____ maths."
- "They're similar ____ each other in many ways."

## Rule 5: Direct and indirect speech

The transformation that appears almost every year.

### Direct speech

Uses the actual words, in quotation marks.

> She said, "I am going to Colombo tomorrow."

### Indirect (reported) speech

Reports what was said, usually with tense shifting backwards.

> She said that she **was** going to Colombo the **next day**.

### Key changes

- **Tense goes back one step**: *am* → *was*; *will go* → *would go*; *has eaten* → *had eaten*.
- **Time and place words change**: *tomorrow* → *the next day*; *yesterday* → *the previous day*; *here* → *there*; *now* → *then*.
- **Pronouns shift** to match the speaker: *I* becomes *he* or *she*; *we* becomes *they*.
- **Questions become statements**: "Are you coming?" → "She asked whether I was coming."

### Where students slip

Forgetting to shift pronouns, or keeping the question word order:

- Wrong: "He asked that where I was going."
- Right: "He asked where I was going."

### Quick drill

Convert to indirect speech:
- Ramya said, "I am tired."
- "Have you finished your work?" asked the teacher.
- "I will see you tomorrow," he said.

## Rule 6: Active and passive voice

Another common transformation on the paper.

### Active

Subject does the action.

> The teacher marked the papers.

### Passive

The action is done to the subject.

> The papers were marked by the teacher.

### Formula

- Active: Subject + verb + object
- Passive: Object + *be* (correct tense) + past participle + *by* + subject

### Where students slip

- Wrong tense of *be*: "The letter is **wrote** by him." → "The letter **was written** by him."
- Omitting *be*: "The cake eaten by the children." → "The cake **was** eaten by the children."

### Quick drill

Convert to passive:
- Sri Lanka won the match.
- Someone has stolen my bag.
- The farmer plants rice every year.

## Rule 7: Conditional sentences

Three common types appear on the paper.

### Type 1 — Real possibility (future)

> If it **rains**, we **will stay** home.

- *If* clause: present simple.
- Main clause: *will* + base verb.

### Type 2 — Hypothetical present/future

> If I **had** more money, I **would travel** to Japan.

- *If* clause: past simple.
- Main clause: *would* + base verb.

### Type 3 — Impossible past

> If she **had studied**, she **would have passed**.

- *If* clause: past perfect (*had* + past participle).
- Main clause: *would have* + past participle.

### Where students slip

- Mixing Type 2 and Type 3: "If I **had** more money, I **would have gone** to Japan."
- Using *would* in the *if* clause: "If I **would** have more money…" → wrong. *If* clauses never take *would*.

### Quick drill

Complete the sentence:
- If I were you, I ______ (study) harder.
- If he had told us, we ______ (help) him.
- If it rains tomorrow, we ______ (not go) to the beach.

## How to practise all seven

Not by reading grammar books. By doing paper questions.

- Download five past papers from doenets.lk.
- Do only the grammar sections, timed.
- Mark against the marking scheme.
- Note which rule you got wrong each time.
- The next week, do the same five rule-types from a different past paper.

Four weeks of this covers the seven rules thoroughly. Better than any grammar textbook.

Our O/L English programme in Matara drills these seven rules directly into weekly past-paper practice rather than teaching them as separate lessons. The point is to catch errors in context, where they actually show up in the exam, not in isolated worksheets.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much grammar do I need to know for O/L English?**

Less than most students think. The seven rules above, plus basic punctuation and sentence structure, cover 90% of the grammar marks.

**Is grammar more important in Paper I or Paper II?**

Paper II has more grammar-specific questions — transformations, gap-fills — while Paper I tests grammar indirectly through comprehension and vocabulary in context.

**Should I use a grammar reference book?**

One good one is enough — the Cambridge *English Grammar in Use* (intermediate level) is the standard. Don't buy multiple.

**Is Sri Lankan English grammar different from British English grammar?**

No, the grammar is the same. Spelling and some vocabulary differ slightly, but the O/L paper uses standard British English conventions throughout.

# Teaching English to a 4-Year-Old: What Works, What Doesn't

Four is a lovely age to start English. Children aren't shy yet. They copy sounds effortlessly. They think everything is a game. But four-year-olds also have the attention span of a butterfly, and teaching them English looks nothing like teaching an older child. If you try to use worksheets and drills at this age, you'll get a bored child and no English.

Here's what actually works with Sri Lankan 4-year-olds, drawn from years of running Little Learners classes and talking to parents.

## The three principles

Before anything else, three rules shape everything:

1. **Learning through play, not through study.** If it feels like school, they'll resist.
2. **Short sessions, often.** Ten minutes four times a day beats one hour once a day.
3. **Zero pressure.** No "say it properly," no corrections, no "do it again." A four-year-old who feels judged shuts down.

Keep these in mind through every activity below.

## What actually works

### Storytime in English

Read a short English picture book every day. Not a textbook. A storybook with pictures — *The Very Hungry Caterpillar*, *Brown Bear Brown Bear*, *We're Going on a Bear Hunt*, or anything with a repeating pattern.

- Point to pictures as you read. Say the word.
- Let your child point to pictures they recognise.
- Read the same book over and over. Children love repetition. Repetition is how vocabulary sticks.

Ten minutes a day, consistent. This alone builds a huge vocabulary over a year.

### Songs and rhymes

Children's English songs — *Twinkle Twinkle*, *Head Shoulders Knees and Toes*, *Old MacDonald*, *If You're Happy and You Know It* — teach vocabulary and rhythm without any effort.

- Sing while doing other things — in the car, at bath time, while cooking.
- Do the actions. Movement helps memory.
- YouTube has endless children's song playlists. Pick one with clear pronunciation and loop it.

### Daily routines in English

Pick one or two parts of the day to do in English:

- **Bath time:** "Let's wash your hair. Where are your toes? Is the water warm?"
- **Mealtime:** "What's on your plate? Is it hot? Can you say 'thank you'?"
- **Getting dressed:** "Red T-shirt or blue T-shirt today?"

Twenty minutes a day across these moments, in English, teaches more vocabulary than any flashcard set.

### Colours, shapes, animals, body parts

The classic early-English vocabulary groups. Teach them through pointing games:

- "Can you find something red?"
- "Where is your nose? Where is my nose?"
- "What's this animal? What sound does it make?"

Make it a game, not a lesson. The score is laughter, not correctness.

### Simple instructions

Give your child short English instructions to follow:

- "Bring me the ball."
- "Put the book on the table."
- "Jump five times."

Following instructions in English — without translating in their head — builds real comprehension fast.

### Short videos

A short English cartoon episode (10 minutes), then a conversation about what happened.

- "Who was in the story?"
- "What did the elephant do?"
- "Was he happy or sad?"

Passive watching builds nothing. Watching plus talking afterwards builds a lot.

## What doesn't work

### Worksheets

A four-year-old filling in blanks or copying letters for 30 minutes is a four-year-old who will hate English by age 5. Writing can wait. Oral and listening comprehension come first.

### Flashcards as drills

Flashcards can be used in games ("find the apple," "match the colours") — fine. Rapid-fire quizzing with flashcards — "What's this? What's this? What's this?" — stops being fun at word four and stays unfun for life.

### Expecting them to speak on command

Many parents want their child to perform English for visiting relatives. "Say good morning in English, say your name in English." The child freezes, the parent gets embarrassed, the child learns that English causes embarrassment. Skip this.

### Correcting pronunciation

If your child says "ellyfant," do not say "no, elephant." Just say "yes, the elephant!" and move on. The correct version is now in the air. They'll adjust naturally.

### Only screens

An iPad with an English app for an hour isn't teaching English. It's teaching how to use an iPad. Real spoken interaction with a human — you, a teacher, another child — does far more at this age.

### Long sessions

A 60-minute "English class" for a four-year-old is a 60-minute meltdown waiting to happen. Fifteen minutes is the upper limit for focused activity at this age.

## A realistic weekly plan

### Daily (15–20 minutes total, in small bursts)

- 5 minutes of storybook reading.
- 10 minutes of English-speaking during a routine — bath, breakfast, dressing.
- 5 minutes of a song.

### Weekly

- One short English cartoon with a conversation afterwards.
- One new vocabulary group (fruits one week, animals another).
- One playdate or social moment where the child hears or uses English with someone new.

### Not every day

- Worksheets. Avoid them at four.
- Writing practice. Save for five or six.

This builds a solid English foundation by age six without turning the child off.

## When to think about a class

Not every four-year-old needs a formal English class. But a good one can help if:

- You don't speak English yourself and can't model it comfortably at home.
- Your child is very social and thrives with other kids.
- You want structured exposure without having to plan it yourself.

What a good class looks like at this age:

- **Small group** — six to ten children.
- **Short lessons** — 45 minutes to an hour, not more.
- **Play-based** — songs, stories, games, movement. Not worksheets.
- **Warm teachers** — the child should come home happy.

Avoid classes that drill alphabet writing for an hour. At four, that's not learning. That's endurance.

## The "English only at home" trap

Some parents try to speak only English at home, thinking it will accelerate things. For most Sri Lankan families, this backfires:

- The parents' English isn't strong enough to carry full-day conversations naturally.
- The child misses out on rich Sinhala or Tamil conversation at the age when their first language is forming.
- The household gets quieter and more awkward.

A short daily English window is usually better than a half-hearted all-day attempt.

## Signs of progress at four

Every child moves differently, but after six months of consistent gentle exposure you can usually expect:

- Recognises 50–100 English words when they hear them.
- Can follow simple two-step English instructions ("Bring me your shoes and sit here.").
- Uses 20–40 English words themselves, mostly single words or two-word phrases.
- Enjoys English storybooks and asks for favourites.
- Doesn't freeze when someone speaks English to them.

If none of this has happened after six months, the issue is probably the method — not the child.

Sessions are short — around an hour — and parents are welcome to observe for the first few weeks until the child settles.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it okay to start English with my four-year-old if we only speak Sinhala or Tamil at home?**

Yes. Many Sri Lankan children start English at four without any English spoken at home and do very well. What matters is consistency, not a bilingual household.

**Will learning English confuse my child if they're still learning Sinhala or Tamil?**

No. Young children handle two or three languages at once without trouble. Each language fills its own space in their mind.

**Should my 4-year-old be writing English letters?**

Writing can wait until 5 or 6. Fine motor skills aren't ready at four, and pushing writing early builds frustration. Focus on listening and speaking.

**How much screen time is okay for English learning at this age?**

Around 15–30 minutes a day of good-quality English content with a conversation afterwards. More than that and screens stop teaching and start distracting.

# English Classes vs School English: What Private Tuition Actually Adds

Not every child needs English tuition. Some children get strong English at home, at school, through reading, and never see the inside of a tuition class. If that's your family, good — save the money and the evenings.

But many parents sign up for English tuition out of a vague feeling that it's necessary, without knowing what the class is actually supposed to add. Here's a clearer way to think about it.

## What school English does well

Sri Lankan schools, both government and private, cover a lot of English well:

- **Grammar foundations.** Tenses, parts of speech, sentence structure.
- **Reading comprehension.** Texts at age-appropriate levels, standard question types.
- **Writing basics.** Letter writing, essays, short compositions.
- **Vocabulary.** Through readers, textbooks, and classroom discussion.
- **Exam format familiarity.** Students see past paper questions from the O/L and A/L syllabus through the year.

If your child has a good English teacher and a reasonable class size, school English covers the written side of the subject reasonably well.

## What school English struggles with

Class sizes of 35 to 45 children mean three things get crowded out, whatever the teacher's skill:

### 1. Speaking practice

Thirty-five children, forty minutes, maybe one student speaks per minute for a few seconds. By the end of a year, each child has spoken English in class for a total of maybe five hours. That's not enough to build fluency.

### 2. Individual feedback on writing

A teacher with 45 essays to mark can give ticks and grades, not detailed feedback. Students don't know exactly what pushed them from a B to a C, or how to get to an A.

### 3. Specific weakness coaching

A child who struggles with articles, or reported speech, or essay structure can't get individual attention in a large class. The teacher has to move on.

These three gaps are what tuition classes exist to fill. Any English class that doesn't actively fix at least one of them isn't really adding anything.

## What a good English tuition class should add

If you're considering tuition, this is the measure. A class is worth it when it adds one or more of these:

### Real speaking time

Small groups where every student speaks every lesson. Not big "spoken English" classes where speaking is a theoretical topic. If your child doesn't come home more comfortable speaking English after three months, the class isn't adding speaking.

### Hand-marked writing

Essays and letters returned with corrections, specific comments, and suggestions. Not a tick and a grade. If the only mark on the page is a tick, writing isn't improving.

### Exam-specific coaching

Past paper drills, marking scheme familiarity, specific question-type weaknesses addressed. Especially important in Grades 10, 11, and A/L.

### Fluency through small groups

Peer learning. Children speak more freely around other children than around adults, and more freely still in groups of eight than in classes of forty.

### Confidence

A child who's shy in school because the class is huge may be the loudest in a small tuition group. That's real progress.

## What a bad English tuition class adds

Be honest with yourself about what you're paying for. Tuition isn't worth it if:

- The class is just school material covered again, in a different room.
- The batch has 30 students (same as school).
- Written work is returned with ticks only.
- Your child says nothing in class.
- The teacher reads from a book for an hour.

In this case, you're paying for a second school day. Your child would do better with more sleep.

## When tuition genuinely helps

Some clear scenarios where tuition adds real value:

### For speaking

A child reads English fluently but freezes when asked to speak. School can't fix this. A small-group spoken English class can.

### For exam prep

Grades 10 and 11 students facing O/L, and A/L students in their final year. Past-paper drilling and marking-scheme familiarity are where tuition pays off most clearly.

### For foundations if school was weak

Children who had weak primary-school English — maybe the teacher was off sick a lot, or the school's English programme is poor — benefit hugely from a solid tuition class to catch up.

### For specific tests

IELTS, TOEFL, specific overseas or scholarship tests. School doesn't teach these. Tuition does.

### For working adults

School English is long behind you. A class is often the quickest way back to usable English.

## When tuition probably isn't needed

A few honest scenarios:

- Your child reads English books for fun. (A reader at home will often outpace a student in class.)
- Your child speaks English at home with at least one parent or sibling.
- Your child is in Grades 1–3 and school English is going fine. More English isn't automatically better — childhood matters.
- You can't actually afford it. A stressed family is worse than a slightly weaker English grade. There are free alternatives — library books, YouTube, English cartoons, conversation practice at home.

## How to pick, if you decide you want tuition

Four questions to ask before you register:

- **Will my child actually speak in class?** Ask the question literally. Get a real answer.
- **How is written work marked?** Ticks only = don't bother. Corrections and comments = good.

# Present Perfect vs Past Simple: The Difference Sri Lankans Miss

"I have gone to Galle yesterday." If you've said something like this in English, you're not alone. Most Sri Lankan learners mix up the present perfect and the past simple, and school rarely explains the difference clearly. Once you see it, though, the rule is simple and the confusion disappears.

Here's the clearest explanation you'll read today, with examples pulled from everyday Sri Lankan life.

## The one-line difference

- **Past simple** — tells you *when* something happened.
- **Present perfect** — tells you *that* something happened, without caring when.

Past simple is attached to a time in the past: *yesterday, last week, in 2019, when I was a child*.

Present perfect floats in time. The result is still true now, but you're not pinning down when it happened.

## Past simple: finished action with a clear time

Form: verb + -ed (regular) or the second form of irregular verbs.

- I **visited** Kandy last month.
- She **ate** rice and curry for lunch.
- We **watched** the match yesterday.
- They **finished** the exam at 4pm.

You can always answer *when* with a past simple sentence. "When did you visit Kandy?" "Last month."

If the sentence has a specific past time — *yesterday, ago, in 2020, last Sunday, when I was 10* — you need past simple. Not present perfect.

- ✗ I have gone to Galle yesterday.
- ✓ I went to Galle yesterday.

## Present perfect: experience, result, or unfinished time

Form: *have / has* + past participle (third form of the verb).

Three common uses:

### 1. Life experience (ever, never, in your life so far)

- I **have been** to Sigiriya. (At some point in my life — doesn't matter when.)
- She **has never eaten** sushi.
- Have you ever **played** cricket?

Notice: no specific time. We're talking about experience, not an event.

### 2. Result still matters now

- I **have lost** my keys. (Result: they're still lost right now.)
- She **has broken** her leg. (Result: she's still in a cast.)
- They **have moved** to Matara. (Result: they live in Matara now.)

The action happened in the past; the consequence is present.

### 3. Unfinished time (today, this week, this year)

Time periods that include the present moment.

- I **have had** three cups of tea today. (Today isn't over.)
- She **has visited** two countries this year. (This year isn't over.)
- We **have seen** each other twice this week. (This week isn't over.)

Once the time period finishes, you switch to past simple.

- I **had** three cups of tea yesterday. (Yesterday is over.)

## The signal words

Some words almost always go with each tense.

### Past simple signals

*yesterday, ago, last week/month/year, in 2019, when I was young, at 3pm, on Monday*

### Present perfect signals

*ever, never, already, yet, just, so far, recently, lately, since, for*

- I have **just** finished my homework.
- Have you **ever** seen a whale?
- She hasn't called me **yet**.
- We've lived in Matara **for** ten years.
- They've been friends **since** school.

### Watch for *since* and *for*

Both go with present perfect when describing a duration continuing to now.

- *since* + a point in time: *since Monday, since 2019, since I was a child.*
- *for* + a length of time: *for two years, for a week, for my whole life.*

- We **have lived** in Matara **since** 2010.
- We **have lived** in Matara **for** 15 years.

## Common Sri Lankan mix-ups

### Mix-up 1: Present perfect with "yesterday"

- ✗ He has come yesterday.
- ✓ He came yesterday.

If there's a specific past time, use past simple.

### Mix-up 2: Past simple with "ever"

- ✗ Did you ever go to London?
- ✓ Have you ever been to London?

*Ever* for life experience signals present perfect.

### Mix-up 3: Past simple with "already"

- ✗ I already ate my lunch.
- ✓ I have already eaten my lunch.

*Already* usually takes present perfect in British/Sri Lankan English. (In American English, both are accepted, but for O/L and IELTS, use the present perfect version.)

### Mix-up 4: Confusing "been to" and "gone to"

- *have been to* = visited and returned.
- *have gone to* = went and is still there.

- She **has been to** Colombo. (She visited and came back.)
- She **has gone to** Colombo. (She's still there now.)

### Mix-up 5: Over-using the present perfect

Some learners use present perfect for everything after they discover it. "I have ate breakfast. I have went to work." Both wrong. If you can name the time, use past simple.

## Quick test yourself

Fill in the correct tense:

1. I ______ (not finish) my homework yet.
2. We ______ (visit) Yala National Park last December.
3. She ______ (live) in this house since 2015.
4. ______ you ever ______ (try) crab curry?
5. The bus ______ (leave) five minutes ago.
6. He ______ (just / arrive) from Colombo.
7. They ______ (not see) each other for a long time.
8. I ______ (watch) the movie yesterday.

Answers: 1. haven't finished. 2. visited. 3. has lived. 4. Have / tried. 5. left. 6. has just arrived. 7. haven't seen. 8. watched.

If you got 6 or more right, you've got this. Fewer than that, read the examples again.

## Where this tense pair shows up

### O/L English

Transformation questions, gap-fills in the grammar section, and constructed sentences in Paper II. The rule above handles almost every question.

### IELTS

Comes up constantly in Speaking (life experiences) and in Writing Task 2 (unfinished time: *the world has changed*, *governments have introduced*).

### Everyday English

The two tenses carry a huge amount of conversation. Mixing them up isn't a crime, but using them correctly makes your English sound noticeably more natural.

## How to drill this

- **Narrate your day in past simple.** "I woke up at 6. I had tea at 7. I left for work at 8."
- **Describe your life in present perfect.** "I have lived in Matara all my life. I have travelled to three countries. I have never tried bungee jumping."
- **Convert sentences.** Write 10 past simple sentences about yesterday, then rewrite the same actions in present perfect as if they just happened: *I ate rice for lunch* → *I have just eaten rice for lunch*.

Fifteen minutes a day for a week and you'll stop mixing them up.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our O/L English and spoken English programmes drill tenses through writing and conversation, not just worksheets. If present perfect still feels confusing after reading this, a few weeks of structured practice usually locks it in.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is present perfect so difficult for Sri Lankans?**

Sinhala and Tamil don't have an exact equivalent, so learners have to think about it rather than feel it. With practice it becomes automatic.

**Can I use present perfect with "ago"?**

No. *Ago* always takes past simple. "Two years ago" means a specific past time, which the present perfect doesn't allow.

**Is present perfect only for finished actions?**

No. It can also describe ongoing situations from the past to now, especially with *for* and *since*. "She has worked here for ten years" — still working.

**What's the easiest way to remember which to use?**

Ask yourself: "Can I say when?" If yes, past simple. If no, usually present perfect.

# English Articles (a, an, the): The Simplest Explanation You'll Read

Articles are the first words English learners meet and the last ones they get right. Sinhala and Tamil don't use articles the way English does, so most Sri Lankan learners either skip them, overuse them, or sprinkle *the* randomly hoping something lands.

Good news: the core rules fit on a single page. Here they are, with examples pulled from daily Sri Lankan life.

## The three articles (and the fourth option)

- **a** — before a word that starts with a consonant sound. *A book, a car, a university.*
- **an** — before a word that starts with a vowel sound. *An apple, an umbrella, an hour.*
- **the** — specific, known to both speaker and listener.
- **No article** — the "zero article" — general ideas, plural generalisations, most uncountable nouns.

Notice: it's the **sound**, not the letter, that matters. *A university* (sounds like "yoo"), *an hour* (sounds like "ow").

## When to use *a* or *an*

Use *a / an* when you're introducing something for the first time, or when any one of a type will do.

- I need **a** pen. (Any pen.)
- She's reading **a** book. (First mention.)
- He's **a** teacher. (One of many teachers.)
- I had **an** egg for breakfast. (One of several.)

Use with **singular countable nouns** only. Not with plurals (*a books*) and not with uncountables (*a milk*).

## When to use *the*

Use *the* when both you and the listener know which specific one you mean.

### 1. Already mentioned

- I bought **a** book. **The** book was expensive.

The second time, the listener knows which book.

### 2. Only one exists

- **The** sun rose at 6 am.
- Please close **the** door. (The one in this room.)
- **The** President addressed the nation.

### 3. Specified by context

- **The** boy in the blue shirt is my brother. (Which boy? The one in the blue shirt.)
- Pass me **the** salt. (The one on the table we're sitting at.)

### 4. Superlatives and ordinals

- **The** best student in class.
- **The** first day of school.

### 5. Places with compound or plural names

- **The** United Kingdom, **the** Philippines, **the** Netherlands.
- **The** Indian Ocean, **the** Nile, **the** Himalayas.

But: Sri Lanka (no *the*), India (no *the*), Mount Everest (no *the*), Lake Gregory (no *the*).

## When to use no article

The zero article trips up most learners. Use nothing in these cases.

### 1. General plural nouns

- Children love ice cream. (Children in general.)
- Elephants are big.

### 2. Most uncountable nouns speaking generally

- Water is essential.
- Rice is grown in Sri Lanka.
- Happiness matters.

### 3. Most countries, cities, continents

- I live in Sri Lanka.
- She's from Matara.
- We travelled to Asia.

### 4. Meals, days, months

- I have lunch at noon.
- See you on Monday.
- It rains in November.

### 5. Languages and subjects

- She speaks Sinhala.
- He teaches mathematics.

### 6. Many common phrases

- Go to school, go to bed, go to work, go home, go to church, go to hospital (BrE — means as a patient).

## Common Sri Lankan article mistakes

### Mistake 1: Dropping *the* before unique things

- ✗ Sun rises in east.
- ✓ **The** sun rises in **the** east.

### Mistake 2: Using *the* with general plurals

- ✗ The children are noisy. (If you mean children in general.)
- ✓ Children are noisy.

But: *The children in Grade 3 are noisy* — specific group, use *the*.

### Mistake 3: Using articles with abstract uncountables

- ✗ The happiness is important.
- ✓ Happiness is important.

Unless you're specifying — *the happiness of a child is different from the happiness of an adult.*

### Mistake 4: Missing article before a job title

- ✗ She is doctor.
- ✓ She is **a** doctor.

### Mistake 5: *A* before vowel sounds

- ✗ A honour, a hour.
- ✓ **An** honour, **an** hour.

The *h* is silent, so the word starts with a vowel sound.

### Mistake 6: Confusing institutions

- *Go to school* — meaning as a student. No article.
- *Go to the school* — meaning the physical building, for any reason.
- *Go to bed* — meaning to sleep. No article.
- *Go to the bed* — the specific piece of furniture.

Same pattern with hospital, church, university, prison. The article changes the meaning.

## The one-second test

Not sure which article to use? Ask these three questions in order.

1. **Is the noun specific — can both of us point to it?** If yes, use *the*.
2. **Is the noun one of many, singular, countable, and first mentioned?** If yes, use *a* or *an*.
3. **Is it a general plural, uncountable, or a name without an article?** If yes, use nothing.

Run this for the first week and articles stop feeling random.

## Quick drill

Add *a*, *an*, *the*, or nothing:

1. I bought ___ bike yesterday. ___ bike is red.
2. Can you pass me ___ salt?
3. ___ elephants are intelligent animals.
4. She's ___ doctor at ___ Matara hospital.
5. We're going to ___ Sri Lanka next month.
6. ___ Nile is ___ longest river in Africa.
7. He eats ___ rice every day.
8. There's ___ owl in ___ garden.

Answers: 1. a / The. 2. the. 3. – (zero). 4. a / – (or "the" if specific). 5. – (zero). 6. The / the. 7. – (zero). 8. an / the.

## Why articles are worth getting right

- O/L and A/L gap-fills test articles directly.
- IELTS writing rubrics mark grammar range — article accuracy lifts your band.
- Spoken English with consistent articles sounds significantly more natural.
- Business emails without articles read as abrupt or incorrect.

Fixing articles alone can move a Writing band half a point.

## How to practise

- **Read a page of a novel and circle every article.** Ask why it's there.
- **Write a paragraph with no articles.** Then go back and add them. See what the paragraph needs.
- **Listen to native English and notice articles.** They're spoken quickly but they're always there.

Four weeks of this builds an instinct. The rules become automatic.

Our O/L and IELTS classes catch article errors in students' writing week by week. We don't teach articles as a single topic — we flag them in context, which is how they actually stick.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do Sri Lankans struggle with English articles?**

Sinhala and Tamil don't use articles the same way. Most learners don't need articles in their first language, so English feels like adding unnecessary words. Practice makes them feel natural.

**Should I use *a* or *an* before "hour"?**

*An hour.* The *h* is silent, so the word starts with a vowel sound.

**Is it "the Sri Lanka" or "Sri Lanka"?**

Just *Sri Lanka* — no article. Most single-word country names skip *the*.

**Can I ever use *the* with a person's name?**

Rarely, and usually for family nicknames ("the Jayasinghes are coming") or titles ("the Prime Minister"). Not with individual names.

# Prepositions After Verbs: The 40 You Actually Need

Prepositions are the small words that don't follow rules. *Interested in*, not *interested on*. *Married to*, not *married with*. *Depend on*, not *depend of*. There's no logic — only memory.

Here are the 40 verb-preposition pairs that show up most often in Sri Lankan English, grouped so they're easier to remember. Learn these cold and your English stops sounding translated.

## Agree

- **agree with** (a person) — I agree **with** you.
- **agree on / about** (a topic) — We agreed **on** the price.
- **agree to** (a proposal) — She agreed **to** help.

## Argue

- **argue with** (a person) — Don't argue **with** your parents.
- **argue about** (a topic) — They argued **about** money.

## Apologise

- **apologise to** (a person) — I apologised **to** my teacher.
- **apologise for** (an action) — He apologised **for** being late.

Note: *apologise*, not *apologise with* or *apologise from*.

## Ask

- **ask for** (something you want) — She asked **for** help.
- **ask about** (information) — He asked **about** the exam.

## Believe

- **believe in** (something exists / have faith in) — I believe **in** hard work.
- **believe (someone)** — I believe you. (No preposition.)

## Belong

- **belong to** — This book belongs **to** my sister.

## Care

- **care about** (matters to you) — She cares **about** the environment.
- **care for** (look after) — He cares **for** his grandmother.

## Complain

- **complain about** (a problem) — She complained **about** the noise.
- **complain to** (a person) — He complained **to** the manager.

## Depend / Rely

- **depend on** — Success depends **on** effort.
- **rely on** — You can rely **on** her.

Never *depend of*, never *depend from*.

## Dream

- **dream about** (during sleep) — I dreamt **about** flying.
- **dream of** (a wish) — She dreams **of** becoming a doctor.

## Hear

- **hear about** (news) — I heard **about** the accident.
- **hear from** (receive a message) — Have you heard **from** her?
- **hear of** (know the name) — I've never heard **of** that band.

## Insist

- **insist on** — He insisted **on** paying.

## Laugh

- **laugh at** (mocking) — Don't laugh **at** him.
- **laugh about** (something funny) — We laughed **about** it later.

## Listen

- **listen to** — I listen **to** music every day.

Never *listen music* (very common Sri Lankan error).

## Look

- **look at** — Look **at** this picture.
- **look for** (search) — I'm looking **for** my keys.
- **look after** (take care of) — She looks **after** her younger brother.
- **look forward to** (await happily) — We look forward **to** the holidays.

## Pay

- **pay for** (something bought) — She paid **for** the meal.
- **pay someone** (direct, no preposition) — I paid the driver.

## Prepare

- **prepare for** — He's preparing **for** the IELTS.
- **prepare (a meal)** — She prepared dinner. (No preposition.)

## Succeed

- **succeed in** — She succeeded **in** finding a job.

Note: *succeed in*, not *succeed to* (that's a throne).

## Suffer

- **suffer from** (an illness) — He suffers **from** asthma.

## Talk / Speak

- **talk to / speak to** (a person) — I spoke **to** the teacher.
- **talk about / speak about** (a topic) — They talked **about** cricket.

## Think

- **think about** (consider) — Think **about** the question.
- **think of** (have an opinion) — What do you think **of** this plan?

## Wait

- **wait for** — I'm waiting **for** the bus.

Never *wait* alone when there's an object. Not *I'm waiting the bus*.

## Worry

- **worry about** — Don't worry **about** me.

## Write

- **write to** (a person) — She wrote **to** her cousin.
- **write about** (a topic) — He wrote **about** climate change.

## Adjective + preposition (bonus, but essential)

These aren't verbs, but they appear with verbs constantly and get mixed up:

- *good at*, *bad at*, *clever at* (skills)
- *afraid of*, *proud of*, *tired of*, *fond of*
- *angry with / at* (a person), *angry about* (a situation)
- *married to*, *similar to*, *related to*, *kind to*, *rude to*
- *interested in*, *involved in*, *experienced in*
- *responsible for*, *famous for*, *known for*, *suitable for*
- *pleased with*, *disappointed with*, *satisfied with*

## The six that trip up Sri Lankans most

1. **Listen to** music — not *listen music*.
2. **Depend on** — not *depend of*.
3. **Married to** — not *married with*.
4. **Discuss** (no preposition!) — *We discussed the problem*, not *we discussed about the problem*.
5. **Different from** — not *different than* (BrE).
6. **Agree with** a person, **agree on** a topic.

## Three verbs that take no preposition (contrary to first instinct)

Sri Lankan learners often add a preposition where none is needed.

- **discuss** — *We discussed the issue.*
- **enter** — *She entered the room.* (Not *entered into* — that's a contract.)
- **answer** — *Please answer the question.* (Not *answer to*.)

## How to learn these without a boring list

### Method 1: Chunks, not pairs

Learn whole phrases, not verb-plus-preposition in isolation.

- "I'm looking forward to seeing you."
- "She's good at maths."
- "He depends on his parents."

Memorise the sentence, not the grammar point.

### Method 2: Personal examples

Write 10 sentences using the prepositions above, about your own life.

- "I'm interested in cricket."
- "I'm good at cooking."
- "My brother is afraid of dogs."

Your own sentences stick far better than someone else's examples.

### Method 3: Correct your past mistakes

Read something you wrote last month. Underline every verb. Check the preposition. Fix anything wrong. Rewriting your own errors is the fastest way to never repeat them.

### Method 4: Flashcards (yes, really)

For stubborn ones. Verb on one side, preposition on the other. Five minutes a day.

## Where these show up in exams

### O/L English

Gap-fills regularly test verb-preposition combinations. "She is good ___ English." (*at*.)

### A/L General English

Preposition questions appear in the grammar section almost every year.

### IELTS

Writing and Speaking both mark grammatical accuracy. Wrong prepositions don't always lose a point, but a pattern of wrong prepositions keeps your band at 6.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are English prepositions so irregular?**

Historical reasons. Many were set centuries ago and never standardised by rule. There's no shortcut — you memorise the common ones and the rest become natural.

**Is it *different from* or *different to*?**

Both are grammatically acceptable in British English. *Different from* is the safer, more formal choice. Avoid *different than* (that's American).

**Should I say *married with* or *married to*?**

*Married to.* "Married with" means something different: "She is married with two children" (i.e., she's married and has two children).

**Is it *discuss about* or just *discuss*?**

Just *discuss*. The verb already includes the "about" meaning. "We discussed the problem" is correct.

# Active vs Passive Voice: When to Use Each (and Why)

Most English teachers tell students "avoid the passive voice." That's bad advice. The passive voice is useful — in the right places. The real skill is knowing when to use each.

Here's a clear guide, with examples from everyday Sri Lankan writing and speaking, plus the transformation rule for O/L and A/L exam questions.

## The core difference

- **Active:** the subject does the action.
  - *The teacher marked the papers.*
- **Passive:** the action is done to the subject.
  - *The papers were marked by the teacher.*

Same event, different focus. In active, the teacher is the star. In passive, the papers are.

## The passive formula

- **Passive = the right form of "to be" + past participle (3rd form of the verb)**

### By tense

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple | She writes the report. | The report is written. |
| Present continuous | She is writing the report. | The report is being written. |
| Past simple | She wrote the report. | The report was written. |
| Past continuous | She was writing the report. | The report was being written. |
| Present perfect | She has written the report. | The report has been written. |
| Past perfect | She had written the report. | The report had been written. |
| Future | She will write the report. | The report will be written. |

The pattern is always: form of *be* (correct tense) + past participle.

## When to use the passive

### 1. The doer is unknown

- *My bike was stolen.* (I don't know who stole it.)
- *The window has been broken.* (By someone — we don't know.)

### 2. The doer is obvious

- *He was arrested.* (By the police. We don't need to say.)
- *She was born in Galle.* (By her parents, obviously.)

### 3. The doer is unimportant

- *The road has been repaired.* (The focus is the road, not who repaired it.)
- *English is spoken worldwide.* (By many people — not relevant.)

### 4. Formal writing — reports, academic, news

- *The results were analysed.*
- *The policy has been reviewed.*
- *Three suspects have been detained.*

Passive gives distance. It's the voice of news and officialdom.

### 5. When the object is more interesting than the subject

- Active: *A tsunami hit the south coast in 2004.*
- Passive: *The south coast was hit by a tsunami in 2004.*

Both work. The passive puts the coast first, where the focus is.

## When to avoid the passive

### 1. Everyday conversation

Passive sounds stiff in speech.

- ✗ *The ball was kicked by me.*
- ✓ *I kicked the ball.*

### 2. When you want to assign responsibility

- *Mistakes were made.* (Passive — nobody blamed.)
- *I made mistakes.* (Active — honest.)

Politicians love the passive for exactly this reason. Honest writers avoid it.

### 3. When active is simply clearer and shorter

- ✗ *The cake was eaten by the children.*
- ✓ *The children ate the cake.*

Nothing is gained by using the longer version.

### 4. In most IELTS writing

The IELTS writing band rewards clear, varied sentences. Over-using passive drops your Lexical Resource and Coherence scores. One or two passive sentences per essay is fine — more than that and the essay reads as awkward.

## Common Sri Lankan passive mistakes

### Mistake 1: Missing "be"

- ✗ *The letter wrote by him.*
- ✓ *The letter was written by him.*

The *be* form is not optional.

### Mistake 2: Wrong participle form

- ✗ *The cake was eat by the children.*
- ✓ *The cake was eaten by the children.*

Use the past participle (third form), not the base form.

### Mistake 3: Keeping the active object

- ✗ *The ball was kicked the goal.*
- ✓ *The ball was kicked into the goal.*

### Mistake 4: Forgetting *by* with the agent

- ✗ *The book was written him.*
- ✓ *The book was written **by** him.*

If you mention the doer at all in a passive sentence, it needs *by*.

## The transformation rule (for O/L and A/L)

Exam questions often ask you to change active to passive or back.

### Active to passive — four steps

1. **Find the object** of the active sentence (the thing receiving the action).
2. **Move it to the subject position.**
3. **Change the verb** to the correct tense of *be* + past participle.
4. **Add the original subject** at the end with *by* (or drop it if not needed).

### Example

Active: *The chef cooked a delicious meal.*

- Object: *a delicious meal*.
- New subject: *A delicious meal*.
- Verb: past simple active (*cooked*) becomes past simple passive (*was cooked*).
- Original subject: *the chef* → *by the chef*.

Passive: *A delicious meal was cooked by the chef.*

### Try these

Convert to passive:

1. The teacher explains the lesson.
2. Someone has broken the window.
3. They will announce the results tomorrow.
4. The farmers are planting rice.
5. The cat ate the fish.

Answers:

1. The lesson is explained by the teacher.
2. The window has been broken.
3. The results will be announced tomorrow.
4. Rice is being planted by the farmers.
5. The fish was eaten by the cat.

## Passive tips for IELTS Writing

- Use passive 2–3 times in a Task 1 (data) essay, where "The data shows" and "It can be seen" sound natural.
- Use active mostly in Task 2 — your opinions and examples sound stronger in active voice.
- Avoid stacking passive sentences — two or more in a row sounds heavy.

## Passive for O/L English

- Transformation questions appear almost every year.
- Dialogue and short composition questions sometimes require you to report what was done (often passive).
- Comprehension answers using text evidence are often passive.

Learn the four-step transformation and most exam questions become mechanical.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our O/L and A/L English programmes drill the transformation in both directions until students can do it without thinking. Our IELTS classes focus on *when* to use passive, which matters more for Writing bands than *how*.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is passive voice bad in English?**

No — only overused passive. Used in the right places (unknown doer, formal writing, when the object is the focus), passive is correct and natural.

**How do I know if a sentence is passive?**

Look for the pattern: form of *be* + past participle (often ending in *-ed* or *-en*). If the subject is receiving the action, it's passive.

**Why does IELTS mark down heavy passive?**

Overuse of passive makes writing less varied and less clear. The marking rubric rewards range and clarity, both of which active supports better.

**Do all languages have passive voice?**

Not in the same way. Sinhala and Tamil express similar ideas differently, which is why the English passive can feel odd to Sri Lankan learners at first.

# Reported Speech: A Clear Guide for Sri Lankan Students

Reported speech — turning what someone said into a report of what they said — appears on almost every O/L English paper and shows up in A/L General English too. Students lose marks on this question not because the rules are hard, but because the rules aren't taught in a clear order.

Here's the clear order.

## The core idea

- **Direct speech** — the exact words someone said, in quotation marks.
  - *She said, "I am hungry."*
- **Reported (indirect) speech** — a report of what they said, without quotation marks.
  - *She said that she was hungry.*

Three things change when you move from direct to reported:

1. The **tense** shifts backwards one step.
2. The **pronouns** shift to match the reporter's point of view.
3. The **time and place words** shift.

Everything else stays the same.

## Rule 1: Tense shifts backwards

When the reporting verb (*said, told*) is in the past tense, the reported tense usually moves back one step.

| Direct speech | Reported speech |
|---|---|
| Present simple — *I am* | Past simple — *she was* |
| Present continuous — *I am eating* | Past continuous — *she was eating* |
| Present perfect — *I have eaten* | Past perfect — *she had eaten* |
| Past simple — *I ate* | Past perfect — *she had eaten* |
| *will* | *would* |
| *can* | *could* |
| *may* | *might* |
| *must* | *had to* |

### Example

Direct: *He said, "I am going to Colombo."*
Reported: *He said that he **was going** to Colombo.*

Direct: *She said, "I have finished my homework."*
Reported: *She said that she **had finished** her homework.*

Direct: *They said, "We will travel next week."*
Reported: *They said that they **would travel** the following week.*

## Rule 2: Pronouns change

Pronouns shift to match the perspective of the person reporting.

- *I* → *he / she*
- *we* → *they*
- *my* → *his / her*
- *our* → *their*
- *you* → depends on context

### Example

Direct: *She said, "I love my school."*
Reported: *She said that **she** loved **her** school.*

Direct: *The children said, "We are tired."*
Reported: *The children said that **they** were tired.*

## Rule 3: Time and place words change

Words tied to the moment of speaking shift when you report later.

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| now | then |
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| yesterday | the previous day / the day before |
| next week | the following week |
| last week | the previous week |
| here | there |
| this | that |
| these | those |

### Example

Direct: *He said, "I will see you tomorrow."*
Reported: *He said that he would see me **the next day**.*

Direct: *She said, "I bought this yesterday."*
Reported: *She said that she had bought **that** **the previous day**.*

## Reporting questions

Questions become statements in reported speech. Two differences:

1. The question mark disappears.
2. The word order becomes subject-verb (not verb-subject).

### Yes / no questions

Use *if* or *whether*.

Direct: *He asked, "Are you ready?"*
Reported: *He asked **if** I was ready.*

Direct: *She asked, "Did you call him?"*
Reported: *She asked **whether** I had called him.*

### Wh-questions

Keep the question word (*who, what, where, when, why, how*).

Direct: *He asked, "Where do you live?"*
Reported: *He asked where I lived.*

Direct: *She asked, "Why are you late?"*
Reported: *She asked why I was late.*

Common mistake: keeping the question order.

- ✗ *He asked where do I live.*
- ✓ *He asked where I lived.*

## Reporting commands and requests

Use *to* + base verb. The reporting verb is often *told, asked, ordered, begged, requested.*

Direct: *The teacher said, "Open your books."*
Reported: *The teacher told us **to open** our books.*

Direct: *My mother said, "Please help me."*
Reported: *My mother asked me **to help** her.*

For negative commands, use *not to* + base verb.

Direct: *He said, "Don't be late."*
Reported: *He told me **not to be** late.*

## When tense does NOT shift

Two common exceptions:

### 1. Universal truths

If the reported statement is a fact that's still true, you can keep the present tense.

Direct: *The teacher said, "The earth moves around the sun."*
Reported: *The teacher said that the earth **moves** around the sun.* (Optional — you could also say *moved*.)

### 2. Reporting verb in present tense

If *say* / *says* is in the present tense, no tense shift happens.

Direct: *She says, "I am happy."*
Reported: *She says that she **is** happy.*

## Common Sri Lankan reported-speech mistakes

### Mistake 1: Keeping question order

- ✗ *He asked where am I going.*
- ✓ *He asked where I was going.*

### Mistake 2: Keeping "that" where it should be "if / whether"

- ✗ *She asked that I would come.*
- ✓ *She asked **if / whether** I would come.*

### Mistake 3: Forgetting to shift pronouns

- ✗ *He said that I am tired.* (Where "I" refers to him.)
- ✓ *He said that **he** was tired.*

### Mistake 4: Double-shifting past perfect

Past perfect doesn't shift back — it's already as far back as it goes.

- Direct: *She said, "I had finished my work."*
- Reported: *She said that she had finished her work.* (Not *had had*.)

### Mistake 5: Using "said" for everything

Vary reporting verbs: *told, asked, explained, replied, mentioned, admitted, warned, promised.* Using the right one lifts your writing.

Note: *told* needs an object. *Said* doesn't.

- ✓ She **said** that she was happy.
- ✓ She **told me** that she was happy.
- ✗ She told that she was happy.

## Quick practice

Convert these to reported speech. Reporting verb in past tense.

1. He said, "I am watching a movie."
2. She asked, "Where are you going?"
3. They said, "We will come tomorrow."
4. The teacher said, "Don't talk during the exam."
5. "Have you eaten?" she asked.
6. My father said, "I bought this car last year."

Answers:

1. He said that he was watching a movie.
2. She asked where I was going.
3. They said that they would come the next day.
4. The teacher told us not to talk during the exam.
5. She asked if I had eaten.
6. My father said that he had bought that car the previous year.

## Where reported speech shows up

### O/L English

Paper II grammar section — a transformation of 5–10 marks that appears almost every year. Fast marks if you know the rules.

### A/L General English

Shorter but similar — usually one or two questions in the grammar section.

### IELTS Writing

Not tested directly, but appears constantly in academic writing. "The author argues that..." is a form of reporting.

### Everyday English

Any time you tell someone what someone else said. This is one of the most-used structures in daily life.

## How to drill reported speech

- **Rewrite dialogues from short stories** in reported form.
- **Report conversations from your day** at night — out loud or in a notebook.
- **Watch a short film clip, pause after each line**, and report what the character just said.

Fifteen minutes a day for two weeks and this stops feeling like grammar — it becomes a habit.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our O/L English programme covers reported speech through writing and spoken reporting, not just gap-fills. By the time the exam comes, students handle the transformation question in under five minutes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is reported speech always needed in formal writing?**

No. You can also use direct quotes in formal writing, especially for reports, journalism, and academic papers. Reported speech is for summarising what someone said.

**Can I ever skip the tense shift?**

Yes — for universal truths and for present-tense reporting verbs. Otherwise, the shift is expected in formal writing.

**Why is *told* different from *said*?**

*Tell* takes a direct object (*told me, told her, told the class*). *Say* doesn't (*said that, said quietly*). This is one of the most common mix-ups in O/L writing.

**Does reported speech appear in IELTS?**

Directly, no. Indirectly, yes — you'll use it in Writing Task 2 whenever you paraphrase or discuss ideas. Learning it well helps your academic writing.

# 50 Business English Words Every Sri Lankan Office Worker Needs

If you work in an office in Sri Lanka — a bank, a BPO, a tech firm, a hotel, a shipping company — the English you need is a specific flavour. Not literary English. Not IELTS English. Workplace English, which rewards precision and confidence over fancy vocabulary.

Here are 50 words and phrases that come up in Sri Lankan offices every day, grouped by situation. Pick the ones you don't use yet and build them into your emails and meetings this week.

## In a meeting

1. **Agenda** — the list of topics for a meeting. *"What's on today's agenda?"*
2. **Minutes** — the written record of a meeting. *"Who's taking the minutes?"*
3. **Action item** — a specific task assigned in a meeting. *"That's an action item for Nimal."*
4. **Follow up** — contact again later to check progress. *"I'll follow up with the client on Monday."*

10. **Pushback** — resistance or objection. *"Expect some pushback from the vendors."*

22. **Scope** — what the project covers. *"That's outside the scope of this project."*
23. **Budget** — the money allocated. *"We're over budget this quarter."*
24. **Forecast** — a prediction of future performance. *"The sales forecast looks strong."*
25. **Rollout** — launching or releasing something. *"The rollout is scheduled for January."*
26. **Pilot** — a small test before full launch. *"We'll pilot this with ten customers first."*
27. **Stakeholder** — anyone affected by the project. *"Let's get all stakeholders in one room."*

Many Sri Lankan offices use "I will revert to you" to mean "I will reply." *Revert* actually means "go back to a previous state." In modern international English, use **reply**, **respond**, or **get back to you** instead. *Revert* for reply is still common in South Asian English but sounds odd elsewhere.

# Common English Mistakes Sri Lankans Make (and How to Fix Them)

Every English speaker makes mistakes. The ones below are specific to Sri Lankan learners — not because our English is worse, but because our first languages (Sinhala and Tamil) work differently, and some errors get reinforced in local classrooms until they feel right.

Here are the 25 we hear most often, with the fix for each.

## Vocabulary and word-choice errors

### 1. *Revert back*

**Wrong:** *I will revert back to you tomorrow.*
**Right:** *I will get back to you tomorrow.* / *I will reply tomorrow.*

*Revert* is already dated usage for "reply" in South Asian English. *Revert back* doubles up — you only ever revert to something, not back to it.

### 2. *Do the needful*

**Wrong:** *Please do the needful.*
**Right:** *Please take the necessary action.* / *Please handle this.*

Old colonial phrase. Fine in some Sri Lankan offices, odd in international contexts.

### 3. *Prepone*

**Wrong:** *Can we prepone the meeting?*
**Right:** *Can we move the meeting earlier?* / *Can we bring the meeting forward?*

*Prepone* isn't standard international English. The opposite of *postpone* in English is "move earlier" or "bring forward."

### 4. *Pass out* (meaning graduate)

**Wrong:** *I passed out from Richmond College.*
**Right:** *I graduated from Richmond College.*

In international English, *pass out* means to faint. You don't want your CV saying you fainted from your school.

### 5. *Myself [Name]*

**Wrong:** *Myself Kasun.*
**Right:** *I'm Kasun.* / *My name is Kasun.*

### 6. *Cousin brother / cousin sister*

**Wrong:** *He's my cousin brother.*
**Right:** *He's my cousin.*

In English, *cousin* already means the child of your uncle or aunt — no need to add brother or sister.

### 7. *Real brother*

**Wrong:** *That's my real brother.*
**Right:** *That's my brother.* (Or *my biological brother* if the context needs that distinction.)

### 8. *Native place*

**Wrong:** *What is your native place?*
**Right:** *Where are you from?* / *Where's your hometown?*

### 9. *Out of station*

**Wrong:** *He's out of station this week.*
**Right:** *He's out of town this week.* / *He's away on a trip.*

### 10. *Discuss about*

**Wrong:** *We discussed about the issue.*
**Right:** *We discussed the issue.*

*Discuss* doesn't take *about*. It already means "talk about."

## Preposition errors

### 11. *Listen music*

**Wrong:** *I like to listen music.*
**Right:** *I like to listen to music.*

### 12. *Married with*

**Wrong:** *She is married with Kasun.*
**Right:** *She is married to Kasun.*

### 13. *Depend of*

**Wrong:** *It depends of the weather.*
**Right:** *It depends on the weather.*

### 14. *Different than*

**Wrong (BrE):** *This is different than that.*
**Right:** *This is different from that.*

### 15. *Good in* (meaning skilled)

**Wrong:** *She is good in English.*
**Right:** *She is good at English.*

(*Good in* works for subjects you study — "She is good in school" — but for a skill, use *good at*.)

## Tense and verb errors

### 16. *I am having two brothers*

**Wrong:** *I am having two brothers.*
**Right:** *I have two brothers.*

Verbs of possession (*have, own, belong, know, understand, love*) don't usually take the continuous (-ing) form.

### 17. Present perfect with "yesterday"

**Wrong:** *I have gone to Galle yesterday.*
**Right:** *I went to Galle yesterday.*

If there's a specific past time, use past simple. See our [full present perfect vs past simple guide](https://woodberryplace.com/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/).

### 18. *What is the time?*

**Wrong (grammatically fine, culturally odd):** *What is the time?*
**Right / more natural:** *What's the time?* / *Do you have the time?*

Contracted versions sound more natural in speech.

### 19. *Doubt* for a question

**Wrong:** *I have a doubt about the homework.*
**Right:** *I have a question about the homework.*

In English, *doubt* means uncertainty or suspicion, not a question.

### 20. *Open the light*

**Wrong:** *Can you open the light?*
**Right:** *Can you turn on the light?* / *Can you switch on the light?*

Lights get turned on or switched on, not opened. Same for the TV, radio, and fan.

## Article errors

### 21. Missing *the* before unique things

**Wrong:** *Sun rises in east.*
**Right:** *The sun rises in the east.*

### 22. Using *the* with general plurals

**Wrong:** *The children should respect the elders.* (If meant generally.)
**Right:** *Children should respect elders.*

Use *the* only when referring to specific children or specific elders.

### 23. Missing *a* before a job

**Wrong:** *He is engineer.*
**Right:** *He is an engineer.*

## Pronunciation errors that affect writing

### 24. *Then* vs *than*

**Wrong:** *She is taller then me.*
**Right:** *She is taller than me.*

*Than* is for comparisons. *Then* is for time or sequence.

### 25. *Their / there / they're*

**Wrong:** *Their going to the shop.*
**Right:** *They're going to the shop.*

- *Their* = belongs to them.
- *There* = that place.
- *They're* = they are.

These three sound identical in speech. Writing mixes them up.

## Culture-specific politeness

Some Sri Lankan politeness patterns translate strangely into English.

### "Please come, come."

Fine in Sinhala or Tamil. In English, saying "come, come" sounds like you're hurrying someone. Just *"please come in"* is clearer.

### "Yes, yes."

Repetition is common in South Asian speech. Native English speakers may read a double "yes" as impatience. One is usually enough.

### Formal titles

In Sri Lanka, calling a superior *"Sir"* even outside work is common. In international English, *"Sir"* can sound stiff or sarcastic. First names after an introduction are usually fine.

## How to actually fix these

### Method 1: Catch yourself in writing

Every time you write an email or message, scan for the ones above. Fix any you find. Over three months, the patterns rewire.

### Method 2: Read your emails out loud

Many errors sound off when spoken. "I have gone yesterday" sounds wrong immediately. Reading silently misses them.

### Method 3: Ask for honest corrections

If you have a colleague or friend whose English is strong, ask them to flag three errors a week. Honest corrections, not polite silence, are how learners improve.

### Method 4: Keep a personal error list

Write down each mistake you make. After a few weeks you'll see which ones repeat. Focus on those.

## What Sri Lankan English gets right

Before this list sounds too critical: Sri Lankan English is a real variety of English with its own history and strengths. Clear pronunciation, generous use of politeness markers, and strong grammar instruction in schools all travel well. The mistakes above are small corrections, not an indictment.

Many international businesses actively value Sri Lankan English because it's comprehensible across accents and tends to be grammatically careful.

Our Spoken English and Business English classes flag these specific Sri Lankan patterns and replace them with international alternatives through practice, not lectures. By the end of a term, most students stop making the top five errors above entirely.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do so many Sri Lankans make the same mistakes?**

Most come from direct translation from Sinhala or Tamil, or from older colonial English that got taught in schools and stuck. They're not random — they follow patterns.

**Is Sri Lankan English wrong?**

No. It's a variety of English, like Indian English, American English, or Australian English. In local contexts, Sri Lankan English works perfectly. For international work, some phrases are worth adjusting.

**How quickly can I fix these mistakes?**

Fast — most take weeks, not years. The key is noticing them. Once you know what to look for, your brain catches them automatically.

**Which of these are most important to fix first?**

For CVs and job interviews: *pass out*, *prepone*, *do the needful*. For emails: *revert back*, *discuss about*. For speaking: *myself [name]*, *open the light*.

# Phrasal Verbs for Sri Lankan Learners: The Top 30

Phrasal verbs are the single biggest reason Sri Lankan English learners sound textbook-y instead of natural. We learn *delay* but not *put off*. We learn *invent* but not *come up with*. We learn *investigate* but not *look into*. Native speakers reach for the phrasal verb almost every time.

Here are the 30 you'll hear most — grouped by situation, with examples and the ones Sri Lankan learners usually mix up.

## Why phrasal verbs feel difficult

A phrasal verb is a verb + preposition or particle (*look up, put off, give in*) that together mean something you can't always guess from the parts. *Give up* has nothing to do with giving. *Put off* has nothing to do with putting.

That's the challenge: you have to learn each one as a single unit, like a word. The payoff is huge — once you have 30 or 40 common ones, your English sounds much more natural.

## Daily life (10)

### 1. **Get up** — leave bed

*"I get up at 6 every morning."*

### 2. **Wake up** — stop sleeping

*"I wake up before my alarm these days."*

Note: *wake up* is the moment your eyes open. *Get up* is the moment you leave bed.

### 3. **Turn on / off** — start / stop a machine

*"Turn off the fan before you leave."*

(Not *open* and *close* — that's a common Sri Lankan mix-up.)

### 4. **Switch on / off** — same as turn on / off

*"Please switch off the lights."*

### 5. **Pick up** — collect, lift, or learn

- *"I'll pick up the children at 4pm."*
- *"She picked up French in Paris."*

### 6. **Drop off** — leave someone or something somewhere

*"Can you drop me off at the station?"*

### 7. **Run out of** — use up the supply

*"We've run out of milk."*

### 8. **Fill up** — fill completely

*"I need to fill up the car."*

### 9. **Take off** — remove (clothes) or begin flight

- *"Please take off your shoes."*
- *"The plane takes off at 10."*

### 10. **Put on** — wear

*"Put on your jacket — it's cold."*

## Work and study (10)

### 11. **Look up** — find information

*"I'll look up the meaning in the dictionary."*

### 12. **Look into** — investigate

*"The manager is looking into the complaint."*

### 13. **Figure out** — understand or solve

*"I can't figure out this problem."*

### 14. **Come up with** — invent, generate an idea

*"She came up with a great idea for the project."*

### 15. **Put off** — postpone

*"Let's put off the meeting until next week."*

(Not *postpone the meeting until next week to postpone* — just *put off*.)

### 16. **Carry out** — perform, execute

*"The team carried out the survey."*

### 17. **Set up** — establish, arrange

*"They set up a new office in Matara."*

### 18. **Hand in** — submit

*"Hand in your homework by Friday."*

### 19. **Go over** — review

*"Let's go over the notes before the exam."*

### 20. **Work out** — solve, exercise, or end well

- *"We worked out the answer together."*
- *"I work out at the gym three times a week."*
- *"Things worked out in the end."*

## Social and emotional (10)

### 21. **Get on with** — have a good relationship

*"I get on well with my cousins."*

(Not *get on well with my brother for a long time* with that specific tense pattern — but the phrase is standard.)

### 22. **Fall out with** — have an argument

*"They've fallen out with each other."*

### 23. **Make up** — reconcile, or invent (a story)

- *"The siblings made up after a week."*
- *"He made up an excuse for being late."*

### 24. **Catch up** — reach the same level, or hear news

- *"I missed a class — I need to catch up."*
- *"Let's catch up over coffee."*

### 25. **Hang out** — spend time casually

*"We hang out at the beach every weekend."*

### 26. **Bring up** — raise a child, or raise a topic

- *"She was brought up in Galle."*
- *"He brought up an interesting point in the meeting."*

### 27. **Calm down** — become less angry

*"Please calm down and tell me what happened."*

### 28. **Cheer up** — become happier

*"Cheer up — tomorrow's another day."*

### 29. **Give up** — stop trying

*"Don't give up — you're close."*

### 30. **Look forward to** — await happily

*"I'm looking forward to the holidays."*

(Note: *look forward to* + -ing form. "I'm looking forward to **seeing** you," not "to **see** you.")

## Transitive vs intransitive — quick note

- **Transitive** phrasal verbs take an object. *Pick up the keys.*
- **Intransitive** phrasal verbs don't. *The plane took off.*

For many transitive phrasal verbs, you can split them with the object:

- *Pick up the keys* / *Pick the keys up.* Both work.
- With pronouns, you **must** split: *Pick them up.* (Not *pick up them.*)

## Sri Lankan learner mistakes with phrasal verbs

### 1. Translating word by word

*"Get up"* becomes *"get upwards"* or *"rise upwards"* in direct translation. Learn the phrasal verb as one unit.

### 2. Missing the particle

Dropping *up, off, out, in* is common when speaking fast.

- ✗ "I'll pick you from the airport."
- ✓ "I'll pick you **up** from the airport."

### 3. Using the wrong particle

- ✗ "Switch on from the light."
- ✓ "Switch **on** the light." (No *from*.)

### 4. Over-formalising to avoid phrasal verbs

Some learners use *"investigate"* when *"look into"* would sound more natural. For speech and emails, the phrasal verb usually fits better.

### 5. *Pass out* for graduate

- ✗ "I passed out from Mahinda College."
- ✓ "I graduated from Mahinda College."

In international English, *pass out* means to faint.

## How to actually learn phrasal verbs

### Method 1: Learn 5 a week, use them

Pick 5 from the list above. Use each in a real sentence this week — text, email, or conversation. Next week, 5 more. Twelve weeks and you have 60 phrasal verbs you actually use.

### Method 2: Watch and notice

English TV shows and films are thick with phrasal verbs. Pick one episode a week. Write down every phrasal verb you hear. Look up any you don't know. 15 minutes.

### Method 3: Replace one formal word per sentence

When you write, scan for formal-sounding words. Many have a natural phrasal-verb replacement.

- *"We will investigate the issue."* → *"We'll look into the issue."*
- *"She proposed a new idea."* → *"She came up with a new idea."*
- *"I will submit the report by Friday."* → *"I'll hand in the report by Friday."*

### Method 4: Avoid memorisation lists

Lists of 500 phrasal verbs are the single worst way to learn them. The brain forgets faster than it learns from a list. Context sticks.

## Where phrasal verbs show up

### In IELTS Speaking

Using natural phrasal verbs lifts your Lexical Resource band. "I pick up new words quickly" sounds more natural than "I acquire new vocabulary quickly."

### In job interviews

Phrasal verbs make you sound less rehearsed, more conversational. "I handled the pushback" sounds stronger than "I managed the resistance."

### In O/L and A/L English

Appear in comprehension, vocabulary, and writing sections. Students who recognise them score better.

### In everyday life

If you work in tourism, hospitality, or any customer-facing role, phrasal verbs are what guests speak.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our Spoken English and IELTS classes build phrasal verbs into every lesson through conversation practice, not through list memorisation. Students who stick with a class for a term typically add 40–60 active phrasal verbs to their everyday English.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are phrasal verbs informal?**

Some are, some aren't. *Pick up, look into, carry out, work out, hand in* are fine in formal writing. *Hang out, chill out, mess up* are more casual.

**Can I avoid phrasal verbs completely?**

In writing, yes. In speaking, no — you'll sound unnatural without them. English speakers reach for the phrasal verb in most conversations.

**Why do phrasal verbs have multiple meanings?**

Many developed over centuries with slight shifts in meaning. *Make up* can mean reconcile, invent, or apply makeup — three different meanings in different contexts.

**Which are the most important to learn first?**

The 30 above cover most daily situations. After that, work-specific phrasal verbs for your industry (*follow up, touch base, reach out* for office work) come next.

# English Idioms You'll Actually Hear in Sri Lanka

Most idiom lists online include phrases nobody actually says. *"Bob's your uncle"*? *"It's raining cats and dogs"*? Not useful. This list is different — 25 idioms you'll genuinely hear in Sri Lankan English, workplaces, and conversations with visitors.

## What idioms are, and why they matter

An idiom is a phrase whose meaning isn't obvious from its parts. *Break a leg* means "good luck." *Hit the roof* means "get very angry." You can't translate word by word.

Learning common idioms does three things:

- Makes your English sound natural.
- Helps you understand films, TV, and native speakers.
- Avoids confusion when someone uses one with you.

## Common idioms used in Sri Lanka

### 1. **A piece of cake** — very easy

*"The exam was a piece of cake."*

### 2. **Hit the books** — study hard

*"IELTS is next month — time to hit the books."*

### 3. **Burn the midnight oil** — work late into the night

*"She's been burning the midnight oil before the presentation."*

### 4. **Call it a day** — stop working for the day

*"Let's call it a day — we've done enough."*

### 5. **Hit the nail on the head** — exactly right

*"You hit the nail on the head — that's the real problem."*

### 6. **Pull someone's leg** — joke with them

*"I was just pulling your leg — don't take it seriously."*

### 7. **Under the weather** — feeling unwell

*"I'm feeling a bit under the weather today."*

### 8. **Butter someone up** — flatter to get a favour

*"He's just buttering up the boss for a promotion."*

### 9. **Spill the beans** — reveal a secret

*"Don't spill the beans about the surprise party."*

### 10. **Break the ice** — start conversation in a new group

*"I told a joke to break the ice at the meeting."*

### 11. **Cost an arm and a leg** — very expensive

*"That phone costs an arm and a leg."*

### 12. **In the same boat** — in the same situation

*"We're all in the same boat — nobody understood the lecture."*

### 13. **Let the cat out of the bag** — accidentally reveal

*"He let the cat out of the bag about the new office."*

### 14. **Cut corners** — do something cheaply or poorly

*"Don't cut corners on the foundation — it'll cost you later."*

### 15. **Bite the bullet** — do something unpleasant that must be done

*"I'll have to bite the bullet and tell him the news."*

### 16. **Get cold feet** — lose courage at the last minute

*"He got cold feet and didn't go to the interview."*

### 17. **Once in a blue moon** — very rarely

*"We see each other once in a blue moon these days."*

### 18. **On the same page** — in agreement

*"Let's make sure we're on the same page before the meeting."*

### 19. **Kill two birds with one stone** — do two things at once

*"I kill two birds with one stone by jogging while listening to lectures."*

### 20. **Miss the boat** — miss an opportunity

*"If you don't apply now, you'll miss the boat."*

### 21. **See eye to eye** — agree with someone

*"My brother and I don't see eye to eye on politics."*

### 22. **Break even** — neither profit nor loss

*"The business is just breaking even now."*

### 23. **Keep an eye on** — watch carefully

*"Keep an eye on the pot — the rice is almost done."*

### 24. **Ring a bell** — sound familiar

*"That name rings a bell, but I can't place it."*

### 25. **Take it with a grain of salt** — don't fully believe

*"Take what he says with a grain of salt — he exaggerates."*

## Idioms that ARE used in Sri Lanka but sound outdated

Some idioms were common in colonial English and stuck around in Sri Lanka longer than elsewhere. They're not wrong, just dated:

- *"At the eleventh hour"* — at the last minute. Still used, but "at the last minute" is more natural.
- *"Beat about the bush"* — avoid the main point. Fine, but slightly dated in modern speech.
- *"Gentle as a lamb"* — very gentle. Still understood, but sounds literary.

Use these if you like — they're correct — but newer alternatives sound more current.

## Idioms NOT to use in Sri Lanka's professional English

Some idioms are regional (American or British specifically) and will confuse Sri Lankan listeners or readers:

- *"Hit a home run"* — American baseball reference. Not widely understood here.
- *"Bob's your uncle"* — very British, sounds old-fashioned.
- *"The whole nine yards"* — American, meaning unclear without context.
- *"The ball is in your court"* — fine, widely understood.

Rule of thumb: if you had to pause and explain the idiom to a Sri Lankan colleague, it's probably not the right one for a local email.

## Where to use idioms — and where to avoid them

### Use them in

- Casual conversation.
- Informal emails to colleagues.
- Social media.
- IELTS Speaking (lifts your Lexical Resource — but only use ones you're comfortable with).

### Avoid them in

- Formal academic writing.
- IELTS Writing Task 2 (overusing idioms can lower your band).
- Business emails to clients you don't know well.
- Legal or technical documents.
- Any communication with non-native English speakers who may not recognise them.

## How to learn idioms naturally

### Method 1: Context first, meaning second

If you read an idiom in a story and guess the meaning from context, it sticks. If you memorise it from a list, it doesn't.

### Method 2: Three a week, used three times

Pick three idioms. Use each one three times in different contexts over a week. The ones that feel natural stay. The others drop away.

### Method 3: Films and TV

English-language shows are packed with idioms. Pick a 30-minute episode. Note any idiom you hear. Look up the ones you don't know. One show a week builds a serious collection over months.

### Method 4: Don't force them

Nothing sounds worse than an idiom used wrong. Better to leave it out than to say "it's raining cats and horses" or "cost an arm and a tooth."

## Idioms in IELTS Speaking

Band 7+ Speaking rewards idiomatic expressions — but only when used accurately. Two or three well-placed idioms in a Speaking test lift your band. Six awkward ones drop it.

Safer bets for IELTS:

- *"A piece of cake"*
- *"On the same page"*
- *"Keep an eye on"*
- *"Ring a bell"*
- *"Once in a blue moon"*

These are common, clear, and hard to use wrong.

Our Spoken English and IELTS classes build idioms into conversation practice gradually. Students pick them up in context, not through list memorisation — which is the only way they actually stick.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are idioms the same in all varieties of English?**

No. Many are shared, but some are specific to British, American, Australian, or Indian English. Stick to idioms you've actually heard used in Sri Lanka or widely in international English.

**Can I use Sinhala or Tamil idioms translated into English?**

Usually no. Idioms don't translate — they confuse. A literal translation of *"ඇට්ට ළඟ රත්තරං"* makes no sense in English.

**How many idioms do I need to know?**

For comfortable everyday fluency, about 50. For strong IELTS speaking, around 30 used accurately.

**Do idioms help with reading comprehension?**

Yes — a lot. Newspaper headlines and editorials rely on idioms heavily. Knowing the common ones unlocks faster reading.

# Collocations: Why "Fast Food" Not "Quick Food"

We say *fast food*, not *quick food*. We say *heavy rain*, not *strong rain*. *Make a mistake*, not *do a mistake*. There's no grammar rule for this — the words just go together. Native speakers know these pairs by feel. Learners don't, and that's the biggest reason intermediate English can sound "off" even when the grammar is perfect.

These word pairings are called **collocations**. Here's what they are, why they matter, and the ones Sri Lankan learners most need to know.

## What a collocation is

A collocation is a pair of words that English speakers habitually use together. There's often a less-common alternative that means the same thing — but native speakers never use it.

- ✓ *Make a decision* ✗ *Do a decision*
- ✓ *Heavy traffic* ✗ *Strong traffic*
- ✓ *Fast food* ✗ *Quick food*
- ✓ *Take a shower* ✗ *Make a shower*
- ✓ *High expectations* ✗ *Big expectations*

Grammar is correct in all the wrong versions. They still sound wrong — because collocations aren't about grammar.

## Why collocations matter more than you think

### 1. They're the biggest marker of natural English

You can have perfect grammar and still sound foreign because your word pairings are off. Fix your collocations and your English suddenly sounds fluent, even if nothing else changes.

### 2. They matter for IELTS

The Writing and Speaking bands mark *Lexical Resource* — and collocation accuracy is a big part of that. Band 7 requires you to use collocations naturally.

### 3. They matter for business English

*Reach a decision, meet a deadline, raise an issue, break a promise.* These are the default pairs in office English. Using the wrong one marks you as a non-native speaker.

### 4. They matter for reading speed

When you know collocations, your eye glides over the familiar pair instead of processing each word. Reading speeds up.

## The high-frequency collocations every learner needs

### Make vs Do (the biggest trap)

*Make* and *do* have no clear rule — you memorise which goes with which.

**Make:**
- make a decision, make a mistake, make a plan, make an effort, make progress, make noise, make friends, make money, make breakfast / lunch / dinner, make a call

**Do:**
- do homework, do the dishes, do business, do a favour, do your best, do exercise, do research, do the shopping, do a course

### Take

- take a shower, take a bath, take a break, take a nap, take notes, take a photo, take a look, take a chance, take advice, take medicine, take an exam

### Have

- have breakfast / lunch / dinner, have a meeting, have a shower (also acceptable), have a look, have a chance, have fun, have a problem, have a question

### Get

- get married, get divorced, get lost, get angry, get better, get ready, get started, get a job, get a message

### Weather

- heavy rain, strong wind, bright sunshine, thick fog, deep snow, extreme heat

### Traffic and travel

- heavy traffic, light traffic, a long journey, a short flight, a direct flight, a connecting flight, miss the bus, catch the train

### Money

- save money, spend money, earn money, lose money, waste money, borrow money, lend money, pay cash, open an account, close an account

### Work and study

- attend a meeting, miss a class, submit a report, meet a deadline, raise an issue, solve a problem, reach a conclusion, give a presentation, take a course

### Emotions

- feel tired, feel happy, feel sick, strong feelings, deep regret, mixed feelings, a quick temper

### Health

- catch a cold, have a headache, take medicine, see a doctor, make an appointment, strict diet, serious illness

## Collocations that trip up Sri Lankan learners

### 1. *Make vs do mistake*

- ✓ Make a mistake.
- ✗ Do a mistake.

### 2. *Say vs tell*

- ✓ Say something to someone / Tell someone something.
- *Say* takes the thing said. *Tell* takes the person.

### 3. *Watch vs see vs look at*

- Watch a movie, a match, TV. (Follow something that moves over time.)
- See a friend, a doctor, a film. (Meet or witness.)
- Look at a picture, the sky. (Direct attention.)

### 4. *Listen vs hear*

- Listen to music. (Active.)
- Hear a noise. (Happens to you.)

### 5. *Bring vs take*

- Bring = towards the speaker. (*Bring the book to me.*)
- Take = away from the speaker. (*Take the book to him.*)

### 6. *Lend vs borrow*

- Lend = give for a short time. (*I can lend you a pen.*)
- Borrow = receive for a short time. (*Can I borrow a pen?*)

Note the direction. "Can you borrow me a pen?" is wrong — should be "lend me."

## How to learn collocations

### Method 1: Chunks, not words

Stop learning single words. Learn phrases. Not *decide* — learn *make a decision* and *reach a decision*. Not *problem* — learn *solve a problem* and *face a problem*.

### Method 2: Collocation notebooks

Keep a small notebook. When you see a strong-sounding pair in reading, write it down with its context.

- "She made a quick decision."
- "They faced a serious problem."
- "The company reached an agreement."

A few weeks of this builds a personal library of natural phrases.

### Method 3: Replace weak verbs

Scan your writing for weak verbs like *do, get, have, make, say, go, give*. Ask if there's a more precise collocation.

- "She did good at the exam." → "She did well in the exam." → better: "She performed well in the exam."
- "He got the award." → "He won the award."
- "They had a party." → "They threw a party." / "They held a party."

### Method 4: Read widely

Collocations are everywhere in English news, novels, and business writing. 15 minutes of reading a day is the easiest collocation learning there is.

## Where collocations show up in exams

### IELTS

Writing: *reach a conclusion, raise a concern, address an issue, take measures, implement a policy* — all academic collocations that lift your band.

Speaking: *make a difference, keep in touch, take a chance, have a point* — conversational collocations that mark natural fluency.

### O/L and A/L English

Collocations show up in gap-fills, writing, and dialogue questions. Students who've read widely do better here almost automatically.

### Business English

The collocations above (*reach a decision, meet a deadline, raise an issue*) are the vocabulary of professional English. Learn them before the fancier words.

## A quick collocation self-test

Pick the natural pairing:

1. (make / do) a mistake
2. (strong / heavy) rain
3. (fast / quick) food
4. (take / have) a photo
5. (say / tell) someone a secret
6. (borrow / lend) me 500 rupees
7. (catch / get) a cold
8. (make / do) homework
9. (strong / big) coffee
10. (ride / drive) a bicycle

Answers: 1. make 2. heavy 3. fast 4. take 5. tell 6. lend 7. catch 8. do 9. strong 10. ride (a bicycle). Drive is for cars.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a rule for which words collocate?**

Mostly no. A few patterns exist (strong + nouns, heavy + weather), but the majority are memory. The good news: the most important collocations are a manageable list, maybe 300–500.

**Which collocations should I learn first?**

The *make / do / take / have / get* collocations above. They cover a huge chunk of daily English.

**Do collocations differ between British and American English?**

Slightly. *Take a shower* (American) vs *have a shower* (British, though both are fine now). *Do the dishes* vs *wash up*. Mostly minor.

**Can I translate collocations from Sinhala or Tamil?**

Usually no. Collocations are language-specific. A literal translation almost always sounds wrong in English.

# The English Sounds Sri Lankan Speakers Struggle With

Sri Lankan English is clear — mostly. But a handful of specific sounds give almost every learner trouble, and they're the same sounds whether you're from Jaffna, Colombo, or Matara. These aren't accent issues (an accent is fine and normal). They're sounds that can make the difference between being understood and being asked to repeat yourself.

Here's which sounds, why they're hard, and how to fix each one.

## Why some sounds are hard for us

Sinhala and Tamil have their own sound systems. English has sounds that don't exist in either, or that exist in slightly different versions. When you speak a new language, your mouth defaults to the closest sound from your first language — usually not the exact right one.

Three things fix this:

1. **Hearing the difference first.** You can't make a sound you can't hear.
2. **Feeling where your tongue and lips go.** English sounds require specific positions.
3. **Slow practice, then fast practice.** Slow builds the muscle memory. Fast builds the natural flow.

## Sound 1: V vs W

The classic. *Van* and *wan* sound identical to many Sri Lankan speakers.

**V** — upper teeth touch the lower lip. Air escapes with a buzz. *Van, vote, very, village, voice.*

**W** — lips round into a small circle. No teeth touch. *Want, wait, water, world, wise.*

### Practice pairs

- *vine / wine*
- *vet / wet*
- *very / wary*
- *vest / west*
- *vine / whine*

Say each pair out loud. Touch your lower lip with your upper teeth for V, then round your lips for W. Feel the switch. Record yourself.

## Sound 2: TH

Two TH sounds — both tricky.

### Voiceless TH

Tongue tip touches the back of the upper teeth. Air flows past. No voice.

*Think, thank, thin, thing, thought, through.*

### Voiced TH

Same tongue position, but your voice is on.

*This, that, they, there, them, those, mother, father.*

Most Sri Lankan speakers replace TH with D or T.

- *Think* → "tink"
- *This* → "dis"
- *Thank you* → "tank you"

The fix: stick your tongue slightly between your teeth. Yes, it feels silly. That's where TH lives.

### Practice

- *this that these those* — a daily mantra.
- *Think of three things.*
- *The thirty-third thing.*

Daily. For two weeks.

## Sound 3: P vs F

Most Sri Lankan speakers handle P and F fine, but there's one trap:

**P** at the start of a word has a small puff of air in English. In Sinhala and Tamil, it doesn't.

*Pen* in English has a slight *puh* before the vowel. Without the puff, it sounds like *ben* to a native listener.

### Practice

Hold a thin piece of paper close to your mouth. Say *pen, pie, pot, pat*. The paper should move. If it doesn't, you're not aspirating.

## Sound 4: Short vs long vowels

English makes a meaningful difference between short and long vowels. Sinhala and Tamil don't always.

### Short I vs long EE

- *ship* (short) vs *sheep* (long)
- *live* (short) vs *leave* (long)
- *bit* (short) vs *beat* (long)

### Short A vs long E / UH

- *man* (short) vs *men* (short, different vowel)
- *bat* vs *bet* vs *but* — three different vowels, often collapsed into one by Sri Lankan speakers.

### Fix

Listen to recorded minimal pairs on YouTube — type "minimal pairs English pronunciation." Say each pair aloud. The difference is usually in how long you hold the sound and the position of your mouth.

## Sound 5: Word endings

Sri Lankan English often softens or drops final consonants:

- *walked* becomes "walk"
- *books* becomes "book"
- *fast* becomes "fas"

For listeners outside Sri Lanka, this is the single most common comprehension issue. They miss tense endings (*ed, s*) and lose track of what you're saying.

### Fix

Slow down. Over-pronounce final consonants when practising.

*"Yesterday I walkED to the shop and bought threeeeeeeee bookS."*

Exaggerate in practice. In real speech it'll settle at the right level.

## Sound 6: Stress on the wrong syllable

English puts stress on specific syllables in each word. Sri Lankan English sometimes stresses the wrong one.

- *develop* — stress on the second syllable (*de-VEL-op*), not the first.
- *photograph* — stress on the first (*PHO-to-graph*).
- *photographer* — stress on the second (*pho-TOG-ra-pher*).
- *economy* — stress on the second (*e-CON-o-my*).

Using the wrong stress can make a word unrecognisable even if every sound is right.

### Fix

Any online dictionary shows stress with a mark. Look up new words and check stress. After a few months this becomes automatic.

## Sound 7: R

Different versions of R exist across English. The safest is a soft, barely-there R — closer to British English than American.

Sri Lankan R is sometimes too strong (rolled) or too soft (dropped).

- *Water* — not "wataR" (strong) and not "wata" (dropped). Soft R at the end.
- *Red* — soft, not rolled.

For most Sri Lankan learners, aiming for standard British R is simplest. Lips slightly rounded, tongue not touching.

## Sound 8: Intonation

Not a sound exactly — the music of the sentence. Sri Lankan English can sound flat or sing-song to outside ears.

Key patterns:

- **Statements** fall at the end: *"I live in Matara."* (Voice drops on "Matara.")
- **Yes/no questions** rise at the end: *"Are you ready?"* (Voice rises on "ready.")
- **Wh- questions** fall at the end: *"Where do you live?"* (Voice drops on "live.")
- **Lists** rise on each item, fall on the last: *"I bought rice, dhal, vegetables, and fish."*

Record yourself asking three types of question. Listen. If every question sounds the same, work on this.

## How to practise pronunciation without a teacher

### Method 1: Shadow-reading

Pick a 1-minute English clip (a news reader is best — clear and slow). Listen once. Then play it again and read along out loud, matching the pace. Then try without looking. Do this once a day for 10 minutes.

### Method 2: Record and compare

Record yourself saying 5 sentences. Listen back. Compare with a native speaker version (YouTube, podcast, film clip). The gap is your work.

### Method 3: Minimal pairs

Find a minimal pairs list online (ship/sheep, bat/bet, live/leave). Work through 10 pairs a day until you can hear and say them accurately.

### Method 4: Mirror practice

Watch your mouth in a mirror while saying difficult sounds. For TH, you should see your tongue tip. For V, your top teeth should touch your lower lip. Visual feedback matters.

## What NOT to do

- **Don't try to lose your accent entirely.** It's unnecessary and almost impossible. Clear Sri Lankan English is a perfectly good target.
- **Don't mimic American accents from films.** Sounds forced and inconsistent when you're tired.
- **Don't speak too fast to "sound fluent."** Fast and unclear is worse than slower and precise.
- **Don't compare yourself to native speakers.** Compare yourself to where you were six months ago.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can adults change their pronunciation?**

Yes — just slower than children. Specific sounds can be fixed at any age with focused practice. Full accent change is much harder but also unnecessary.

**Should I learn American or British pronunciation?**

For Sri Lanka, British is slightly more common and consistent with what's taught in schools. For international business, either works — pick one and stick with it.

**Why do I understand English on TV but not in person?**

TV speakers slow down for the audience. Real speakers don't. Shadow-reading helps bridge this gap faster than passive listening.

**Is the accent I have now going to affect my IELTS Speaking score?**

Only if it affects comprehension. Band 7 rewards clarity, not accent reduction. A clear Sri Lankan accent regularly scores Band 7+ in Speaking.

# V and W in English: How to Tell Them Apart

Of all the English sounds Sri Lankan speakers mix up, V and W is the most famous. *Van* and *wan* sound identical. *Vote* and *wrote* come out the same. *Very well* turns into *wery vell*. The sounds exist in English as two very different things, but in Sinhala and Tamil the distinction isn't as sharp.

Here's how to tell them apart — and more importantly, how to say them so they're different.

## Why they get mixed up

In Sinhala and Tamil, there's a sound close to a soft V/W that sits somewhere between the two English sounds. Most Sri Lankans use this neutral sound for both, which is why *van* and *wan* come out identical.

In English, V and W are produced in completely different ways. Once you know what your mouth should do for each, the fix is mechanical.

## Making the V sound

**Mouth position:** your upper teeth gently touch your lower lip.

**Airflow:** air pushes between the teeth and lip, creating a buzzing sound.

**Voice:** on. V is a voiced consonant — your throat should vibrate.

**Try it:** put your finger on your throat and say *vvvvv* for five seconds. You should feel vibration. If not, you're saying F (voiceless), not V.

### V words to practise

- *van, very, voice, vote, visit, village, victory, violet*
- *river, over, seven, driver, oven, lover*
- *give, live, love, five, have, drive, arrive*

Say each word slowly. Touch your top teeth to your lower lip at the V. Feel the buzz.

## Making the W sound

**Mouth position:** round your lips into a small circle, like you're about to blow out a candle.

**Airflow:** air passes through the rounded lips smoothly.

**Voice:** on. W is also voiced.

**Teeth:** no teeth touching anywhere. This is the biggest difference.

**Try it:** round your lips, then say *wwwww* while sliding into the next sound. Your lips should start closed and small, then open as the word continues.

### W words to practise

- *want, wait, water, world, wise, winter, work, warm*
- *away, between, however, question, reward, sandwich*
- *wonder, window, woman, wedding, weather, wake*

Watch your lips in a mirror. They should round noticeably at the start of each word.

## The pair test

Say these pairs slowly, feeling the switch between teeth-on-lip (V) and rounded lips (W):

- vine / wine
- vet / wet
- vest / west
- very / wary
- veil / wail
- verse / worse
- vine / whine
- vow / wow
- vain / wane
- V (the letter) / we

Say each pair five times. Record yourself. Play it back.

## Common mistakes to watch for

### 1. Saying V with rounded lips

You've done half of each sound and neither correctly. If you're rounding your lips for V, it'll come out somewhere between V and W.

### 2. Saying W with teeth touching

Same problem the other way. W has no teeth contact.

### 3. Dropping the buzz

V without vibration becomes F. "Very" becomes "ferry." Keep the voice on.

### 4. Doing the same sound for both

The most common pattern. Both come out as a soft, weak version that confuses listeners. If you catch yourself, slow down and pronounce each one exaggeratedly.

## Daily 5-minute drill

Five minutes a day for two weeks fixes most V/W confusion.

- **Minute 1:** warm up with *vvvvv* (teeth on lip, buzzing) and *wwwww* (rounded lips, smooth).
- **Minute 2:** five V words, slowly. *Very, voice, visit, village, victory.*
- **Minute 3:** five W words, slowly. *Want, water, world, wonder, winter.*
- **Minute 4:** ten V/W pair sentences:
  - "I want very fresh water."
  - "We visited a lovely village."
  - "The winter was very cold."
  - "Vinod walked away with victory."
  - "Vinita worked very well."
  - "We voted on Wednesday."
  - "A woman in a velvet dress."
  - "I had a vision while waking up."
  - "Wild violets grew near the well."
  - "The wind was very wet."
- **Minute 5:** record yourself reading all ten. Listen back.

Do this for 10–14 days. Your mouth builds the muscle memory.

## Tongue twisters

Once the basics feel comfortable, try these:

- *Vincent vented his vexation very visibly.*
- *Wayne went west with wild wild weasels.*
- *Vicky visited Victor's village last weekend.*
- *Wally the walrus wears a very warm vest.*

These force your mouth to switch quickly between V and W positions, which builds real-time accuracy.

## When it matters most

- **Job interviews.** First impressions matter. "W/Very happy to meet you" that sounds like "wery" can distract the interviewer.
- **Phone calls.** Without visual cues, V/W confusion causes miscommunication. "Can I have your name?" "Vikram." "Wikram?"
- **Spelling words aloud.** "V as in Victor" or "V for Victory" works. Make the habit.
- **IELTS Speaking.** Not a huge band drag, but clear V/W pushes Pronunciation toward Band 7.

## What NOT to worry about

- **Accent.** Having a Sri Lankan accent is fine. Making V and W distinct is not about accent — it's about being understood.
- **Perfection.** Occasional slips are normal. Consistent confusion is what you're fixing.
- **Comparing to American or British speakers.** Many native speakers have regional variations. Your goal is clear distinction, not imitation.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is V/W so hard specifically for Sri Lankans?**

Because the sound in our first languages sits between the two English sounds. Our mouths default to the neutral version, which English treats as neither sound.

**Will fixing V/W change my whole accent?**

No. You'll still sound Sri Lankan. You'll just be clearly understood on V and W.

**How long until it becomes automatic?**

Two to four weeks of daily 5-minute practice for most adults. Children pick it up faster.

**Does this matter for writing?**

Writing, no — you're spelling the letters. For speaking, yes — enough to affect comprehension in professional and international contexts.

# How to Write a Professional Email in English (With Templates)

Most Sri Lankan office workers can write a serviceable email in English. The problem isn't grammar — it's structure, tone, and the specific phrases that make an email sound professional instead of awkward. Fix those and your emails get replies faster, misunderstandings drop, and you look more senior than you are.

Here's the full structure, with templates you can use this week.

## The five parts of a professional email

Every good business email has these five parts, in order:

1. **Subject line** — specific, short, actionable.
2. **Greeting** — appropriate to the relationship.
3. **Opening line** — purpose stated upfront.
4. **Body** — details, grouped logically.
5. **Closing** — next step + sign-off.

Miss any of these and the email feels off, even if the English is fine.

## Subject lines

The subject line decides whether your email gets read today or tomorrow. Three rules:

- **Specific, not vague.** "Meeting" tells the reader nothing. "Agenda for Thursday's Q3 review meeting" does.
- **Keyword at the front.** The reader may only see the first 30 characters.
- **If action is needed, say so.** "Signature needed: client contract" is clearer than "Contract."

### Good vs weak subject lines

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| Question | Question about the invoice format |
| Update | Project update — October delivery on track |
| Important | Urgent: Server downtime Friday 10pm–11pm |
| Meeting | Can we move Friday's meeting to 3pm? |
| Documents | Attached: Signed NDA for review |

## Greetings

Match the greeting to the relationship.

### Formal (first contact, external, senior)

- *Dear Mr Perera,*
- *Dear Ms Fernando,*
- *Dear Dr Jayawardena,*

Note: *Dear* in English email is fine for professional contexts — it's not overly affectionate.

### Semi-formal (internal, known contact)

- *Hi Chaminda,*
- *Hello Anoma,*

### Casual (close colleagues)

- *Hi Sampath,* (first name, no title)

### When you don't know the name

- *Dear Sir or Madam,* (very formal, old-fashioned)
- *Dear [Team name],* — e.g., *Dear Customer Service Team,*
- *Hello,* — modern, acceptable

Avoid: *Respected Sir* (too deferential for international business), *To whom it may concern* (feels cold and dated).

## Opening lines — state the purpose

The first sentence should say why you're writing. Readers skim; don't make them search.

### Good openers by purpose

- **Making a request:** *"Could you please share the sales report for Q3?"*
- **Replying to a question:** *"Thank you for your email. To answer your question..."*
- **Introducing yourself:** *"My name is Dinusha Perera from Woodberry Logistics. I'm writing because..."*
- **Following up:** *"I'm following up on my email from last Tuesday about the pricing proposal."*
- **Sending information:** *"Please find attached the signed agreement."*

Avoid: *"I hope this email finds you well"* as the only opening line. It's fine as a warm-up, but needs the purpose line right after.

## The body — group ideas

- **One topic per email** where possible. Two emails with one topic each beat one email with five.
- **Short paragraphs.** Three sentences max per paragraph. Readers scan.
- **Bullet points** for lists or action items. They're read, long paragraphs often aren't.
- **Bold** for the most important point if the email is longer. Don't overuse.

## Closings

The last sentence should say what happens next or what you need.

### If you need a reply

- *"Could you please confirm by Friday?"*
- *"Let me know if this works for you."*
- *"I'd appreciate your response by end of day Monday."*

### If no reply is needed

- *"No action needed — just sharing for your awareness."*
- *"Please let me know if you have any questions."*

### Sign-offs

| Level | Options |
|---|---|
| Formal | Yours sincerely, Yours faithfully |
| Semi-formal | Best regards, Kind regards, Regards |
| Casual | Thanks, Cheers, Best |

Then your name, your title, and your contact details (phone, company).

## Template 1: Making a request

> **Subject:** Approval needed: October training budget
>
> Hi [Manager's name],
>
> I'd like to request approval for the October training budget of Rs. 50,000 for the sales team's sales training workshop.
>
> Here's a quick summary:
>
> - Trainer: [name], [credentials]
> - Dates: 15–17 October
> - Attendees: 8 sales staff
> - Expected outcomes: [one-line result]
>
> I've attached the detailed proposal and three trainer quotes for your review. Could you please confirm by end of day Friday so I can lock in the booking?
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

## Template 2: Replying to a complaint

> **Subject:** Re: Order #4532 — we're sorry about the delay
>
> Dear Mr [Customer name],
>
> Thank you for reaching out, and I'm sorry for the delay with your order. I've looked into it, and the shipment was held at our warehouse due to a stock check. That's now resolved.
>
> Your order will be dispatched tomorrow morning and you'll receive tracking details by noon. As an apology for the delay, we'll also apply a 10% discount on your next order.
>
> Please let me know if there's anything else I can do.
>
> Kind regards,
> [Your name]

## Template 3: Following up without being pushy

> **Subject:** Following up: Proposal from 18 October
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I hope you're doing well. I'm following up on the proposal I sent on 18 October regarding [brief description]. I wanted to check if you had any questions or feedback before we move ahead.
>
> I'm happy to jump on a short call if that would be easier — I'm free Tuesday and Thursday afternoon.
>
> Looking forward to hearing from you.
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

## Template 4: Introducing yourself (cold email)

> **Subject:** Introduction from Woodberry Place — collaboration on English training
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> My name is [Your name] and I'm the [role] at Woodberry Place English Academy in Matara. [Name of mutual contact] suggested we connect regarding [specific topic].
>
> [One sentence about what you do.] [One sentence about why it's relevant to them.]
>
> Would you be open to a 15-minute call next week to explore whether this could be useful? I'm available Tuesday afternoon or Thursday morning.
>
> Thanks for your time.
>
> Kind regards,
> [Your name]

## Common Sri Lankan email mistakes

### 1. Overly formal openings

*"Respected Sir, With reference to the aforementioned subject..."* reads as colonial-era. Keep it current.

### 2. Burying the point

Starting with three paragraphs of context before stating what you want. Reverse the order — state the request, then explain.

### 3. Over-using *revert*

*"I will revert to you tomorrow."* Use *get back to you* or *reply* in international contexts.

### 4. Missing action step

Emails that end "Please find below for your information" without saying whether anything is needed from the reader. Always say what the next step is.

### 5. Too many thank yous

*"Thank you so much for your kind response and prompt attention to this matter."* Once is enough.

### 6. Inconsistent greetings and sign-offs

Starting with *Hi* and ending with *Yours faithfully* is a clash. Match the register — casual with casual, formal with formal.

## Tone: 5 phrases that lift your emails

- *"Thanks for getting back to me so quickly."* — acknowledges the reply.
- *"Just to confirm,..."* — before summarising what was agreed.
- *"Let me know if this makes sense."* — invites clarification without being pushy.
- *"Quick question — ..."* — frames a small ask politely.
- *"I appreciate your patience with this."* — graceful when you've delayed.

## Email etiquette basics

- **Check the recipient list** before hitting send.
- **Reply within 24 hours** on working days, even if just to acknowledge.
- **Use CC sparingly** — adding people who don't need to be there annoys everyone.
- **Avoid "reply all"** unless truly needed.
- **Proofread** — mobile autocorrect introduces errors in the wrong places.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

## Frequently asked questions

**Is *Dear* too old-fashioned for emails?**

No — *Dear [Name]* is still standard for professional emails. It's warmer than *Hello* but less casual than *Hi*.

**Should I CC my manager on every email?**

No. CC only when they need to see the content. Over-CCing creates inbox noise and signals a lack of confidence.

**How long should a professional email be?**

As short as the content allows — usually 3–8 short paragraphs. If it's longer than 300 words, consider a call or a document attachment instead.

**Can I use emojis in business emails?**

In casual internal emails with colleagues you know well, occasionally. In external or formal emails, no.

# Formal vs Informal Letter Writing for O/L English

The letter writing question in O/L English Paper II is usually the first thing students write — and often the first place they lose marks. Not because the English is poor, but because the format is wrong. Get the format right and you walk in with half the marks already in your pocket.

Here's the clear guide for both formal and informal letters, with examples and the exact mistakes to avoid.

## Why format matters so much

The O/L marking scheme splits the letter writing question into:

- **Format** — your address, the recipient's address, the salutation, the sign-off.
- **Content** — what you actually say.
- **Language** — grammar, spelling, vocabulary.

Content and language are what you spend time on, but format is where you earn the fast, easy marks. A well-formatted letter with average content scores higher than an excellent letter with wrong format.

## Formal letter format

Formal letters go to people you don't know personally or in an official capacity — the principal, the editor, the manager, a company.

### The structure (top to bottom)

1. **Your address** — top right corner. Three lines usually.
2. **Date** — below your address, right side.
3. **Recipient's name and address** — left side, below the date.
4. **Salutation** — *Dear Sir, Dear Madam, Dear Sir/Madam,* or *Dear Mr/Ms [Name]*
5. **Subject line** — brief, underlined (optional but adds marks for clarity).
6. **Body** — three to four short paragraphs.
7. **Closing phrase** — *Yours faithfully* (if you used Sir/Madam), *Yours sincerely* (if you used a name).
8. **Signature** — your signature over your printed name.

### Example — complaint letter

> 42, Beach Road,
> Matara.
> 15th October 2026
>
> The Manager,
> Sunshine Electronics,
> Main Street,
> Matara.
>
> Dear Sir,
>
> **Subject: Complaint regarding a faulty laptop**
>
> I am writing to complain about a laptop (model XYZ) that I purchased from your shop on 1st October 2026. The receipt number is 7823.
>
> Within one week of use, the laptop started shutting down randomly. I visited your shop on 10th October and spoke to a staff member, who promised to call me back. I have not received any response in five days.
>
> Since the laptop is still under warranty, I would like a full inspection and either a repair or a replacement. I would also appreciate a response within seven days.
>
> I look forward to your reply.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> [Signature]
> Kavindu Perera

### Body paragraphs — what goes where

- **Paragraph 1:** State the purpose clearly. Who you are if relevant. What the letter is about.
- **Paragraph 2:** Provide the details — when, where, what happened.
- **Paragraph 3:** State what you want. Be specific.
- **Paragraph 4 (optional):** Polite close inviting a response.

## Informal letter format

Informal letters go to friends, family, and people you know personally.

### The structure (simpler)

1. **Your address** — top right corner.
2. **Date** — below the address.
3. **Salutation** — *Dear Priya, Dear Uncle, My dear sister.*
4. **Body** — relaxed, conversational, two to four paragraphs.
5. **Closing phrase** — *Yours lovingly, With love, Your friend,* or similar.
6. **Signature** — just your first name.

No recipient's address in an informal letter.

### Example — informal letter

> 42, Beach Road,
> Matara.
> 15th October 2026
>
> Dear Nilakshi,
>
> I hope you and your family are all doing well. Sorry I haven't written in a while — the term has been busy with model papers.
>
> I wanted to tell you about our school trip to Yala last weekend. We left early Saturday morning and reached by lunchtime. The safari was incredible — we saw elephants, deer, and even a leopard from a distance! I'm attaching a few photos with this letter.
>
> How is life in Colombo? Are you still doing your dance classes? Let me know when you're free — maybe we can catch up during the term break.
>
> Give my love to your mother and father.
>
> Your friend,
> Kavindu

### Tone — the real difference

- **Formal** — impersonal, factual, polite, structured.
- **Informal** — personal, warm, flowing, specific questions and details.

An informal letter should sound like a conversation. A formal letter should sound like a business document.

## Common format mistakes that lose marks

### 1. Missing your address

Your address goes in the top right. Missing it alone can cost 2–3 marks.

### 2. Wrong closing phrase

- *Dear Sir* needs *Yours faithfully.*
- *Dear Mr Perera* (with a name) needs *Yours sincerely.*

Mismatching these is a classic error.

### 3. Including your address in an informal letter

Technically allowed. But if space is tight, prioritise your date — that's the required part.

### 4. Formal tone in an informal letter

- ✗ *Dear Father, I would like to inform you that I have successfully completed my school examinations.*
- ✓ *Dear Appa, Just wanted to let you know the exams are finally over!*

Match the tone to the relationship.

### 5. One giant paragraph

A letter with no paragraph breaks loses structure marks. Three to four paragraphs minimum.

### 6. Word count mistakes

Most O/L letter questions specify 100–150 words. Writing 60 or 250 both lose marks. Learn roughly how many lines of your handwriting make 120 words.

## Common content mistakes

### 1. Not answering the actual prompt

If the question asks you to complain about late bus service, don't write about why buses are good. Read the prompt twice.

### 2. Making up extreme details

A letter about a stolen bicycle doesn't need "the thief had a gun and the police came in a helicopter." Keep details realistic.

### 3. Forgetting the subject line in formal letters

Not required, but a clear subject line (e.g., *Subject: Request for library membership*) earns easy clarity marks.

### 4. No closing paragraph

Ending abruptly after the complaint or request without a polite close feels incomplete.

## Types of formal letters in the O/L syllabus

The O/L paper usually asks for one of these types:

- **Complaint letter** — about a product, a service, a public issue.
- **Request letter** — asking for information, permission, or a job.
- **Application letter** — for a job, membership, or admission.
- **Invitation / RSVP letter** — formal invitations and responses.
- **Letter to the editor** — about a newspaper topic or community issue.
- **Thank you letter** — formal appreciation.

Each has a slight variation in body content, but the format stays the same.

## Types of informal letters

- **Letter to a friend** — most common.
- **Letter to a family member** — usually with warmer salutations.
- **Letter of invitation** (informal) — birthdays, outings.
- **Letter of congratulations** — exam results, achievements.
- **Letter of sympathy** — rare but may appear.

## Templates to memorise

Memorising full letters doesn't help — the topic changes. But memorising the format skeleton saves time and secures the format marks.

### Formal letter skeleton

> [Your address]
> [Your address]
> [Your address]
> [Date]
>
> [Recipient's name and title]
> [Recipient's address]
>
> Dear Sir / Dear Madam / Dear Mr [Name],
>
> Subject: [Clear subject line]
>
> [Paragraph 1: Purpose.]
>
> [Paragraph 2: Details.]
>
> [Paragraph 3: Request / expected action.]
>
> [Paragraph 4: Polite close.]
>
> Yours faithfully / Yours sincerely,
> [Signature]
> [Printed name]

### Informal letter skeleton

> [Your address]
> [Date]
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> [Paragraph 1: Warm opening, how are you.]
>
> [Paragraph 2: Main news or purpose.]
>
> [Paragraph 3: Details, personal touches.]
>
> [Paragraph 4: Ask about them, invite reply.]
>
> With love / Your friend,
> [First name]

Learn both skeletons by heart. In the exam, you fill in the content — the structure is automatic.

**Is *Yours sincerely* okay with *Dear Sir*?**

No. *Dear Sir / Dear Madam* always takes *Yours faithfully*. *Dear Mr [Name] / Dear Ms [Name]* takes *Yours sincerely*. Mismatching costs marks.

**How many words should an O/L letter be?**

Usually 100–150 words for the shorter letter, more for a long composition letter. Check the question — the word count is always specified.

Not required, but it adds clarity and often earns a presentation mark. Use one.

# CV Writing in English for Sri Lankan Job Applicants

Your CV is read for 20 to 30 seconds. That's it. In that window, the recruiter decides whether to invite you to interview or move on. A strong CV makes that decision easy. A weak one — even from a strong candidate — doesn't make it past the pile.

Here's how to write a CV in English that works in Sri Lanka and translates internationally. Based on what recruiters actually ask for, not what old textbooks say.

## The right length

- **Freshers / 0–3 years' experience:** one page.
- **Mid-career / 3–10 years:** one to two pages.
- **Senior / 10+ years:** two pages maximum.

A CV longer than two pages tells the reader you can't prioritise. Long CVs get skimmed and shortened mentally — making your worst points stand out.

## The structure (in this order)

1. **Name and contact details** — top of page.
2. **Professional summary** — 3–4 lines.
3. **Work experience** — most recent first.
4. **Education** — most recent first.
5. **Skills** — relevant, specific.
6. **Certifications / training** — if relevant.
7. **Languages** — important in Sri Lanka.
8. **References** — "Available on request" line, not actual names.

Freshers can swap the order of work experience and education.

## 1. Name and contact details

At the top. Clean. No logo, no photograph unless the job specifically asks.

- **Full name** — larger font, bold.
- **Phone** — with country code if applying internationally: +94 77 123 4567.
- **Email** — professional-sounding. *dinusha.perera@gmail.com*, not *supercoolboy99@gmail.com*.
- **Location** — city is enough. "Matara, Sri Lanka." No need for your full address.
- **LinkedIn URL** — optional, recommended for mid-career and above.

What NOT to include:

- Photograph (unless the company specifically requests one — European CVs sometimes do).
- Age, date of birth.
- Marital status.
- NIC number.
- Religion, race, caste.

International CVs leave out personal details. Local CVs sometimes include them, but you can safely skip them now.

## 2. Professional summary

Three to four lines. Not an objective ("Seeking a challenging position..."). A statement of who you are and what value you bring.

### Example — fresher

> Recent BBA graduate from the University of Ruhuna with internship experience in retail banking and strong analytical skills. Confident in Excel, SAP, and client communication. Looking for a graduate role in operations or analytics.

### Example — mid-career

> Operations supervisor with 5 years of experience in logistics, managing teams of up to 12 and reducing warehouse errors by 20% through process improvement. Proficient in SAP, inventory systems, and team coaching.

### Example — career changer

> Customer service manager transitioning into training and development after 7 years of coaching new hires. Strong communicator, experienced in building onboarding programmes. CELTA-qualified (2026).

Keep it specific. Avoid buzzwords like *passionate, dedicated, hardworking* without evidence.

## 3. Work experience

Most recent job first. For each role:

- **Job title**
- **Company name** — and city if not obvious.
- **Dates** — month and year. "Jan 2022 – Present."
- **Three to five bullet points** — what you did, with results where possible.

### Example

> **Customer Service Executive**
> Nations Bank, Matara — Jan 2023 to Present
>
> - Handle 60+ customer enquiries daily across phone, email, and branch channels.
> - Resolved 95% of complaints within 24 hours, exceeding the team target of 85%.
> - Trained 4 new hires during onboarding, cutting time-to-productivity by 30%.
> - Supported the rollout of a new CRM system across the branch (May 2024).

### The STAR approach for bullets

Each bullet point should answer:

- **What did you do?**
- **How well did you do it?** (Numbers if possible.)
- **What was the result?**

Weak: *Responsible for customer service.*
Strong: *Handled 60+ customer enquiries daily and resolved 95% within 24 hours.*

Numbers matter. If you can measure it, measure it.

### Action verbs that work

Start each bullet with a strong verb:

- **Leadership:** led, managed, coached, mentored, supervised, directed
- **Delivery:** delivered, completed, launched, implemented, rolled out
- **Problem solving:** resolved, improved, reduced, streamlined, optimised
- **Analysis:** analysed, assessed, evaluated, researched, forecasted
- **Communication:** presented, trained, negotiated, briefed, published

Avoid: *was responsible for, did, worked on* — these are weak.

## 4. Education

Most recent first. For each entry:

- **Qualification** — Degree, Diploma, Certificate.
- **Institution and location**
- **Dates**
- **Specialisation, class, or grade** — if strong.

### Example

> **Bachelor of Business Administration (BBA)**
> University of Ruhuna, Matara — 2019–2023
>
> - Specialisation: Finance. Second Class (Upper Division).
> - Relevant modules: Corporate Finance, Managerial Accounting, Data Analysis.

For O/L and A/L, include them for recent graduates. After 3–5 years of work experience, A/L details become less important.

- **A/Levels (2019):** Mathematics stream — 2A, 1B, 1C (Z-score 1.8).
- **O/Levels (2017):** 9 passes including 2 Distinctions in English and Mathematics.

Include the specific subject grades only if they're strong.

## 5. Skills

Specific skills, not generic traits.

### Good

- Excel (advanced — pivot tables, VLOOKUP, macros)
- SAP (3 years, SD module)
- SPSS, Tableau, Power BI
- Project management (Agile/Scrum)
- Content writing (English — blog, LinkedIn, long-form)

### Weak

- Hardworking
- Team player
- Passionate about learning
- Good communicator

Hiring managers see "hardworking" on every CV and skip past it. Specific skills they can test for lift your CV.

## 6. Certifications and training

Only relevant ones. Short, dated.

- CELTA (Cambridge, 2024)
- IELTS Band 7.5 (Academic, 2025) — important for overseas-facing roles.
- Digital Marketing Fundamentals (Google, 2024)
- Chartered Accountancy — Business Level (CA Sri Lanka, 2023)

## 7. Languages

Important in Sri Lanka. List with real levels.

- **Sinhala** — Native.
- **English** — Fluent.
- **Tamil** — Conversational.

Avoid vague "Reading and writing" — recruiters know that's weak. Use native / fluent / conversational / basic.

## 8. References

One line: *"References available on request."*

Don't list referee names and contact details on the CV — it looks dated and clutters the page. Provide them when asked.

## Common Sri Lankan CV mistakes

### 1. *Passed out* from school

- ✗ *Passed out from Richmond College.*
- ✓ *Graduated from Richmond College.*

*Pass out* in international English means to faint. Catches recruiters off guard.

### 2. Photograph without being asked

Many European and North American recruiters are trained to discard CVs with photographs to reduce bias. Even where it's accepted, leave it out unless asked.

### 3. Personal details overload

NIC, religion, caste, date of birth, marital status — none of these belong on an international CV, and most aren't needed locally either.

### 4. *Objective* at the top

Dated. Replace with a professional summary that's about value, not aspiration.

### 5. School-era vocabulary

- ✗ *Headed many school societies.*
- ✓ *Led three student societies as president, organising events for 200+ members.*

Shift from passive "was involved in" to active "led" / "managed" / "delivered."

### 6. Lists of soft skills

Hardworking, team player, quick learner — add nothing. Skip and use specifics instead.

### 7. Inconsistent dates and tenses

Past jobs in present tense, current jobs in past tense — looks careless. Current role: present tense. Past roles: past tense. Always.

## Formatting dos and don'ts

**Do:**

- Clean, professional font — Arial, Calibri, or Garamond at 10–11 pt.
- Plenty of white space.
- Consistent spacing and bullet style.
- Save as PDF unless asked for Word.
- Name the file professionally: *CV_DinushaPerera_2026.pdf*.

**Don't:**

- Coloured borders or decorative fonts.
- Comic Sans.
- Random bolding and italics.
- Photographs with poor lighting.
- 10 pages of achievements.

## For international applications

- Add country code to phone number.
- Use British English spelling (*organise* not *organize*) unless applying to US companies.
- Mention visa / work authorisation status if relevant.
- Keep to two pages maximum.
- Remove photograph, DOB, religion, marital status.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our Business English programme includes a CV writing and job interview module. Students work on their actual CV with teacher feedback and run mock interviews in English.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should my CV be in British or American English?**

In Sri Lanka, British English is more common. For international applications, match the country you're applying to — American English for US, British English for UK, Australia, and most other English-speaking countries.

**Do I need to include my A/L subjects on my CV?**

Yes, for recent graduates (within 3 years of A/Ls). For mid-career applicants, A/L subjects are less important — just the overall result or stream is enough.

**Should I have different CVs for different jobs?**

Yes. Customise your summary and bullet points to match each role. The underlying structure stays the same; the emphasis shifts.

**Do I need to put a photograph on my CV?**

No — unless the job posting specifically asks. Most international recruiters prefer no photograph.

# Overcoming Shyness in English: A Guide for Sri Lankan Adults

You can read English. You can write emails in English. You even think some things in English. But the moment you have to speak — in a meeting, to a visitor, in front of a group — your throat tightens, your mind blanks, and you'd rather say nothing than make a mistake. If this is you, you're one of the most common people in any English class in Sri Lanka.

Here's the honest guide to fixing it. No motivational quotes.

## The problem isn't English

It really isn't. If you can read this blog, you know enough English to have a conversation. The issue is one of two things, or both:

### 1. Fear of judgement

Somewhere — a classroom, a boss, a relative at a wedding — you felt judged for your English. Your brain tagged English as unsafe. Now every time you need to speak it, your nervous system reacts as if you're in danger.

### 2. Lack of practice

You can't drive a car well if you only drive once a year. You can't speak English well if you only speak it in emergencies.

Both are fixable. The first needs patience. The second needs a schedule.

## Step 1: Give yourself permission to be bad

This sounds soft, but it matters. Adults carry an unspoken rule: *I should already be good at this*. And when they aren't, they avoid practising, which means they stay bad, which confirms the rule.

Break the cycle by deciding — out loud if needed — that bad practice is allowed. Bad English this month is how you get to good English in six months.

## Step 2: Pick a small, repeatable practice

Big goals ("I'll become fluent this year") die fast. Small daily habits don't.

Pick one of these. Start tomorrow.

- **Two minutes of narration.** Narrate what you're doing in the kitchen or car, out loud, in English. Alone. No one is listening. "I'm making tea. I'm turning on the stove. The kettle is heating. I need sugar."
- **One voice note a day.** Record a 60-second voice note on any topic. Save it. Compare week one to week four.
- **One conversation.** Find one person who speaks English and have a short chat with them daily. A colleague. A friend. A trusted cousin.

Pick one. Do it for 14 days. Don't skip. The habit matters more than the content.

## Step 3: Build a small circle of safe speakers

Shyness thrives when every English conversation feels high-stakes. Lower the stakes by building a small group of people you're comfortable speaking imperfect English with.

- A colleague who's also learning.
- A spoken English class, especially an adult one with similar-level peers.
- A patient friend or family member.
- An online language exchange partner.

Three or four people are enough. You just need somewhere to practise without a performance.

## Step 4: Delay judgement

The biggest reason adults stay shy is they judge themselves mid-sentence. You start a sentence, notice a mistake, stop, apologise, restart. This kills fluency and reinforces the fear.

The rule: finish every sentence you start. Even if it comes out wrong. Even if you know a better word. Finish it. Then move on.

Correcting yourself mid-sentence trains your brain to expect failure. Finishing the sentence, even badly, trains your brain that nothing bad happens when you speak.

## Step 5: Handle mistakes the right way

When you make a mistake — and you will — try this:

- **Notice it silently.** No apology. No "sorry my English is bad."
- **Rephrase or move on.** If the other person understood, keep going. If they didn't, rephrase.
- **Write it down after.** Not during. After the conversation, note the word or structure you got wrong. Look it up later.

Apologising for your English during a conversation makes the other person uncomfortable and reinforces your own fear. Skip the apology.

## Step 6: Switch to shorter sentences when nervous

When shyness peaks, long sentences fall apart. Short sentences hold up under pressure.

Instead of: *"I would like to inform you that there is a problem with the report that was prepared yesterday regarding the quarterly sales figures, which seems to have some data entry errors."*

Say: *"There's a problem with yesterday's sales report. Some data entries are wrong."*

Two short sentences. Same meaning. Easier to say. Less to go wrong with.

## Step 7: Rehearse the first sentence

You don't need to rehearse a whole meeting. You need to rehearse the first sentence. The first sentence is where nerves hit hardest. Once you've started, momentum carries you.

Before a meeting, repeat your opening line three times in your head. Even just *"Thanks for coming — let me start with a quick update."* Owning the first sentence steadies the whole conversation.

## Step 8: Fix the physical nerves

Shyness lives in the body as much as the mind.

- **Breathe out slowly before you speak.** Not deep breaths — slow exhales. This calms the nervous system.
- **Lower your shoulders.** Shy posture is tight shoulders and a slight slump. Relax both.
- **Make eye contact for two seconds.** Not stare — just a steady look before speaking. Cuts the fear of being looked at.
- **Smile slightly.** It changes your voice tone and the other person's response.

These sound small. They add up.

## Step 9: Find one win per week

Shyness feeds on evidence of failure. Starve it with evidence of wins.

Each week, note down one moment you spoke English and it went okay. Not perfect. Just okay.

- "I asked for the bill in English at the restaurant."
- "I said 'good morning' to the foreign colleague instead of avoiding him."
- "I answered a question in the meeting instead of staying quiet."

One a week. Keep the list on your phone. On bad days, read it.

## Step 10: Know this takes time

Shy adults rarely become fully confident English speakers in six weeks. The honest timeline:

- **Week 2:** easier to start a sentence. Still nervous mid-conversation.
- **Week 6:** can hold a short conversation without blanking.
- **Month 3:** physically feel less nervous. Can speak up in a small meeting.
- **Month 6:** comfortable enough to initiate English conversations yourself.
- **Year 1:** confident in most everyday situations, still nervous for very formal ones.

This is normal. Faster is possible with intense practice. Slower is also fine.

## What doesn't help

- **Watching more English TV.** Builds listening, not speaking.
- **Grammar books.** You already know enough grammar to speak.
- **Learning more vocabulary.** New words don't come out when you're frozen.
- **Thinking in English.** Classic advice; nearly impossible to force.
- **Memorising phrases.** They never come out when you need them.

## Where a class fits

A good adult spoken English class is the single fastest fix for most shy adults. Not because the teacher has a secret. Because:

- You get regular low-stakes speaking time.
- You see others also making mistakes.
- You build a few familiar faces to speak with.
- The structure removes the "when do I practise?" question.

Look for: small groups (6–12), adults at similar levels, no public performances, no shaming.

Avoid: large groups, mixed levels, teachers who correct every mistake harshly, "fun speaking sessions" that are really grammar lectures.

## Where Woodberry Place fits

Our adult Spoken English classes run small, peer-level groups where every student speaks every lesson. We don't correct mid-sentence, we don't single students out, and most adults are comfortable speaking within three weeks.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal for Sri Lankan adults to feel shy about English?**

Very. Most working adults in Sri Lanka report some discomfort speaking English, especially in front of people perceived as more fluent. The feeling is almost universal.

**Will I ever be fully confident?**

Most people get to "comfortable" rather than "fully confident." Even native English speakers feel nervous in formal settings. Comfortable is enough.

**Should I take a group class or 1-on-1 lessons?**

For shyness, a small group usually works better than 1-on-1. Peers normalise mistakes. A teacher watching you alone can feel more judgemental.

**Can I fix shyness without speaking to anyone?**

Partly — narration and voice notes help build familiarity with your own English voice. But at some point real people are needed. Shyness goes away with exposure, not avoidance.

# English for Job Interviews in Sri Lanka

A job interview in Sri Lanka tests English twice. Once directly — through questions you have to answer in English. Once indirectly — through how fluently and confidently you sound across the whole conversation. Even for roles where the day-to-day work is in Sinhala or Tamil, interviewers watch your English.

Here's how to prepare so your English carries your interview, not drags it.

## Why English matters in Sri Lankan interviews

Three reasons, in order:

- **Employers often work with international clients.** Even for local-facing roles, email English matters.
- **English is treated as a proxy for other skills.** Rightly or wrongly, strong English signals a strong candidate.
- **The interviewer is also being watched.** Panels and senior managers want to hire someone they can defend. Confident English makes defence easy.

You can't change the system in one interview. You can use it.

## The structure of a typical interview

Most structured interviews in Sri Lanka follow this pattern:

1. **Warm-up** — small talk, how was your journey, tea or water.
2. **Tell me about yourself.** The most dangerous question. More below.
3. **Why this company? Why this role?**
4. **Technical / skill-based questions.** Specific to the job.
5. **Behavioural questions.** "Tell me about a time when..."
6. **Your questions for us.**
7. **Closing.** Next steps, salary expectations sometimes.

Prepare one solid answer for each of the first six sections. Don't memorise — rehearse until the structure is natural.

## "Tell me about yourself"

The question everyone fumbles. Here's the structure that works:

- **Present:** what you do now, one sentence.
- **Past:** how you got there, one or two sentences.
- **Future:** why you're here — why you want this role.

### Weak answer

> *My name is Dinusha. I am 26 years old. I passed out from University of Ruhuna. I like cricket and reading. I am interested in this job.*

### Strong answer

> *I'm currently a customer service executive at Nations Bank in Matara, where I've spent the last two and a half years handling retail queries and training new staff. Before that I studied business administration at the University of Ruhuna, focusing on finance. I'm applying to this role because I'd like to move from customer-facing work into the operations side, which is where my coursework was strongest and where I see my long-term career.*

Three sentences. Clear structure. No personal details that aren't asked for.

## "Why this company? Why this role?"

Not: *"Because I need a job"* or *"Because it's a good company."*

Preparation: read the company's website, LinkedIn page, and any recent news. Pick two specific things.

### Framework

- One thing about the company you genuinely admire (not generic — specific).
- One thing about the role that matches your skills.
- One thing about the industry's future that interests you.

### Example

> *Two things drew me to Dialog. First, the recent push into enterprise cloud services — I've been following that transition closely and it's where I'd like to spend my career. Second, this particular role combines customer-facing work with backend systems knowledge, which matches what I've been doing at my current job but with a bigger scale.*

Specific beats generic every time.

## Behavioural questions — the STAR method

"Tell me about a time when..." questions test how you've actually handled situations. Structure your answer with STAR:

- **Situation** — set the scene briefly.
- **Task** — what you were asked to do.
- **Action** — what you specifically did.
- **Result** — what happened.

### Example

> **Interviewer:** Tell me about a time you handled a difficult customer.
>
> **You:** Last year we had a customer who was angry because his monthly statement had an error that had carried over for three months. Nobody had owned the issue — it kept getting transferred.
>
> I took responsibility for it, apologised, and walked through the statement with him line by line. The underlying issue was a wrong charge code applied at account setup. I corrected the record, got the refund approved within a day, and called him back to confirm.
>
> He wrote to our branch manager afterwards praising the resolution. I also wrote up what I'd found as a process note so the same error wouldn't repeat.

Specific. One clear incident. Numbers or evidence where possible.

## Questions you should ask

At the end, when they say "Do you have any questions?" — always say yes. Candidates with no questions look uninterested.

### Safe, strong questions

- *"What does success in this role look like in the first six months?"*
- *"How is performance evaluated in this team?"*
- *"What are the biggest challenges someone in this role would face?"*
- *"What do you enjoy about working here?"*
- *"What are the next steps in the hiring process?"*

### Don't ask (yet)

- Salary — unless they bring it up first.
- Working from home policy as the first question.
- How much leave you get.

Save practical questions for the second interview or offer stage.

## Common Sri Lankan interview mistakes

### 1. *Myself [Name]*

- ✗ "Myself Dinusha Perera."
- ✓ "I'm Dinusha Perera." / "My name is Dinusha Perera."

### 2. *Passed out from*

- ✗ "I passed out from University of Ruhuna."
- ✓ "I graduated from the University of Ruhuna."

### 3. Over-formal language

*"I am humbly requesting an opportunity to serve your esteemed organisation."* Reads as colonial-era. Keep it conversational and modern.

### 4. Too many personal details

Your age, religion, marital status, number of siblings — not relevant. Don't volunteer them.

### 5. Weak closing

Ending the interview with "That's all from my side" is weak. Close strongly:

> *"Thanks for the conversation — I really enjoyed hearing about the role. I'd love to be considered."*

### 6. Apologising for your English

Never. If you make a mistake, pause, rephrase, continue. Apologising highlights it.

### 7. Reading from notes

For technical questions, having short notes is fine. For personal answers, reading from a paper looks scripted. Memorise the structure, not the words.

## Phrases to practise

### Opening

- *"Thanks for having me."* — walk in with this.
- *"Thanks for the question."* — before a hard question, buys you two seconds.

### Buying time

- *"Let me think about that for a second."*
- *"That's a good question."*
- *"To make sure I understand, you're asking about [X]?"*

### Showing you listen

- *"Based on what you've described..."*
- *"To build on what you mentioned..."*

### Closing

- *"Could I ask when I might hear back?"*
- *"Thanks again for the opportunity to interview."*

## A 2-week prep plan

### Week 1

- **Day 1:** read the job description three times. List the skills they want.
- **Day 2:** write your "tell me about yourself" using the Present-Past-Future structure.
- **Day 3:** list five STAR stories from your work. One difficult customer, one team conflict, one failure, one win, one leadership moment.
- **Day 4:** research the company. Read their website, LinkedIn, and news.
- **Day 5:** draft answers to the five most common behavioural questions.
- **Day 6:** draft your three questions for the interviewer.
- **Day 7:** rest, review.

### Week 2

- **Day 1:** rehearse your "tell me about yourself" out loud, five times.
- **Day 2:** rehearse one STAR story out loud until it flows.
- **Day 3:** do a mock interview with a friend. Ask them to be strict.
- **Day 4:** work on the answers that stumbled.
- **Day 5:** second mock interview.
- **Day 6:** rest. Early sleep.
- **Day 7:** interview day.

## Interview day

- Arrive 15 minutes early.
- Dress one level smarter than the office norm.
- Switch off your phone. Not silent — off.
- Shake hands firmly but not aggressively.
- Make eye contact, smile, start clearly.
- Water on hand for the dry mouth.
- Breathe out slowly before each answer.

**Should I speak British or American English in a Sri Lankan interview?**

British-leaning English is standard in most Sri Lankan professional settings. Don't try to switch to American — your natural English, clearly spoken, is best.

**What if my English is weaker than other candidates'?**

Lean into your other strengths and show real preparation. Clear, honest, structured English beats fast English full of errors every time.

**Is it okay to switch to Sinhala or Tamil during the interview?**

Only if the interviewer does first. Stay in English until invited to switch.

**How much preparation is enough for an interview?**

Two weeks at 30–45 minutes a day is enough for most mid-career candidates. For a dream role or a senior job, start four weeks ahead.

# How to Give a Presentation in English Without Panic

Standing in front of a room and speaking in English feels bigger than it is. It's the same English you use every day — just with people looking at you. But the extra pressure is real, and for most Sri Lankan professionals, it's one of the biggest career hurdles. Here's how to get through a presentation without your voice shaking or your mind blanking.

Not confidence tricks. Actual preparation and specific English that works.

## The structure that holds up under pressure

Every good presentation has the same shape:

1. **Opening** — grab attention, state the point.
2. **Agenda** — what's coming.
3. **Three main points** — the body.
4. **Summary** — restate the point.
5. **Close** — call to action or question invitation.

Five parts. Any presentation — a 5-minute update or a 30-minute pitch — fits this skeleton.

## 1. The opening

The first 30 seconds decide whether the room listens. Don't waste them.

### Weak openings

- *"Good morning. My name is Kasun. Today I will be talking about..."*
- *"Firstly, I would like to thank everyone..."*

### Strong openings

- *"Three months ago, we had a problem. Today I want to show you how we solved it — and what it saved us."*
- *"Let me start with a number: 47%. That's how much our customer response time improved last quarter. Here's how."*
- *"If you remember one thing from today, let it be this: [one sentence]."*

Open with the point, not the preamble. The thanks and introductions can wait.

## 2. The agenda

One short sentence. Not a bulleted slide listing every section.

> *"I'll cover three things: what we tried, what worked, and what's next."*

Done. Move on.

## 3. The body — three main points

Not five, not seven. Three. Any more and the room forgets.

For each point:

- **State the point.** One sentence.
- **Explain it.** Two or three sentences.
- **Example or evidence.** One specific case.
- **Transition.** One sentence into the next point.

### Example

> *"Point one: our old process took five days. [Explain.] It involved three handoffs between teams, with waiting time at each step. One request in May took eleven days to close. [Example.] Let's look at what we changed."*

Simple. Specific. Clear.

## 4. The summary

Restate your three points in one sentence each. Thirty seconds. The audience has had 10 minutes of information — remind them what mattered.

> *"So, to recap: our old process was slow, the new one cuts the time in half, and next quarter we're rolling it out across all three branches."*

## 5. The close

The last 30 seconds matter almost as much as the first. Don't fade out.

### Weak close

- *"That's all from my side. Any questions?"*

### Strong close

- *"If this works, we save Rs. 2 million a year and win back customer trust. I'd like to start the rollout next month — I'll send the plan this afternoon. Questions?"*

The close names what happens next. Vagueness here undoes the whole presentation.

## Phrases to anchor each section

### Opening

- *"Thanks for making time."*
- *"Let me start with..."*
- *"The reason we're here is..."*
- *"If you take one thing from this session..."*

### Signposting (moving between sections)

- *"Let's move to the second point."*
- *"That brings me to..."*
- *"Which leads to..."*
- *"Before we go on, a quick note."*

### Highlighting

- *"The key point here is..."*
- *"What really matters is..."*
- *"Notice that..."*
- *"This is important because..."*

### Giving examples

- *"For example,..."*
- *"To give you a specific case,..."*
- *"Take Monday last week, when..."*

### Closing

- *"To wrap up,..."*
- *"In summary,..."*
- *"The next step is..."*
- *"I'll leave you with this thought:..."*

### Questions

- *"Good question — the short answer is..."*
- *"I don't have the exact figure, but I'll follow up this afternoon."*
- *"Does that answer your question?"*

## Handling the panic

### Breathing

Nerves tighten your chest. Before you walk on, do three slow exhales. Inhale naturally, exhale for four seconds. Repeat three times. Lowers heart rate immediately.

### Pace

Panic makes people speed up. Deliberately slow down. Pause at the end of each sentence for a second. Pauses feel long to you — they don't to the audience.

### Hands

Don't know what to do with your hands? Hold them loosely in front of you. Or on the podium. Or holding a pen. Not in your pockets. Not crossed.

### Eye contact

Pick three friendly faces — one on each side of the room, one in the middle. Rotate among them. Feels like you're talking to the whole room without staring at anyone.

### If you lose your place

Pause. Look at your notes. Say *"Let me just check where I was."* Take a sip of water. Resume. The audience barely notices.

## Slides — rules that help, not hurt

- **One idea per slide.** Not five bullet points.
- **Big text.** 24pt minimum. If someone in the back row squints, the slide is too busy.
- **Pictures help.** A photo or chart often works better than words.
- **Don't read the slides.** The audience can read. Your job is to explain.
- **Hide your notes on the slides.** Put your detailed notes in the speaker's notes, not on the slide itself.

## Rehearsing

### Day 1

Write the presentation. Full sentences. Three main points, example for each.

### Day 2

Say it out loud. Alone. Twice. Time yourself.

### Day 3

Rehearse with one person as audience. Ask them for three pieces of feedback — start, middle, end.

### Day 4

Rehearse standing up. The posture and breathing are different when you're sitting.

### Day 5

Final rehearsal. Time it. Stop fiddling with slides.

Five days is enough for most presentations. For a big one, add a mock presentation to a wider audience.

## Handling Q&A

The question period after a presentation is where nerves often return. Three tricks:

### 1. Repeat the question

Buys you five seconds and ensures you heard it right.

*"The question is: how will we handle the stock levels during the transition?"*

### 2. If you don't know, say so

Honest beats fake. *"I don't have that number — I'll send it this afternoon."* Credibility goes up, not down.

### 3. Don't argue

If someone challenges you, acknowledge first, then respond. *"That's a fair point — my thinking was..."* Defensive answers lose the room.

## Common Sri Lankan presentation mistakes

### 1. Reading every word from slides

Audience reads faster than you speak. Skip ahead or watch their eyes glaze.

### 2. Memorising the whole talk

Memorised presentations sound robotic and collapse the moment you lose your place. Memorise structure, not sentences.

### 3. Starting with *"Firstly, I would like to thank..."*

Colonial-era. Replace with a direct opening.

### 4. Apologising for your English

Never. If you make a mistake, keep going.

### 5. Too many thank yous

*"Thank you for giving me this opportunity to thank you for your valuable time."* Once at the end. Not before every section.

### 6. Running over time

Every minute over your allotted slot irritates the room. Rehearse to come in 30 seconds under.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I write out my full presentation word for word?**

Write it out for the first rehearsal, then move to bullet-point notes. Word-for-word scripts sound robotic in delivery.

**What if I freeze mid-presentation?**

Pause. Breathe out. Take a sip of water. Check your notes. Resume. The audience sees 2 seconds; you feel 30. Pauses are fine.

**How much eye contact is enough?**

Pick 3–5 friendly faces and rotate through them. Avoid staring at one person. Also avoid staring at your laptop or the back wall.

**Is it okay to use humour in presentations?**

A light, one-line opener that fits the room is fine. Don't lead with a joke, don't push humour if it's not your style.

# Raising a Bilingual Child in Sri Lanka

A lot of Sri Lankan parents grow up speaking one language at home and wish they'd grown up speaking two. So when they have children of their own, the question comes up fast: can we raise this child to speak Sinhala (or Tamil) and English comfortably? And if yes, how do we do it without confusing them?

Good news: the research is clear. Children handle two or three languages at the same time without harm. The practical question is how to do it in a Sri Lankan household.

## What "bilingual" actually means

A bilingual child doesn't speak two languages identically. They have a *stronger* language (usually the one spoken most at home) and a *supporting* language (used elsewhere). This is normal. Perfect balance isn't the goal.

A bilingual child typically:

- Has a slightly smaller vocabulary in each language than a monolingual child, but a larger total vocabulary across both.
- May mix languages for a year or two when young — this is called *code-switching* and it's completely normal.
- Catches up fully in vocabulary by late primary school.
- Thinks flexibly between languages for life.

## Myths that need to go

### Myth 1: Two languages confuse children

False. Children from birth can separate languages. Yes, they sometimes mix words ("Mummy, pass the කටුව"). This is sorting, not confusion.

### Myth 2: Bilingual children start speaking later

Mixed evidence. Some do start a few months later; others don't. Either way, by age 3–4 they catch up and the delay has no long-term effect.

### Myth 3: You must only speak English at home to raise a bilingual child

False. In fact, this often backfires in Sri Lankan families where parents' own English isn't strong enough to sustain a full childhood of conversation. The quality of English your child hears matters more than the quantity.

### Myth 4: Children must start young or they'll never be bilingual

Partly true. Earlier is easier, but children added into English at Grade 3 or 4 still become comfortably bilingual with consistent exposure.

## The three household models that work

### Model 1: One Parent, One Language (OPOL)

- One parent speaks Sinhala (or Tamil) only to the child.
- The other parent speaks English only to the child.

Works best when one parent is fully comfortable in English. In Sri Lanka, often achievable if one parent grew up in an English-medium school, worked abroad, or works in an English-first profession.

### Model 2: Home language, English outside

- Family language at home (Sinhala or Tamil).
- English at school, class, screen time, and specific activities.

This is the most common Sri Lankan model. Works well if the family actively supports the English side with reading, cartoons, and classes rather than leaving it to school.

### Model 3: Daily English window

- Family language most of the day.
- One or two defined English times — storytime, car rides, dinner — kept consistent.

A gentler version of Model 2, useful when parents aren't fully comfortable with English but want to build steady exposure.

Pick the one that fits your household. You can shift between models as the child grows.

## Age by age: what to do

### Birth to 2

- Talk constantly to your baby in your strongest language.
- Introduce short English words through play — colours, animals, food names.
- Read English picture books aloud. The baby doesn't need to understand every word.
- Sing English nursery rhymes.

### Ages 2–4

- Continue family language as the main conversation language.
- Add 15–30 minutes a day of dedicated English time — stories, songs, routines.
- Exposure through English cartoons (short, well-chosen) with conversation afterwards.
- Avoid formal English worksheets. Play-based only.

### Ages 4–6

- Start phonics informally. (See our [phonics for Sri Lankan kids guide](https://woodberryplace.com/blog/phonics-for-kids-sri-lanka/).)
- Continue English storytime, now with the child reading along.
- Consider a small group English class — Little Learners or similar.
- Praise effort, not accuracy. Mistakes are expected.

### Ages 6–10

- School English adds structured exposure.
- Independent English reading starts to matter — chapter books, graded readers.
- Daily English conversation at home, even briefly.
- Supplementary classes help if school English is thin.

### Ages 10–14

- Peer conversations become a strong driver. Encourage English-medium activities.
- Watching, reading, and writing in English become personal choice.
- Exam-focused English (O/L) overlaps with general English — both matter.
- Don't lose the family language — the goal is bilingual, not English-only.

## Common parent mistakes

### Mistake 1: Forcing English before the child is ready

A 3-year-old who's just started speaking shouldn't be drilled on verb tenses. Play comes first, structure comes later.

### Mistake 2: Correcting every mistake

A child who feels corrected mid-sentence stops speaking. Model the right version in your reply, don't lecture.

### Mistake 3: Making the family language feel less important

Praising English fluency more than Sinhala or Tamil teaches the child that the family language is lower-value. Both matter.

### Mistake 4: Switching to English only before public

Many parents speak Sinhala or Tamil at home, then switch to English in front of visitors. This signals that English is for showing off. The child picks it up.

### Mistake 5: Comparing children

"My sister's son speaks English fluently at 5." Every child is different. Forcing a child to match another sets up resentment.

### Mistake 6: Over-relying on screens

An iPad with English cartoons for an hour isn't enough. Interaction is where language is built.

## When your English isn't strong

A lot of Sri Lankan parents worry that their own English isn't good enough to model for their children. The honest answer: your willingness to try matters more than your fluency.

- **Read English storybooks aloud.** Even if your pronunciation isn't perfect, your child learns the rhythm.
- **Watch English shows together and talk about them** in whichever language.
- **Let the child correct you** sometimes. Builds confidence on both sides.
- **Consider a class for them, not you.** A good small-group English class fills the gap.

What you absolutely shouldn't do: pretend your English is better than it is. Children sense this, and it damages trust.

## Multilingual households (Sinhala + Tamil + English)

Some Sri Lankan families have Sinhala on one side, Tamil on the other. Adding English makes three. Children handle three languages fine — same principles apply.

Common setup that works:

- Mother speaks Sinhala.
- Father speaks Tamil.
- School and class in English.
- Family conversations mixed, then clarified in each language.

Children end up trilingual with no extra effort as long as all three languages get real daily use.

## Signs it's going well

- The child switches languages depending on who they're talking to.
- Vocabulary is growing in both languages (even if one is bigger).
- The child uses English spontaneously, not just when asked.
- They're happy to read, listen, and play in both languages.

## Signs to pay attention to

- Child refuses English entirely and seems stressed about it.
- School teachers flag that the child is far behind peers in one language.
- Child isn't speaking much in either language by age 3.

The first two usually mean the method needs adjusting (less pressure, more fun). The third usually means a short speech assessment — not always a problem, but worth checking.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will my child be behind in Sinhala / Tamil if we add English early?**

Not if you keep speaking the family language regularly at home. Children handle multiple languages without long-term cost to any of them.

**Should I correct my child when they mix languages?**

No, especially under age 5. Mixing languages is a normal phase. Model the correct version in your reply and move on.

**When should we start a formal English class?**

Around age 4–5 is a comfortable starting point for a play-based class. Earlier is possible but not necessary.

**My child speaks English at school but Sinhala at home. Is that okay?**

Yes — this is one of the most common bilingual patterns in Sri Lanka. Keep both languages alive and your child will develop both.

# Screen Time for English Learning: What's Helpful, What's Not

Screens are everywhere in Sri Lankan homes now. Phones, tablets, smart TVs, YouTube on demand. Every parent faces the same question: does any of this actually help my child's English, or is it just background noise with English words in it?

Here's the honest answer, by age and by type. Based on what actually works, not what app advertising claims.

## The short version

- **Good screen time builds English.** Interactive, conversation-led, limited in time.
- **Bad screen time wastes time.** Passive, long sessions, no follow-up.
- **The difference is what happens after.** A child who watches a cartoon then talks about it learns. A child who watches and scrolls doesn't.

## How much is right by age

Rough rules, drawn from paediatric guidance and real family patterns:

| Age | Total daily screen time | English-learning share |
|---|---|---|
| Under 2 | None (except video calls) | None |
| 2–5 | 30–60 minutes | 15–30 minutes |
| 6–10 | 60–90 minutes | 30–45 minutes |
| 11–14 | 90–120 minutes | Up to an hour, overlapping |

These are upper limits for learning use, not daily minimums. Half of these numbers is fine.

Note: these don't include school screen time (online classes, homework). Assume school time is separate.

## What actually helps

### 1. English cartoons watched actively

A 15-minute cartoon followed by 5 minutes of conversation builds language faster than 30 minutes of passive watching.

After the cartoon, ask simple questions:

- *Who was in the story?*
- *What happened at the beginning / middle / end?*
- *Was that a good decision? Why?*
- *What would you do?*

Child does 80% of the talking. You listen. Correct mistakes only by modelling the right version in your reply.

### 2. Audiobooks and podcasts for children

Underrated in Sri Lanka. Audio-only means the child builds listening skills without screen fatigue.

- 10–15 minutes before bed.
- During car rides.
- During quiet play.

Good options: BBC children's podcasts, Audible Stories for Kids (free library), Spotify children's story playlists.

### 3. Interactive apps for phonics and reading (ages 4–7)

For very young children, apps that teach phonics — reading short words, matching sounds to letters — can work well. Rules:

- 10 minutes a session, not 30.
- Child reads aloud, not just taps.
- One app, not ten.

Good general types: phonics apps with voice recording, early readers with tap-to-hear.

### 4. Video chats with English-speaking family or friends

If you have relatives abroad or a family friend who speaks English, a weekly 15-minute video chat for the child is gold. Real conversation, real stakes, in English.

### 5. YouTube channels specifically for learners

A few channels run by teachers for English learners work well:

- *British Council LearnEnglish Kids* (official, well-structured)
- *Ms Rachel* (very young children, interactive)
- *Alphablocks* (phonics-based, for 4–6 year olds)
- *Peppa Pig in English* (for younger children — simple vocabulary, clear speech)

Save them in a playlist. Don't rely on autoplay.

### 6. Reading apps with real books

Platforms with proper children's books (Epic!, Oxford Reading Tree app, Reading Eggs) are better than game-based apps for genuine reading growth.

## What doesn't help (much)

### 1. YouTube on autoplay

Autoplay serves whatever gets clicks, not what builds English. A child watches one cartoon, then slides into unboxing videos, then into increasingly bizarre algorithm-fed content. Little English gain, lots of time lost.

Fix: disable autoplay or curate a playlist.

### 2. Shorts, TikTok, Instagram Reels

Short video formats train attention span to 15 seconds and build almost no language. The child may pick up a few phrases but miss how English works at the sentence or paragraph level.

For English learning, under-15s are better off with longer-form content.

### 3. Dubbed content

A Peppa Pig episode dubbed into Sinhala teaches almost no English. If you're using Peppa Pig for English, make sure it's the original English audio.

### 4. Passive family TV in English

Having the TV on in English while the family does other things is better than nothing, but it builds English much more slowly than focused viewing. Treat it as a bonus, not a strategy.

### 5. Apps that promise fluency

Apps marketed as full English solutions (especially for children) rarely deliver. Language is built through interaction, not through quizzing.

### 6. Educational games with endless rewards

Games that reward every correct answer with coins, stars, or badges can train the child to care about the game, not the English. Choose simpler apps with less gamification.

## The "watch together" rule

For any screen time under age 8, the strongest rule is simple: watch together when you can. Not for every minute. But often enough that you can talk about what you saw.

- "Why did he do that?"
- "What was the funny part?"
- "What English word did we hear a lot?"

Shared viewing doubles the language impact of the same watching time.

## Setting up a screen routine that works

### 1. Pick slots, not durations

"20 minutes after school" is clearer than "up to 30 minutes a day." Specific slots are easier to enforce.

### 2. Use a timer

A kitchen timer or a phone alarm. When it beeps, the screen goes off. Reduces arguments.

### 3. Pair screens with offline follow-up

After the show, the child draws a picture of the story, writes one sentence about it, or retells it to another family member. Converts passive viewing into active practice.

### 4. Device off meals and off bedrooms

Meals are family language time. Bedrooms are sleep. Screens stay in the living room.

### 5. Model it

Children watch what parents do. If you scroll through your phone all evening, they'll want to as well.

## For older children (11–14)

Teenagers won't follow a strict routine, but you can shift what they watch.

- Encourage English-language film and TV over dubbed content.
- Help them find English YouTube channels in subjects they already love (cricket, gaming, music, fashion).
- Suggest podcasts — most teenagers will listen if you find one that matches their interest.
- Watch one English film together per month. Discuss it afterward.

You're not controlling their screens at this age — you're nudging the mix.

## When screens are doing more harm than good

- Child is irritable or tired from constant screen use.
- Screen is replacing play, reading, or face-to-face time entirely.
- Child speaks almost only in short, meme-style English phrases.
- Screen time causes arguments every day.

If any of these appear, dial back. English learning doesn't require screens — it requires conversation, books, and curiosity. Screens are a supplement, not the main dish.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do English cartoons really help my child's English?**

They help when watched actively and followed up with conversation. They do very little when they run in the background.

**How much screen time is too much for a 5-year-old?**

Current paediatric guidance suggests no more than one hour a day of high-quality content for ages 2–5. For English learning specifically, 15–30 minutes of focused viewing is enough.

**Which is better — apps or cartoons?**

Both have a role. Cartoons build listening and natural English rhythm. Apps can drill phonics and reading. For most children, cartoons plus a parent are more powerful than apps alone.

**Should I let my child watch English content without subtitles?**

For ages under 7, English audio without subtitles is fine — they learn by listening. For older children who can read, English subtitles with English audio can speed up vocabulary growth.
